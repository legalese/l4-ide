On 16 December 2019, in the Rolls Building on Fetter Lane, Mr Justice Fraser handed down the sixth judgment in the group litigation that a subpostmaster named Alan Bates and others had brought against the Post Office. Paragraph 676 records a number: "Prior to the commencement of this litigation, the Post Office had acknowledged the existence of only two bugs in Horizon." The claimants' expert had counted 29 relevant to the issues before the court, 21 of them with lasting impact on branch accounts. Fraser's own summary, at paragraph 975 of a judgment that runs past a thousand: "Legacy Horizon was not remotely robust."[^1]

Horizon is the accounting system Fujitsu built for the Post Office's branches. For sixteen years, when it said a branch was short, the Post Office treated the number as the fact and the person behind the counter as the thief: more than 900 prosecutions between 1999 and 2015, about 236 people imprisoned, at least thirteen suicides since linked to the scandal.[^2] The claimants' pleading said that Fujitsu kept a "Known Error Log" of the system's faults, which had been provided to the Post Office "but which has not been disclosed"; the Post Office's defense admitted the log existed and said it was not used by the Post Office.[^1] A list of the software's known defects sat at the vendor, in a document that nobody convicted on the software's output was shown.

Nothing in Horizon was a law. It was a ledger, and the ledger was believed.

In November 2014, lawyers inside Australia's Department of Social Services were asked about a proposal from the department that actually paid the benefits: take a welfare recipient's annual income from the tax office, divide it into fortnights, compare each fortnight to what the person had declared, and raise a debt on the difference. The advice was that averaging "did not accord with legislation." The Royal Commission that reported in July 2023 found no evidence the advice was communicated to the delivery department at the time. The scheme, which the public called Robodebt, ran from 2015 to 2019. The Federal Court, approving the class settlement in 2021, put the damage at debts of at least $1.763 billion unlawfully asserted against about 433,000 people, of which about $751 million had been recovered from 381,000 of them, with $112 million paid on top in the settlement; the Commission called it "a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal," and found the averaging "was in fact inconsistent with social security legislation."[^3] The defect was known before the code shipped. It lived in a memo. The code ran for four years.

The Dutch case is the same shape with a different mechanism. In April 2014 the tax authority's anti-fraud team classified 317 clients of one childcare agency as fraudsters. By the time a parliamentary committee reported, on 17 December 2020, under the title *Ongekend onrecht* — unprecedented injustice — some 26,000 parents had been wrongly accused of fraud between 2005 and 2019, many of them ordered to repay years of benefit in full over a missing signature or an undeclared change of income; the country's data-protection authority had found, that July, that the tax authority's methods, which used nationality as a risk indicator, were "unlawful, discriminatory and improper"; the cabinet resigned on 15 January 2021.[^4]

Three countries, three systems, one shape. In each, the thing that bound the person was the running system: the balance Horizon printed, the debt the averaging computed, the risk score that turned a missing signature into fraud. The text — the Social Security Act, the benefits statute, the subpostmaster's contract — was the law in the sense a court recognizes, *de jure*, and it was somewhere else. The code was the law *de facto*: what happened to you was what it computed. Lawrence Lessig gave the phrase its modern currency in 1999, in *Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace*: "This regulator is code — the software and hardware that make cyberspace as it is. This code, or architecture, sets the terms on which life in cyberspace is experienced."[^5] He meant the architecture of a network, what it lets you do without asking anyone. In a benefits office the phrase is literal: the code is the rule that was applied to you, and the statute is the rule you would have to hire a lawyer to have applied instead.

## Tax as code, in 2026

Tax is where the code has been the law longest, and where the machinery for living with that is most developed. The National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) has maintained TAXSIM, a program that computes federal and state income-tax liabilities from survey data, since a 1993 paper by Daniel Feenberg and Elisabeth Coutts described it.[^6] OpenFisca began in May 2011 inside a French government policy unit and is now the engine behind benefit calculators in a dozen jurisdictions; in 2021 "members of civil society started modelling British and then USA legislation, leading to the creation of PolicyEngine," a fork that runs its US model against TAXSIM on every release.[^7] Avalara, a vendor whose business is knowing what rate applies where, published the tenth edition of its annual tax-changes report this January: in 2025, it says, nine states broadened their sales-tax bases, and tariff activity added "nearly 3 million new tariff rates worldwide."[^8] Each of those is a version of a rule, with a date on which it started binding somebody.

The state can do it too. The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) launched Direct File as a pilot for the 2024 filing season in twelve states, 140,803 accepted returns; it expanded to twenty-five states for 2025, and in May 2025 the agency published the code, public domain, on GitHub. The repository describes itself carefully: Direct File "interprets the United States' Internal Revenue Code (26 USC) as plain language questions," and the questions and their logic were "developed in close collaboration with the IRS Office of Chief Counsel"; the logic lives in a "Fact Graph," a declarative knowledge graph "designed to reason about incomplete information, such as a partially completed tax return." The same README now opens with a notice that the repository "has been archived and is no longer maintained." The program was suspended in November 2025.[^9] For two seasons the government's own reading of its own tax code, compiled with its own counsel's office, ran on citizens' returns; then it was switched off, and the reading is still there, versioned "as of Tax Year 2024."

The newest entrant makes the versioning explicit. The Axiom Foundation launched on 28 July 2026 with "over 3,000 provisions of law encoded to the Axiom Foundation's new RuleSpec standard" — federal tax and benefit programs, with the United Kingdom's Universal Credit regulations beside them. Its pipeline is a large language model: it "reads a statute, encodes it section by section, and verifies encodings before shipping." The discipline it imposes is the one the three scandals lacked: "Every value cites a statute, every clause carries an effective date, and every available encoding is cross-checked against external engines and datasets, including PolicyEngine and TAXSIM." The encodings compile to Rust or WebAssembly; a certification system for encodings "with full validation against external sources" is planned.[^10] Our own research memo on RuleSpec, written on 9 September, found the format's `versions:` list — each version with its own `effective_from` and formula — to be "the closest structural match to L4's own design values of any backend researched in this programme so far," and found no deontic layer in it at all: RuleSpec concludes amounts and eligibility, never obligations.[^11] Axiom's home page states the reason the thing exists in three sentences: "AI needs ground truth. People are asking models policy questions. There has to be an answer key."[^10]

The critics of rules as code have been saying for years that this is the danger. Mireille Hildebrandt wrote in 2020 that code-driven law "fold[s] enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke, collapsing the distance between legislator, executive and court"; Tom Barraclough, Hamish Fraser and Curtis Barnes told the New Zealand Law Foundation in 2021 that rules written in code "should never be given the status of legislation" and are "better understood as an interpretation of what the law requires."[^12] They are right about the status. The three cases above are what happens when the folding is done by an accounting package, a debt-recovery pipeline, and a fraud classifier, none of which called itself law, and none of which anyone had thought to give the status of an interpretation either. The question that follows from the critics' point, and that they do not answer, is what a person who has been bound by a wrong rule can do about it — and the software industry has an answer to that, which it arrived at in 1999.

## A CVE for a rule

On 8 January 1999 two engineers at the MITRE Corporation, David Mann and Steven Christey, circulated a paper called "Towards a Common Enumeration of Vulnerabilities." Their problem was mundane. MITRE ran several vulnerability scanners and intrusion-detection systems (IDS) from several vendors, and "when we receive vulnerability information from multiple assessment and/or IDS tools, we need to know if they have identified the same vulnerability or not." There was "no common naming convention and no common enumeration." Their proposal was an identifier: one name per flaw, so that a vendor's advisory, a scanner's report and a customer's patch record could be joined.[^13] The list went public that September. The program is now Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE), run by MITRE for the United States government; since 2005 vendors and projects have numbered their own flaws as CVE Numbering Authorities.[^13] What the identifier does is small: it lets everyone downstream of a defect answer the same question in the same words — am I affected, from which version to which, and what is the fix.

Fraser's judgment needs a technical appendix to establish which bugs existed, when, and what each did to a branch account, because until 2019 no one outside Fujitsu had a list. The Robodebt settlement had to identify 433,000 people and work out what each had been assessed under and what they should have been. The Dutch committee's number, 26,000, was assembled after the fact from a system that had never recorded "this family was flagged by the nationality indicator." In each case the remediation began with archaeology: which version of the rule ran, on whom, from when to when, and what the right rule would have said. A CVE for a rule would be an identifier attached to exactly that: rule R, version V, effective from A to B, computed X wrongly for population P, corrected by version V′. Archive what ran; replay it under the corrected rule; classify who is affected and by how much; remediate; explain, to each person, in the rule's own words, why the number changed. None of the five steps is exotic. None was cheap in Canberra or The Hague because the systems had not been built to remember what they had done.

Databases learned to remember in the 1990s. Richard Snodgrass proposed temporal extensions to SQL in 1992, and the TSQL2 specification of September 1994 gave the field its vocabulary: *valid time*, "the time period during or event time at which a fact is true in the real world," and *transaction time*, "the time at which a fact was recorded in the database." A table that carries both is *bitemporal*, and it can answer two questions a plain table cannot: what did we believe on this date, and what was actually true on that one. The SQL:2011 standard folded the idea in; Datomic, from Rich Hickey and colleagues in 2012, made time "a basic feature of data entities"; in XTDB, built for compliance work, "every table is bitemporal."[^14] The literature's worked example is a man whose address is corrected after the fact — the database learns on the fifteenth about a move on the second — which is the Dutch case in miniature: a file corrected years later, with what the authority believed at the time still answerable.

Law needs a third axis, and the database literature does not have it. A rule is not a fact with a valid time; the rule itself changes. The rate on a sale depends on which version of the statute was in force, which can differ from both when the sale happened and when the auditor is asking. The tutorial in our documentation puts it as a tax office auditing a 2019 transaction in 2026, and names the three axes: system time, when the evaluation is running; valid time, when the facts held; and rule-effective time, which version of the law applies.[^15] A CVE for a rule is an identifier on the third axis.

## What the tree has, and what I ran

L4 has primitives for the three axes, and I ran them today rather than describe them from the specification.

The rule-version axis is a clause called `EVAL UNDER RULES EFFECTIVE AT`, registered as a builtin at line 79 of the type checker's environment, typed `DATE → a → a` in the reference, and wired to the evaluator by a pull request merged on 8 July 2026.[^16] The documentation's worked example is a goods-and-services tax whose rate rose from 7 to 9 percent for rules effective on or after 1 January 2024. The rate is one rule that reads the axis: if the rules-effective date is at least 1 January 2024, nine, else seven. Pinned to 1 June 2023 the rate evaluates to 7; pinned to 1 July 2024, 9; the tax on 1,000 comes out 70 and 90 through a dependent rule that knows nothing about versions. I ran the file with the installed binary and got those numbers.[^15] The default when nobody pins the rule version is the part a lawyer will recognize: the axis falls back to the valid time of the facts, so a sale pinned to 2019 is taxed under the 2019 law, which the tutorial calls "the presumption against retroactivity, built into the default," and an explicit pin overrides it, which is how a transitional provision or a savings clause is expressed.

The other two axes ride on the ledger. In L4 a durable fact is written with `RECORD` and read with `RECALL`, and the specification that ruled it — shipped in a pull request merged on 7 July 2026 — refuses to make that a variable assignment: "in a domain where audit is the product, raw destructive State is the wrong primitive: it discards provenance at the moment a value changes — the one thing L4 sells." The store is an append-only event ledger, and the current value is a projection over it.[^17] Every append is stamped. Run the corpus file that exercises this and the evaluator prints the ledger beside the answer: a rate of 7 recorded as valid from 1 January 2023, a rate of 9 valid from 1 January 2024, both with a transaction time of the harness clock; a read pinned to mid-2023 answers 7, to mid-2024 answers 9, to mid-2022 answers nothing, because the facts had not been recorded yet; and a read *as of system time* 2020 also answers nothing, because in 2020 the ledger was empty — "what did we know in 2020? nothing," as the file's own comment puts it. The list the file prints is `7, 9, 7, 9, 0, 9`.[^18] A sibling file locks the invariant that matters for audit: a write made inside an as-of clause is still stamped with the real clock, because that clause scopes reads and never writes — you can ask what the system believed in 2020, but you cannot forge a 2020 entry.[^18] The unit tests build the database literature's example directly — "John Doe in miniature," their comment says.[^19]

That is archive and replay, in the small. Put the Robodebt rule into this shape — schematically; our group had no part in that system — and the averaging is a version: `fortnightly income` under rules effective July 2015 divides an annual figure by 26; under the rules the 2014 advice described, it reads the fortnight's actual earnings. Each assessment is a `RECORD` stamped with when it was made and which rule version was in scope. The replay that took a Federal Court and a Royal Commission is then a read under the corrected version over the same ledger, and the classification a comparison of the two reads, person by person. The pilot of ours nearest to this was a payments company whose commercial agreements were full of fee tables, which we turned into L4 and served through a query interface to its operational systems; fee tables change, and whether that engagement versioned them by effective date is not in any record I have read. ## What it does not do

Axiom verifies differentially and we verify deductively. A differential check compares an encoding's output to an oracle — PolicyEngine, TAXSIM — on many inputs, and catches what matters most in tax: the encoding disagreeing with the world as the field already understands it. It needs an oracle to exist and inherits the oracle's errors; where two engines have read the statute the same wrong way, the cross-check is silent. Our checker, `l4 verify`, reads each rule as its logical skeleton and reports contradictions, unreachable branches and guards that do nothing; it needs no oracle, and it cannot see the statute at all — it will pass an encoding that is internally consistent and wrong about the law.[^20] Neither would have caught Robodebt. The averaging rule is consistent with itself, and every engine that implemented it would have agreed with every other. What caught it was a lawyer reading the Act, and what our substrate offers that case is the second half only: once the memo reaches the system, the replay is a query rather than a Commission.

There is one question a compiled artifact cannot answer. RuleSpec compiles to Rust and answers "what is the amount" and "is this person eligible"; it has no vocabulary for obligation, so the question post 1 asked of the Personal Data Protection Act — can these two duties, from a state the rules let you reach, both bind at once — cannot be put to it. L4 has the vocabulary and today cannot answer the question either: `l4 verify` is propositional, and the lowering to a timed model checker that would search the states is a proposal with rulings and no prover in the tree.[^20] What is on the table is that the question is expressible; the search is owed.

The replay tooling is owed too. What exists is the axis and the ledger. What the design documents describe and the tree does not contain: `@effective` and `@repealed` annotations that would let a statute's own commencement dates drive the version dispatch instead of an `IF` the encoder writes by hand; a driver-level comparison, `l4 diff-eval`, that would run one ledger under two rule versions and print the differences; and any store of versioned rule sets, as opposed to a date the current encoding branches on.[^21] The rule-version axis has nineteen uses in our corpus, all at the outermost level of a file, by a count made on 5 September.[^22] That is a primitive with a tutorial; the pipeline is not built.

A CVE for a rule is a proposal, and the CVE program is a warning about what a proposal needs. Mann and Christey's paper was an identifier scheme; what made it work was a maintainer, a government funder, and six years before vendors could number their own flaws.[^13] Nobody runs a registry of wrong rules. The nearest things are a Royal Commission's recommendations and an inquiry's 274,604 disclosed documents.[^2]

And the critics' objection survives all of it. An encoding that carries its version history, its valid times and its provenance is still an encoding that binds *de facto* the moment an agency runs it, and Hildebrandt's contestability — the citizen's chance to argue the interpretation in front of a court — is not restored by a better ledger. What the ledger restores is the ability to find out what was done to whom, under which reading, and to undo it at scale when a court says the reading was wrong. Our answer to the status question is post 9's: every encoding in our corpus is labeled `draft` until a named person signs it, and the label the corpus can award tops out at "isomorphic to its source," never "is the law."

The paper this material belongs to does not exist yet. The nearest facet is a design note toward a paper on the political economy of legal legibility, working title *Seeing Like a Citizen*, which asks who supplies a population's understanding of its own rules when the state does not, and on what incentives; the remediation machinery above — archive, replay, classify, remediate, explain — was added to the series on 13 September and has no paper of its own. Fraser's paragraph 676 is the reason it should: two bugs acknowledged, twenty-nine found, and a system that ran for twenty years before anyone outside Fujitsu could count them.
