A subpostmaster at Dalmellington scanned a pouch holding £8,000 out of her core branch and into her outreach branch, the van or village hall where a rural post office does business one day a week. The transfer replicated four times. Her outreach branch now held Horizon receipts for £32,000. She had moved £8,000 in cash, so the system said she was £24,000 short. She could see what had happened only because she stood at both ends of the transfer. It was corrected a few weeks later. Fujitsu then found 112 occurrences in 88 branches over five years. The bug is called Dalmellington because Horizon's bugs are named after the branch where Fujitsu first found them.[^1]

On 16 December 2019, in the Rolls Building on Fetter Lane, Mr Justice Fraser handed down the sixth judgment in the group litigation that Alan Bates and other subpostmasters had brought against the Post Office — one action, many claimants. Paragraph 676 records a number. "Prior to the commencement of this litigation, the Post Office had acknowledged the existence of only two bugs in Horizon." The claimants' expert had counted 29 relevant to two of the fifteen Horizon Issues before the court, 21 of them with lasting impact on branch accounts. The Post Office's counsel had put it to him that Dalmellington "was a human error." "No," he said, "it wasn't a human error, it was a defect of the system. It made it look like a human error." Fraser agreed with him. His summary, at paragraph 975, of a judgment that runs past a thousand: "Legacy Horizon was not remotely robust."[^1]

Horizon is the accounting system that ICL Pathway, later part of Fujitsu, built for the Post Office's branches under a contract awarded in 1996. For sixteen years, when it said a branch was short, the Post Office treated the number as the fact and the person behind the counter as a thief or a false accountant. More than 900 subpostmasters were prosecuted between 1999 and 2015. About 236 went to prison. At least thirteen suicides have since been linked to the scandal.[^2] Until 2000 a computer's output was admissible in an English criminal trial only on proof that the computer had been working properly. The Youth Justice and Criminal Evidence Act 1999 repealed that, on the Law Commission's recommendation, leaving a common-law presumption that the computer was working unless someone could show otherwise. The Ministry of Justice put the presumption out for a call for evidence in January 2025; Horizon is its first example.[^2] Fujitsu kept a "Known Error Log" of the system's faults. The claimants pleaded that the log had been provided to the Post Office "but which has not been disclosed"; the Post Office's defense admitted the log existed and said the Post Office did not use it.[^1]

Nothing in Horizon was a law. It was a ledger, and the ledger was believed.

In November 2014, Australia's Department of Social Services sought legal advice on a proposal from the department that actually paid the benefits. Take a welfare recipient's annual income from the tax office, divide it into fortnights, compare each fortnight to what the person had declared, and raise a debt on the difference. The advice was that averaging "did not accord with legislation." The Royal Commission that reported in July 2023 found no evidence the advice reached the delivery department at the time. The scheme, Robodebt to the public, launched in 2015.[^3] Through 2016 and 2017 the Administrative Appeals Tribunal — where a welfare recipient can take a decision before any court — repeatedly questioned the legal basis of averaging. On 8 March 2017 it found the method unlawful. The departments effectively ignored the decision, and the scheme ran until 2019.[^3] The Federal Court, approving the class settlement in 2021, recorded debts of at least A$1.763 billion raised against about 433,000 people, about A$751 million of it recovered from 381,000 of them, and A$112 million paid in settlement. The Commission called the scheme "a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal," and found the averaging "was in fact inconsistent with social security legislation."[^3]

In April 2014 the Dutch tax authority's anti-fraud team classified 317 clients of one childcare agency as fraudsters. Between 2005 and 2019 some 26,000 parents were wrongly accused of benefit fraud. Many were ordered to repay years of benefit in full. The trigger could be a missing signature on a form, or an undeclared change of income. In July 2020 the country's data-protection authority found that the way the tax authority had stored and used nationality in assessing claims was "unlawful, discriminatory and improper." A parliamentary committee reported on 17 December 2020 under the title *Ongekend onrecht*, unprecedented injustice. The cabinet resigned on 15 January 2021.[^4]

In each case what bound the person was the running system: the £32,000 of receipts Horizon printed at Dalmellington, the fortnightly debt the averaging computed, the clawback a missing signature triggered. The text — the Social Security Act, the benefits statute, the subpostmaster's contract — was the law in the sense a court recognizes, *de jure*, and it was somewhere else. The code was the law *de facto*. What happened to you was what it computed. Lawrence Lessig, whose 1999 book gave the phrase its currency, put it this way in *Harvard Magazine* the next January: "This regulator is code — the software and hardware that make cyberspace as it is. This code, or architecture, sets the terms on which life in cyberspace is experienced."[^5] In a benefits office the phrase is literal. The code is the rule that was applied to you. The statute is the rule you would have to hire a lawyer to have applied instead.

## Tax as code, in 2026

Tax is where code has bound people longest and where the machinery for living with that is furthest along. The OpenFisca family of tax-and-benefit engines keys every parameter by date, so a program asks for a rate as of a day; the older national calculators, the newer forks and the Internal Revenue Service's two-season Direct File are in the notes.[^6][^7] Catala, from Inria and Microsoft Research in 2021, puts the statute and the code on the same page, and applied to French family benefits it uncovered "a bug in the official implementation."[^6]

The Axiom Foundation launched on 28 July 2026 with "over 3,000 provisions of law encoded to the Axiom Foundation's new RuleSpec standard," most of them federal tax and benefit programs. Its pipeline is AI-driven, it says, and its rule is that "every value cites a statute, every clause carries an effective date, and every available encoding is cross-checked against external engines and datasets." The effective dates are what OpenFisca already carries. The pincite on every value and the cross-check at ship time are Axiom's.[^8] Our own research memo on RuleSpec, written on 9 September, called it "the closest structural match to L4's own design values of any backend researched in this programme so far," and found no vocabulary in it for obligation. RuleSpec concludes amounts and eligibility, never who must do what.[^9]

The critics of rules as code have been saying for years that this is the danger. Mireille Hildebrandt wrote in 2020 that code-driven law "fold[s] enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke, collapsing the distance between legislator, executive and court." The parliament that writes a rule, the agency that applies it and the judge who says what it meant become one program, run once. Tom Barraclough, Hamish Fraser and Curtis Barnes told the New Zealand Law Foundation in 2021 that coded rules "should never be given the status of legislation" and are "better understood as an interpretation of what the law requires."[^10] They are right about the status. The three cases above, though, are the folding done by an accounting package, a debt-recovery pipeline and a fraud classifier, none of which called itself law or an interpretation. Hildebrandt's remedy is constitutional: keep the decision contestable, one person before one court. 433,000 Australians and 26,000 Dutch parents cannot each litigate.

## A CVE for a rule

Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) is the software industry's registry of numbered defects, and it began as a thirteen-page paper. On 8 January 1999 David Mann and Steven Christey of the MITRE Corporation (MITRE) circulated "Towards a Common Enumeration of Vulnerabilities." MITRE ran several vulnerability scanners from several vendors. When two of them reported a flaw, nobody could tell whether it was the same flaw. Their proposal was an identifier: one name per vulnerability, so that a vendor's advisory, a scanner's report and a customer's patch record could be joined.[^11]

Each of the three remediations began with archaeology. Fraser needed a technical appendix to establish which bugs existed and when, because the one list sat with the vendor. The Robodebt settlement had to find 433,000 people and work out what each had been assessed under. A CVE for a rule would name what the archaeology has to reconstruct: which version ran, on whom, from when to when, and what the right rule would have said. Minted for Robodebt it might read `RULE-2023-0001`: Social Security Act 1991; income averaging as the delivery department implemented it; applied 2015 to 2019; overstated debts where earnings varied within the year; superseded by assessment on actual fortnightly earnings. The naming half exists — Europe already gives an act, a version and a point in time a resolvable identifier — and what nobody keeps is the defect record hanging off it.[^11]

One disanalogy is built in. A CVE names a deviation from a specification most parties concede. For a rule, the correct version *is* the dispute, and only a court can settle it, so a rule-CVE can be minted the day a court rules and not before. After the ruling, the steps are the ones software runs: archive what ran; replay it under the corrected rule; classify who is affected and by how much; remediate; explain, to each person, in the rule's own words, why the number changed. In Canberra the expensive part was the ruling. Establishing unlawfulness took a tribunal, a court and a Royal Commission, and acting on it was a decision nobody made for four years. The data was there; the settlement found its 433,000. On Fetter Lane the list existed and was withheld, and a better ledger does not cure that.

Databases have carried two clocks since Richard Snodgrass and Ilsoo Ahn separated them in *Computer* in September 1986: transaction time, "the time the information was stored in the database," and valid time, "the time when the relationship in the enterprise being modeled was valid." The textbook example is a man who moves house in August and registers it in December.[^12] Law needs a third clock, and legal informatics has had it for years as metadata. LegalRuleML, an industry standard since August 2021, gives each rule in-force and efficacy intervals. Its own motivating example is rule-version pinning: statutory damages of $500 in 2000, $750 in 2006, $1,000 in 2010, and a reasoner picking "the correct penalty according to the time of the crime."[^13] What that layer never had is anything that runs it: the reasoners that consumed LegalRuleML, Regorous and SPINdle, are dead — we re-checked both hosts today — and the interval is an annotation. A CVE for a rule is an identifier on that third clock.

## What already runs

There is a clause, `EVAL UNDER RULES EFFECTIVE AT`, that pins an evaluation to the version of the rules in force on a given date, and we ran its files rather than describe them from the specification.[^15] The tutorial's example is a goods-and-services tax whose rate rose from 7 to 9 percent for rules effective on or after 1 January 2024. One rule reads the clock: if the rules-effective date is at least 1 January 2024, nine, else seven. Pinned to 1 June 2023 it evaluates to 7; pinned to 1 July 2024, to 9. The tax on 1,000 comes out 70 and 90, through a rule that knows nothing about versions. We ran the file and got those numbers.[^14] Pin nothing and the rule version falls back to the valid time of the facts, so a 2019 sale is taxed under 2019 law; the tutorial calls that "the presumption against retroactivity, built into the default." An explicit pin overrides it, which is how a savings provision — the sentence in an amending act that keeps the old rule alive for matters already under way — is expressed. A phase-in or a taper, neither the old rule nor the new, still has to be written as a rule of its own.

The other two clocks ride on the ledger. A durable fact is written with `RECORD` and read with `RECALL`. The specification that ruled it, shipped on 7 July 2026, refuses to make that a variable assignment, because destructive state "discards provenance at the moment a value changes — the one thing L4 sells." The store is an append-only ledger, and every append is stamped.[^16] Three corpus files show it — two that exercise the bitemporal reads and one that locks the write stamp — and we ran all three today with the clock fixed at 31 January 2025. The first records a rate of 7 valid from 1 January 2023 and a rate of 9 valid from 1 January 2024, then reads. Pinned to mid-2023 it answers 7. Pinned to mid-2024, 9. Pinned to mid-2022, nothing, because no recorded fact covers that date. Unpinned, 9: last write wins. The binary prints `Result: LIST 7, 9, 7, 9, 0, 9` — the two writes echo first — above two ledger rows, the 7 stamped `vt=2023-01-01` and the 9 stamped `vt=2024-01-01`.[^17] The second is the correction case: two writes to the same cell at the same valid time, 1 then 2. A read at that time answers 2, the later write winning by its position in the log. A read *as of system time* 2020 answers nothing, because in 2020 the ledger was empty. It prints `LIST 1, 2, 2, 0`.[^17] The third file has no read in it at all. It writes inside an as-of clause and checks the stamp: the row says `at=2025-01-31T15:45:30Z`, the real clock, not 2020. You can ask what the system believed in 2020. You cannot forge a 2020 entry.[^17]

That is archive and replay, in the small, and Robodebt shows which clock the replay needs. Averaging was never a version of the rule with a commencement date; the Commission's finding is that the legislation never authorized it. So the department's method and the Act's own test sit at the same rule-effective date, and the clock that separates them is the encoding's own history: which encoding ran, on which assessments. Schematically — our group had no part in that system — each assessment is a `RECORD` stamped with when it was made. The replay is the same ledger read under an encoding of the Act instead of an encoding of the method, and the classification is the difference, person by person. The ledger half of that exists today. The replay half is a driver-level comparison the design documents call `l4 diff-eval`, scheduled as their third phase and not built; the in-language form, evaluating under a past commit, is ruled out permanently.[^18]

The nearest we have come to that question in an engagement was with an insurer, in 2023. A team of its claims adjusters had been helping us encode one of its policies. In the encoding the payout formula came apart into two readings, and the adjusters had been paying the larger one. That put us in a room with a problem that was not technical. We had to finesse the explanation to management, because the people who had helped us could be penalized for overpaying. Our presentation put two court decisions in front of management, one from Singapore's Court of Appeal and one from the Court of Justice of the European Union, as the class of risk an ambiguous consumer term carries. Management's response was about that liability and said nothing about which reading to pay in future. The fix was to the wording, at the level of the logic rather than the payout algorithm. The presentation itself is lost, and what is here is my memory of it.[^19] Whether the claims already paid were ever replayed is not part of that memory. A payments company we worked with had commercial agreements full of fee tables, which we turned into L4 and served through a query interface to its operational systems. ## What it does not do

Axiom checks its encodings against other people's engines; we check ours against themselves; neither would have caught Robodebt. A differential check compares an encoding's output to an oracle — PolicyEngine, the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) calculator — and inherits the oracle's errors. Where two engines have read the statute the same wrong way it is silent. Our checker, `l4 verify`, is a propositional consistency check over the boolean skeleton of each rule. Its findings are sound and not complete, so silence proves nothing. Every leaf — a comparison, an arithmetic test, a date — is an opaque atom, so no numeric or date contradiction is visible to it, and it cannot see the statute at all.[^20] Dividing an annual figure by 26 is arithmetic. The checker cannot be right or wrong about it, and every engine that implemented the method would have agreed with every other. What caught averaging was people reading the Act: an adviser in 2014 whose memo went nowhere, tribunal members in 2017 whose decisions were ignored, and a court. What our substrate offers that case is the second half only. Once the ruling exists, the replay is a query rather than a Commission.

RuleSpec has no vocabulary for obligation, so the question [post 1](01-race-condition-in-a-law.md) put to the Personal Data Protection Act — can two duties both bind at once — cannot be put to it. L4 has the vocabulary and cannot answer it yet either. Two duties that collide only because of when each falls due need a search over time, and a proposal in the tree describes that search while nothing in the tree performs it.[^20]

The rule-version clause has nineteen uses in the language repository's legal corpus, all in the Regulation Crowdfunding encoding, three files of 26, every one at the outermost position of a directive or export body; we re-ran the grep today.[^21] A statute's own commencement dates do not yet drive the dispatch; the encoder writes the `IF`. That is a primitive with a tutorial and a ledger with goldens. The pipeline is planned.

And the CVE program is a warning about what a proposal needs: an identifier scheme, then a maintainer, a government funder and six years before vendors could number their own flaws.[^11] Nobody runs a registry of wrong rules. The nearest things are a Royal Commission's recommendations and an inquiry's 274,604 disclosed documents.[^2] The three cases here were chosen for their outcomes, too. Woodcock and Larsen's complaint about formal-methods case studies — success stories, "little systematic data about neutral or unsuccessful adoption attempts" — applies here. Three catastrophes were picked to argue that a ledger would have made remediation cheap, and there is no case on the other side in which archive-and-replay delivered it at scale.[^22] Hildebrandt's objection survives all of it. An encoding that carries its versions, valid times and provenance still binds *de facto* the moment an agency runs it, and a better ledger does not restore the citizen's chance to argue the interpretation before a court. What it restores is the ability to find out what was done to whom, under which reading, and to undo it at scale once a court says the reading was wrong. On status, the answer is the one [the series' closing post](09-one-statute-at-a-time.md) gives: every encoding in our corpus is labeled `draft` until a named person signs it, and the highest label the corpus can award says the encoding matches the text it was made from — "isomorphic to its source" — never that it is the law.[^23]

The paper this material belongs to, on tax as code and the bitemporal substrate, is planned and not written; the political-economy paper, working title *Seeing Like a Citizen*, takes Robodebt for a different argument.[^23] Fraser's paragraph 676 is the reason to write the first one: two bugs acknowledged, twenty-nine found, and a subpostmaster at Dalmellington who was £24,000 short for a few weeks because a transfer replicated four times.
