**STATUS 2026-09-15: DRAFT — machine-drafted, persona-reviewed twice and revised three times; not yet read by Meng. Citations checked live on 2026-09-14; the fact-check corrections of 2026-09-15 are folded in and dated in the notes; voice pass 2026-09-14.**

On day 400 of a crowdfunding offering, an issuer — the company raising the money — advertised the terms of the deal, and our file said the issuer was in the clear. The file was our encoding of the rules, the rules rewritten as code, and it said so for three days in July 2026. The rules are the Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC) Regulation Crowdfunding, the rulebook a startup lives under when it raises from the public under the crowdfunding exemption. Rule 204(a)(1) forbids that advertising, except for oral or written communications that meet paragraph (b)'s requirements or fall within Rule 206. I recovered our draft of those three days from the version saved before the fix and ran it on my laptop. An issuer advertising on day 365 is in breach. On day 366 it is not. And the compiler, the program that reads the file and refuses it if it is malformed, had passed that file with two words: `Check succeeded.`[^1]

The year had been borrowed. Rule 501 of the same regulation restricts a _purchaser_ from reselling the securities for a year from issuance, subject to exceptions, and our draft had given Rule 204's prohibition on the _issuer_ the same one-year deadline. A deadline on a prohibition is the date the prohibition stops running, not the date it starts. Rule 204 names no event to count from, so the count ran from whatever moment a test invoked it. Nobody outside the project asked that file anything in those three days; it was ours and it was new. An issuer who had asked it whether the terms could be advertised in the offering's second year would have been told yes. The rule says no, with no year in it. The commit that created the file on 25 July and the one that fixed it on 28 July are both Meng's, each recording a model as co-author; the fix reads section 227.204 word for word and does not say what prompted the reading.[^1]

## The world first

The file declares its world before it says anything about conduct. Four kinds of actor: `Issuer`, `Intermediary`, `Investor`, `Purchaser`. Seven kinds of act, from filing a Form C offering statement to transferring the securities. Every dollar threshold is bound once, as a named constant dated per amendment, so the same rule can be asked what it said in 2017. The rule text quoted inside the file is copied from the electronic Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) as it stood on 23 July 2026. The header says verbatim; a diff against the CFR finds four places where a statutory citation or a paragraph label was dropped or added, none of them changing what a sentence means.[^2] A programmer knows the shape: types first, then functions over them. A lawyer knows it from the other end: definitions first, then the operative clauses.

Once the world is declared, a rule is a value with a type, and the compiler can hold it to the type. A regulative rule is one that says what a party must do; a constitutive rule says what counts as what, and the division is John Searle's. In L4, the programming language for law that our group at Singapore Management University has been building for six years by Meng's count, a regulative rule has a type, and the type is `DEONTIC Actor Action`. _Deontic_ is the logicians' word for must, may and must not, so the type reads as a contract among those actors over those acts.[^3] Oblige a party the file never declared, or name an act outside the list, and the file is refused. Declare an act as performed by one actor and oblige another to perform it, and the compiler says so in a sentence: `An actor may only perform its own actions.` `` `eat` is performed by `Eater`, not by `Drinker`. `` I ran that on a ten-line file. It is an error, and the file does not compile.[^3]

What can go wrong in an encoding of a rulebook and stay wrong comes in three kinds: a payout formula that adds a date to an amount, a duty on a party the instrument never names, a case the drafter did not consider. Luca Cardelli's handbook chapter on type systems calls errors of that kind untrapped, as against the trapped ones that stop the program. The untrapped "may go unnoticed for an arbitrary length of time."[^5] L4's checker turns the first two into refusals before anything runs. It has no type for money, and a duration is a plain number, which is the slot the borrowed year fit into. The third it leaves alone. A case analysis that misses a branch draws a warning — `The following branches still need to be considered: WHEN Closed THEN` — and the file compiles anyway. A draft of our own paper for the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Law (ICAIL) says the checker "rejects" such a rule; the version of the tool on my laptop warns.[^8] A warning that stops nothing is the one people learn to ignore, so the paper should say warns.

The nearest ancestor checked less. The 1986 encoding of the British Nationality Act at Imperial College ran in Prolog, which has no static types: a predicate you wrote yourself, asked about the wrong kind of term, fails rather than errs, and the failure carries no reason. What Catala and eFLINT, the two closest living languages, refuse and what they let run is in the note.[^7]

## The text is the thing checked

The crowdfunding file puts the rule's own words inside the rule: the three contents paragraph (b) permits are quoted, sentence for sentence, between the conditions. A quoted string in a boolean position is the identity for its connective — true inside an `AND`, false inside an `OR` — so the sentence is inert to the machine and live to the reader.[^2] Trevor Bench-Capon and Frans Coenen argued for this correspondence in 1992 under the name isomorphism, "the well defined correspondence of the knowledge base to the source texts," a term they trace to 1989. The Imperial encoding, they note, "was not necessarily supposed to reflect the structure of these sources."[^9] Three roads lead there. The standards track binds by reference: Akoma Ntoso marks up the legal text and LegalRuleML carries the rules beside it, pointing in. Catala binds by adjacency, a literate module in which "each paragraph of law is immediately followed by its Catala transcription." This file binds by inclusion: the sentence is an operand of the typed value it belongs to. Three bindings, three ways to come apart: a pointer can dangle, a paragraph and its transcription can drift, and an inlined sentence can be edited away from its source.[^9]

An earlier post in this series, [Specs are code](03-specs-are-code.md), was about the surface that makes such quoting possible. What it changes here is what gets checked against what. A model beside the text is checked against itself, and someone then checks the text against the model. When the file quotes the text, a wrong deadline is found by reading the rule in the file against the rule in the CFR, which is how this one was found. The fix reads section 227.204, finds that it sets no clock and that its prohibition is bounded by the offering it speaks of, and then confirms the defect by trace.[^1]

## Must, may, shall not

Types say what a thing is. The second half of the file says what a party must do, may do, and must not do, and that is a different kind of sentence. "The buyer must pay" has no truth value in a situation; it is met or breached as events unfold. Georg Henrik von Wright gave must and may a logic in 1951, and its standard system, as the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy puts it, "does not allow for conflicts to begin with."[^10] Law is made of conflicts and exceptions, so the artificial-intelligence-and-law community built defeasible deontic logics, and Guido Governatori and his co-authors made them compute.[^11]

L4 took the road from the programming side. Tom Hvitved's Contract Specification Language, in a Copenhagen thesis of 2011, treats a contract as the set of timed event sequences that conform to it. L4's regulative layer is a descendant.[^12] The five words are `PARTY`, `MUST` (or `MAY`, or `SHANT`), `WITHIN`, `HENCE`, `LEST`: who, what, by when, what follows if they do, what follows if they do not. Obligation, permission and prohibition are three shapes of process, and they differ in what counts as success. For a `MUST`, doing the act by the deadline is success and the deadline passing is breach. For a `SHANT` the polarity flips: the deadline passing in silence is the success, and the act is the breach.[^13] That flip is what turned a borrowed deadline into a defect. A `SHANT` with a `WITHIN` is a prohibition that ends. The compiler, knowing that, did exactly what it was told: on day 400 the silence had lasted long enough, and the issuer's advertisement matched nothing.

The file carries two test traces for this rule — scenarios written down in advance, an event and the verdict expected, re-run against every later draft. An issuer publishing a conforming notice is `FULFILLED`; an issuer publishing one that overreaches is in `BREACH`. Both act on day 10. I ran both against the pre-fix draft and they pass there too; they were blind to the defect for the whole three days. The trace that separates the two drafts is one line, an overreaching notice on day 400, and it is not in the file.[^14]

## What it does not check

The crowdfunding deadline was a requirements error in a well-typed file. Nancy Leveson had that kind in mind when she told James Somers, for his 2017 piece on formal methods in _The Atlantic_ — the way in, if this is new to you — that "the serious problems that have happened with software have to do with requirements, not coding errors."[^23] A day is a day, and a count of days borrowed from Rule 501 fits the slot in Rule 204 as neatly as leaving it empty would. A finer type — a deadline that carries the section it came from — does not exist, and I am not proposing one. Nor would another example test have caught it, because the test and the bug come from the same blind spot: nobody writes a day-400 trace for a prohibition they believe has no end. What separates the drafts is a property over the whole file: no `WITHIN` whose anchor event is not named in its own section; no prohibition in this file that is ever `FULFILLED` after the prohibited act. L4 cannot check a property like that today. `l4 prove` is proposed, its first design decisions are settled, and no prover has been written.[^18] Tests catch a different class of defect, and a later post in this series, planned, is about them.

One defect, found by its own author, fixed in three days, in a corpus we curate, is the shape of evidence James Woodcock and Peter Gorm Larsen warn about in the formal-methods case-study literature. "Survivorship bias is plausible" is their phrase for it, and the warning applies here before it applies to anyone else.[^20] What the fix log records is a numerator. In this same file, one more well-typed wrong meaning since: a one-year period encoded as 365 days, found on 5 August by running two independent encodings against each other after neither side's tests had reached it. Across the corpus, ten more on 5 September: in ten places the machine had read a negation as covering more of the sentence than its author meant, and the ten were found by reading the parser's source, where no test had looked. Twelve found by three methods, none of them the type checker, in six weeks, in a corpus of 575 files under `jl4/examples`, 26 of them encodings of legal instruments, carrying about 4,500 assertions, evaluations and traces, 94 of them in this file. That is a defect count with no bound on it. Nothing we have estimates how many remain, and the ones not found are not counted, because nothing counts them.[^20]

The largest thing it does not check is a power. A pedestrian may leave. A police officer arrests, and the pedestrian's `MAY leave` becomes `SHANT leave`. Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld, in the Yale Law Journal in 1913, had a name for what the officer holds. It is a power, one of the eight conceptions he called "the lowest common denominators of the law" — and he kept it apart from what the pedestrian had: a privilege, the absence of a duty and not a protected permission.[^15] Four of his eight are about conduct and four about changing the rules of conduct, the floor H. L. A. Hart called secondary rules. The encoding maps duty to `MUST` and privilege to `MAY`, a deliberate flattening that drops two things Hohfeld kept: that every relation runs between two named parties, and that a privilege carries no protection against interference. A power, on this reading, is a function from one party's position to another's. The reading is the received one, not ours, and eFLINT, from Amsterdam, has run "the ability to grant or remove permissions or duties" since 2020.[^16] A permission only ever says whether an act is allowed. What the arrest changed is what the pedestrian's leaving counts as.

How much of that runs today? Part. A permission can carry a consequence, and the consequence can be another party's prohibition — `PARTY Officer MAY detain … HENCE (PARTY Person SHANT leave …)`. I ran a scratch probe of that shape; it is not yet in our repository, so its full text is in the note. When the officer detains at hour one and the person leaves at hour five, the result is breach; when nobody detains and the person leaves, nothing is breached.[^17] That is a `HENCE`: a branch written into the contract from the start, which became active when the permission was exercised. It models the effect of a valid arrest and says nothing about validity, which is what a power actually governs. It cannot do the other half of a power either, naming a position that already exists and revoking it. The draft specification for that — `GRANT`, `REVOKE`, positions as values — carries a status header, audited on 3 July 2026, listing those keywords as not implemented, and the experiment file that sketches the police case does not parse on the installed binary.[^18] The second floor is designed. It is not built.

The critics of putting law into code have the second half in view, and they do not all say one thing. Tom Barraclough, Hamish Fraser and Curtis Barnes, reporting to the New Zealand Law Foundation in 2021, concluded that rules written in code "should never be given the status of legislation" and that "Code should remain subordinate to legislation."[^25] The file above meets that: a second text that quotes the first, checked against itself by a compiler and against the first by a reader, and never the instrument. Mireille Hildebrandt's objection of 2020 it does not meet: "What code-driven law does is to fold enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke, collapsing the distance between legislator, executive and court."[^24] That stroke falls when a decision service returns an answer to a citizen, and `jl4-service` already serves one. Quoting the CFR inside the rule only makes the fold visible, so that the interpretation sits beside the sentence it interprets, where a reader can dispute it.

The neural tools now write files of this kind, in a neighboring language. The best published measurement I found is a 2025 workshop paper: GPT-4.1, shown sixteen worked examples, translated 89 French statute provisions into Catala. Of its output, 88.8 percent parsed, give or take 5.6 at 90 percent confidence. Its similarity to the reference translation, on the paper's percentage-scaled composite where an identical translation scores 100, was 52.2, give or take 6.0 — parsing far more often than meaning the right thing. That was a 2025 model; I looked for the same measurement on a later one and did not find it.[^21] Georgetown's Beeck Center, after a summer of experiments in 2024 on food-assistance and Medicaid rules, found that large language models (LLMs) "still require external knowledge and human oversight within an iterative process for any policies containing complex logic."[^27] Our own file was written with a model recorded as co-author and a human signing the commit, and it carried a well-typed wrong deadline for three days. What a checker adds to that pipeline is an independent verdict: the refusal does not come from the distribution that produced the error. What it cannot add is the refusal of a well-typed wrong norm, which is where the reader, and the test, come in. If you are the issuer, what a file like this gives you today is an answer with the rule's own sentence beside it, checked by a machine that refuses some wrong files and passes others. The file that said yes had been read by the compiler, every line of it, and found well formed; and it was. The compiler never reads the CFR. Only the reader does.

_Where this goes next. The surface that lets the reader do that job is the subject of a paper in draft with Adam Wyner, "Syntactic Affordances for Isomorphic Legal Formalisation," aimed at the workshop on Controlled Natural Language (CNL) and not yet submitted.[^28] The power as a typed operator, with the model checker that would search it, is a section of "Deontics as Domination," a paper in draft for JURIX, the conference on Legal Knowledge and Information Systems; the section's claim to be "model-checked" cannot be submitted until a prover has been written, and none has.[^22] Neither is published._
