**STATUS 2026-09-14: DRAFT — machine-drafted and persona-reviewed; not yet read by Meng. Citations checked live on 2026-09-14.**

For three days in July 2026 the encoding of the Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC) crowdfunding rules in our repository held that an issuer who advertised the terms of its offering on day 400 was in the clear. Rule 204(a)(1) of Regulation Crowdfunding forbids that advertising, except through a notice that says no more than paragraph (b) allows, and the encoding said so: the issuer shall not advertise the terms of the offering. But an earlier draft had given the prohibition a deadline — one year, borrowed from a different rule about a different party, the period in which a *purchaser* may not resell the securities — and a prohibition with a deadline expires when the deadline does. From day 366 the issuer was free. The compiler had passed the file. A compiler is the program that reads your program before it runs and refuses it if the pieces do not fit, and here every piece fit.[^1]

A defect in a file our own group wrote is the right place to start, because a compiler is a filter, and a filter is described by what it lets through.

## The world first

The file declares its world before it says anything about conduct. Four kinds of actor: `Issuer`, `Intermediary`, `Investor`, `Purchaser`. Seven kinds of act, from filing a Form C offering statement to transferring the securities. Every dollar threshold is bound once, as a named constant dated per amendment, so the same rule can be asked what it said in 2017. Every sentence of rule text quoted inside the file is copied from the electronic Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) as it stood on 23 July 2026, and the header says so.[^2] A programmer knows the shape: types first, then functions over them. A lawyer knows it from the other end: definitions first, then the operative clauses.

Once the world is declared, a rule is a value of a type, and the compiler can hold it to the type. A regulative rule in L4 — the programming language for law that our group at Singapore Management University has built over six years — has the type `DEONTIC Actor Action`, a contract among those actors over those acts. Oblige a party the file never declared, or name an act outside the list, and the file is refused. Declare an act as performed by one actor and oblige another to perform it, and the compiler says so in a sentence: `An actor may only perform its own actions.` `` `eat` is performed by `Eater`, not by `Drinker`. `` I ran that today on a ten-line file; it is an error, and the file does not compile.[^3]

The refusal has a lineage. Robin Milner proved in 1978 that under his type discipline "Well-Typed Expressions Do Not Go Wrong" — the title of the section — with the checker already running in ML at Edinburgh.[^4] Luca Cardelli's handbook chapter opens on the purpose, "to prevent the occurrence of execution errors during the running of a program," and singles out the untrapped ones, which "may go unnoticed for an arbitrary length of time."[^5] Daniel Jackson and Jeannette Wing named the wider family in 1996, lightweight formal methods: partial specification, partial analysis, error detection ahead of proof.[^6] A type checker is the lightweight formal method every programmer already runs, each time the editor underlines a line, without calling it one.

What is an execution error in law? The 1986 encoding of the British Nationality Act at Imperial College ran in Prolog, which has no types; a rule applied to the wrong kind of thing fails to answer, and nothing says why.[^7] A rulebook's untrapped errors are Cardelli's kind: a payout formula that adds a date to a sum of money, a duty on a party the instrument never names, a case the drafter did not consider. The first two are refused; the third is not. A case analysis that misses a branch draws a warning — `The following branches still need to be considered: WHEN Closed THEN` — and the file compiles anyway, with exit code zero. Our own draft for the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Law (ICAIL) says the checker "rejects" such a rule; the binary on my machine warns.[^8] A warning at exit zero is the one people learn to ignore, so the paper should say warns.

## The text is the thing checked

The crowdfunding file is not a model of Rule 204 drawn beside it. The rule's own words sit inside the rule: the three contents paragraph (b) permits are quoted, sentence for sentence, between the conditions, where the evaluator reads past them and a reader reads them.[^2] That is what Trevor Bench-Capon and Frans Coenen argued for in 1992 under the name isomorphism, "the well defined correspondence of the knowledge base to the source texts," a term they trace to 1989. The Imperial encoding, they note, "was not necessarily supposed to reflect the structure of these sources." Their case for keeping the structure was engineering: validation "through an examination of the knowledge base itself," and maintenance, since "a given source change can be related to a defined fragment of the knowledge base."[^9]

An earlier post in this series was about the surface that makes such quoting possible. Here the point is narrower: it changes what the compiler is checking. A model beside the text is checked against itself, and someone then checks the text against the model. When the file quotes the text, the reader who finds a wrong deadline finds it by reading the rule in the file against the rule in the register, which is how this one was found: section 227.204 has no temporal limit; its only temporal word is "period," inside a definition, bounding nothing.[^1]

## Must, may, shall not

Types say what a thing is. The second half of the file says what a party must do, may do, and must not do, and that is a different kind of sentence. "The buyer must pay" is not true or false of a situation; it is met or breached as events unfold. Georg Henrik von Wright gave the must and the may a logic in 1951, in a paper in *Mind* titled "Deontic Logic," and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy calls him "the central early figure in the emergence of deontic logic as a full-fledged branch of symbolic logic."[^10] The paradoxes were waiting — Alf Ross's in 1941, that an obligation to mail the letter yields, by ordinary logic, an obligation to mail it or burn it; Roderick Chisholm's in 1963, about what you must do once you have failed to do what you must — and the standard logic, the encyclopedia says, "does not allow for conflicts to begin with."[^10] Law is made of conflicts and exceptions, so the artificial-intelligence-and-law community built defeasible deontic logics — a 1997 collection carries the name — and Guido Governatori and his co-authors made them compute.[^11]

L4 took the road from the programming side. Tom Hvitved's Contract Specification Language, in a Copenhagen thesis of 2011, treats a contract as the set of timed event sequences that conform to it, and L4's regulative layer is a descendant.[^12] The five words are `PARTY`, `MUST` (or `MAY`, or `SHANT`), `WITHIN`, `HENCE`, `LEST`: who, what, by when, what follows if they do, what follows if they do not. Obligation, permission and prohibition are three shapes of process rather than three operators, and they differ in what counts as success. For a `MUST`, doing the act by the deadline is success and the deadline passing is breach. For a `SHANT` the polarity flips: the deadline passing in silence is the success, and the act is the breach.[^13] That flip is what turned a borrowed deadline into a defect. A `SHANT` with a `WITHIN` is a prohibition that ends, and the compiler, knowing that, did exactly what it was told: on day 400 the silence had lasted long enough, and the issuer's advertisement matched nothing. Today, with the deadline removed, the file's two test traces run as the rule reads. An issuer publishing a conforming notice is `FULFILLED`; an issuer publishing one that overreaches is in `BREACH`.[^14]

## A may over a may

There is a second floor, and it is older than deontic logic. Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld, in two articles in the Yale Law Journal in 1913 and 1917, set out eight conceptions he called "the lowest common denominators of the law" and arranged them as opposites and correlatives. Four are about conduct — right and duty, privilege and no-right — a `MUST` and a `MAY`, seen from both parties. Four are about changing the rules of conduct: power and liability, immunity and disability. "A power," he wrote, "is one's affirmative 'control' over a given legal relation as against another; whereas an immunity is one's freedom from the legal power or 'control' of another as regards some legal relation."[^15]

To a programmer the second floor is a function over the first. A pedestrian may leave. A police officer may arrest, and the arrest resets the pedestrian's `MAY leave` to `SHANT leave`. The officer's power is a permission whose object is another party's permission: a may over a may. The trade word is higher-order, a function that takes a function, and the "first-order" and "second-order" that commentators have pinned on Hohfeld turn out to be the order of the type.[^16]

How much of that runs today? Part. A permission can carry a consequence, and the consequence can be another party's prohibition — `PARTY Officer MAY detain … HENCE (PARTY Person SHANT leave …)`. I ran a scratch file of that shape this morning: when the officer detains at hour one and the person leaves at hour five, the result is breach; when nobody detains and the person leaves, nothing is breached. Exercising the permission installed the prohibition.[^17] What does not run is the other half of a power: naming a position that already exists and revoking it. The draft specification for that — `GRANT`, `REVOKE`, positions as values — carries a status header, audited on 3 July 2026, listing those keywords as not implemented, and the experiment file in the tree that sketches the police case does not parse on the installed binary.[^18] The second floor is designed. It is not built.

It was also, in large part, built by others first. Layman Allen and Charles Saxon's A-Hohfeld, at ICAIL in 1995, was a language for "precisely expressing alternative structural interpretations of sets of legal rules" that "draws heavily upon the fundamental legal conceptions formulated by Wesley N. Hohfeld." Andrew Jones and Marek Sergot in 1996 formalized institutional power as the way "designated agents are empowered to create particular kinds of states of affairs by means of the performance of specified types of actions": a head of department signs, and the employer owes.[^19] Two languages have made it run. eFLINT, from Amsterdam in 2020, rests "in transition systems and in Hohfeld's framework of legal fundamental conceptions" and "supports the legal concept of power – the ability to grant or remove permissions or duties to/of actors." Symboleo, from Ottawa the same year, builds contracts from obligations and powers, a power being "the right of a party to create, change, suspend or extinguish legal positions."[^20] Catala, the Inria language for statutes, took the other classic road and made prioritized default logic — a general case, then exceptions, ordered — its foundation.[^21] Our own design note, after a prior-art pass, concedes in terms that the higher-order reading of Hohfeld is the received view and that what we could claim is a typed, model-checked instantiation. That residue is a paper, not a post.[^22]

## What it does not check

Nancy Leveson, in James Somers's 2017 piece on formal methods in *The Atlantic* — the way in, if this is new to you — says the thing that bounds everything above: "The serious problems that have happened with software have to do with requirements, not coding errors."[^23] The crowdfunding deadline was a requirements error in a well-typed file. A day is a day, and a count of days borrowed from Rule 501 fits the slot in Rule 204 as neatly as leaving it empty would. A finer type — a deadline that carries the section it came from — is not in the tree, and I am not proposing one; what would have caught it is a test, which is a later post, planned.

The critics of putting law into code have the second half in view. Philip Leith answered the Imperial group in 1986 by saying they "have a muddled view of the legal process": law is its practice, and its text is only part of that.[^7] Mireille Hildebrandt in 2020: "What code-driven law does is to fold enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke, collapsing the distance between legislator, executive and court."[^24] Barraclough, Fraser and Barnes told the New Zealand Law Foundation in 2021 that enacting legislation as code "undermines the separation of powers and the role of the Judiciary," and that law as code "must remain subordinate to written law."[^25] For an enacted rule I accept all three, and the file above is what accepting them looks like: a second text that quotes the first, checked against itself by a compiler and against the first by a reader. The compiler never reads the register. Only the reader does.

The neural tools now write these files. A benchmark at the Natural Legal Language Processing workshop in 2025 had GPT-4.1, given sixteen examples of French statute text paired with Catala, produce Catala whose syntax was valid 88.8 percent of the time; a preprint of June 2026 proposes making a verifier's verdict on generated Catala the reward that trains the next model.[^26] Georgetown's Beeck Center, after a summer of experiments on food-assistance and Medicaid rules, found that large language models (LLMs) "still require external knowledge and human oversight within an iterative process for any policies containing complex logic."[^27] What a compiler adds to that pipeline is the refusal: a model cannot refuse its own output, and a checker can. What it cannot add is the refusal of a well-typed wrong norm, which is where the reader, and the test, come in.

The surface that lets the reader do that job is the subject of a paper in draft with Adam Wyner, planned for the workshop on Controlled Natural Language (CNL), "Syntactic Affordances for Isomorphic Legal Formalisation."[^28] The second floor — the power as a typed operator, and the model checker that would search it — is a section of "Deontics as Domination," in draft for JURIX, the conference on Legal Knowledge and Information Systems, into which the Hohfeld material was folded on 18 July 2026 after the prior-art pass.[^22] Neither is published, and neither will claim more than the crowdfunding file can show.
