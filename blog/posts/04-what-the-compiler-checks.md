**STATUS 2026-09-14: DRAFT — machine-drafted, persona-reviewed and revised once; not yet read by Meng. Citations checked live on 2026-09-14.**

For three days in July 2026 our encoding of the Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC) crowdfunding rules — Regulation Crowdfunding, the rulebook a startup lives under when it raises money from the public — held that an issuer who advertised the terms of its offering on day 400 was in the clear. Rule 204(a)(1) forbids that advertising, except through a notice that says no more than paragraph (b) allows or a communication within Rule 206, and the encoding said so: the issuer shall not advertise the terms of the offering. But an earlier draft had given the prohibition a deadline. The year was borrowed from a different rule about a different party — Rule 501 of the same regulation, which stops a *purchaser* reselling the securities for a year from the day they were issued — and a prohibition with a deadline expires when the deadline does. Rule 204 names no event to count from, so the count ran from whatever moment a test invoked it. I rebuilt that draft today from the commit before the fix and ran it: an issuer advertising on day 365 is in breach, on day 366 it is not, and the compiler passes the file with two words, `Check succeeded.`[^1]

## The world first

The file declares its world before it says anything about conduct. Four kinds of actor: `Issuer`, `Intermediary`, `Investor`, `Purchaser`. Seven kinds of act, from filing a Form C offering statement to transferring the securities. Every dollar threshold is bound once, as a named constant dated per amendment, so the same rule can be asked what it said in 2017. Every sentence of rule text quoted inside the file is copied from the electronic Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) as it stood on 23 July 2026, and the header says so.[^2] A programmer knows the shape: types first, then functions over them. A lawyer knows it from the other end: definitions first, then the operative clauses.

Once the world is declared, a rule is a value of a type, and the compiler can hold it to the type. A regulative rule — one that says what a party must do, as against a constitutive rule, which says what counts as what — has in L4 the type `DEONTIC Actor Action`: *deontic* being the logicians' word for must, may and must not, so the type reads as a contract among those actors over those acts. Oblige a party the file never declared, or name an act outside the list, and the file is refused. Declare an act as performed by one actor and oblige another to perform it, and the compiler says so in a sentence: `An actor may only perform its own actions.` `` `eat` is performed by `Eater`, not by `Drinker`. `` I ran that today on a ten-line file; it is an error, and the file does not compile.[^3]

Daniel Jackson and Jeannette Wing named the family this belongs to in 1996: lightweight formal methods, an approach that "emphasizes partiality and focused application" — partial in its language, its modeling, its analysis and its composition — and "can bring greater benefits at reduced cost."[^6] A type checker is the lightweight formal method every programmer already runs, each time the editor underlines a line, without calling it one, and the theorem behind it is Robin Milner's of 1978, that well-typed expressions do not go wrong.[^4] Luca Cardelli's handbook chapter says what wrong is, dividing execution errors into the trapped ones, which stop the program, and the untrapped ones, which "may go unnoticed for an arbitrary length of time."[^5]

What is an execution error in law? The 1986 encoding of the British Nationality Act at Imperial College ran in Prolog, which has no types; a goal put to the wrong kind of thing fails, and a failed goal looks the same whatever caused it.[^7] A rulebook's untrapped errors are Cardelli's kind: a payout formula that adds a date to a sum of money, a duty on a party the instrument never names, a case the drafter did not consider. The first two are refused; the third is not. A case analysis that misses a branch draws a warning — `The following branches still need to be considered: WHEN Closed THEN` — and the file compiles anyway, with exit code zero, which is the machine reporting success. Our own draft for the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Law (ICAIL) says the checker "rejects" such a rule; the binary on my machine warns.[^8] A warning at exit zero is the one people learn to ignore, so the paper should say warns.

## The text is the thing checked

The crowdfunding file is not a model of Rule 204 drawn beside it. The rule's own words sit inside the rule: the three contents paragraph (b) permits are quoted, sentence for sentence, between the conditions, where the evaluator reads past them and a reader reads them.[^2] That is what Trevor Bench-Capon and Frans Coenen argued for in 1992 under the name isomorphism, "the well defined correspondence of the knowledge base to the source texts," a term they trace to 1989. The Imperial encoding, they note, "was not necessarily supposed to reflect the structure of these sources." Their case for keeping the structure was engineering: validation "through an examination of the knowledge base itself," and maintenance, since "a given source change can be related to a defined fragment of the knowledge base."[^9] The standards track took the other road to the same correspondence: Akoma Ntoso marks up the legal text, LegalRuleML carries the rules beside it and points into it by reference — both open standards, one since 2018 and one since 2021 — where this file puts the sentences inside the rule. Two bindings, with two ways to come apart: a pointer can dangle, and an inlined sentence can be edited away from its source.[^9]

An earlier post in this series was about the surface that makes such quoting possible. What it changes here is the thing the compiler is checking. A model beside the text is checked against itself, and someone then checks the text against the model. When the file quotes the text, the reader who finds a wrong deadline finds it by reading the rule in the file against the rule in the CFR, which is how this one was found: section 227.204 has no temporal limit; its only temporal word is "period," inside a definition, bounding nothing.[^1]

## Must, may, shall not

Types say what a thing is. The second half of the file says what a party must do, may do, and must not do, and that is a different kind of sentence. "The buyer must pay" is not true or false of a situation; it is met or breached as events unfold. Georg Henrik von Wright gave the must and the may a workable logic in 1951, in a paper in *Mind* titled "Deontic Logic" — Ernst Mally had tried in 1926 and produced a system in which, as the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy puts it, what ought to be collapses into what is the case — and the paradoxes were waiting, Ross's and Chisholm's among them; the standard logic, the encyclopedia says, "does not allow for conflicts to begin with."[^10] Law is made of conflicts and exceptions, so the artificial-intelligence-and-law community built defeasible deontic logics — a 1997 collection edited by Donald Nute carries the name — and Guido Governatori and his co-authors made them compute.[^11]

L4 took the road from the programming side. Tom Hvitved's Contract Specification Language, in a Copenhagen thesis of 2011, treats a contract as the set of timed event sequences that conform to it, and L4's regulative layer is a descendant.[^12] The five words are `PARTY`, `MUST` (or `MAY`, or `SHANT`), `WITHIN`, `HENCE`, `LEST`: who, what, by when, what follows if they do, what follows if they do not. Obligation, permission and prohibition are three shapes of process rather than three operators, and they differ in what counts as success. For a `MUST`, doing the act by the deadline is success and the deadline passing is breach. For a `SHANT` the polarity flips: the deadline passing in silence is the success, and the act is the breach.[^13] That flip is what turned a borrowed deadline into a defect. A `SHANT` with a `WITHIN` is a prohibition that ends, and the compiler, knowing that, did exactly what it was told: on day 400 the silence had lasted long enough, and the issuer's advertisement matched nothing.

The file carries two test traces for this rule — scenarios written down in advance, an event and the verdict expected, re-run against every later draft. An issuer publishing a conforming notice is `FULFILLED`; an issuer publishing one that overreaches is in `BREACH`. Both act on day 10. I ran both against the pre-fix draft today and they pass there too; they were blind to the defect for the whole three days. The trace that separates the two drafts is one line, an overreaching notice on day 400, and it is not in the file.[^14]

## The second floor

There is a second floor, and it is older than deontic logic. Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld, in two articles in the Yale Law Journal in 1913 and 1917, set out eight conceptions he offered, "if a homely metaphor be permitted," as "the lowest common denominators of the law," and arranged them in a table of opposites and correlatives — each with its negation, and each with its view from the other party. Four are about conduct: right and duty, privilege and no-right, which is his coinage for having no claim against what another is free to do — a `MUST` and a `MAY`, seen from both sides. Four are about changing the rules of conduct: power and liability, where liability means being subject to another's power rather than owing money, and immunity and disability. "A power," he wrote, "is one's affirmative 'control' over a given legal relation as against another; whereas an immunity is one's freedom from the legal power or 'control' of another as regards some legal relation."[^15]

To a programmer the four that change the rules are functions over the four about conduct. A pedestrian may leave. A police officer arrests, and the pedestrian's `MAY leave` becomes `SHANT leave`. The officer holds a power, and a power on this reading is a function from one party's position to another's; the trade word is higher-order, and the "first-order" and "second-order" that commentators have pinned on Hohfeld become, on this reading, the order of the type.[^16] The reading is not ours: Layman Allen and Charles Saxon had built a language on Hohfeld's conceptions by 1995, and eFLINT, from Amsterdam, has run "the ability to grant or remove permissions or duties" since 2020, with more neighbors in the note.[^19] One thing the function view must not do is fold a power into a permission, which is the confusion Hohfeld's table exists to keep apart. The two come apart in both directions: an agent forbidden to sign may still bind the principal, and answer for it afterward — power without privilege — and a person free to act may hold no power to change anyone's position at all. A power governs whether an act *counts*; a permission governs whether it is allowed.

How much of that runs today? Part. A permission can carry a consequence, and the consequence can be another party's prohibition — `PARTY Officer MAY detain … HENCE (PARTY Person SHANT leave …)`. I ran a scratch file of that shape this morning: when the officer detains at hour one and the person leaves at hour five, the result is breach; when nobody detains and the person leaves, nothing is breached.[^17] That is a `HENCE`: a branch written into the contract from the start, which became active when the permission was exercised. It models the effect of a valid arrest and says nothing about validity, which is what a power actually governs; and it cannot do the other half of a power, naming a position that already exists and revoking it. The draft specification for that — `GRANT`, `REVOKE`, positions as values — carries a status header, audited on 3 July 2026, listing those keywords as not implemented, and the experiment file in the tree that sketches the police case does not parse on the installed binary.[^18] The second floor is designed. It is not built.

## What it does not check

Nancy Leveson, in James Somers's 2017 piece on formal methods in *The Atlantic* — the way in, if this is new to you — puts it this way: "The serious problems that have happened with software have to do with requirements, not coding errors."[^23] The crowdfunding deadline was a requirements error in a well-typed file. A day is a day, and a count of days borrowed from Rule 501 fits the slot in Rule 204 as neatly as leaving it empty would. A finer type — a deadline that carries the section it came from — is not in the tree, and I am not proposing one. What would have caught it is the day-400 trace: a scenario someone wrote down before the rule was right, run against every draft after. The file's two traces did not, and tests are a later post, planned.

One defect, found by its own author, fixed in three days, in a corpus we curate, is the shape of evidence Jim Woodcock and Peter Gorm Larsen warn about in the formal-methods case-study literature — "survivorship bias is plausible" is their phrase for it — and the warning applies here before it applies to anyone else.[^20] The denominator, as far as the fix log records it: in this same file, one more well-typed wrong meaning since, a one-year period encoded as 365 days, found on 5 August by running two independent encodings against each other after neither side's tests had reached it; across the corpus, ten more on 5 September, negations whose operand swallowed the connective beside them, found by reading the parser rather than any file. Twelve found by three methods, none of them the type checker. The ones not found are not counted, because nothing counts them.[^20]

The critics of putting law into code have the second half in view. Philip Leith attacked the Imperial group in 1986 for having "a muddled view of the legal process": law is its practice, and its text is only part of that.[^7] Mireille Hildebrandt in 2020: "What code-driven law does is to fold enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke, collapsing the distance between legislator, executive and court."[^24] Tom Barraclough, Hamish Fraser and Curtis Barnes, reporting to the New Zealand Law Foundation in 2021, concluded that rules written in code "should never be given the status of legislation," since enacting code "risks undermining the separation of powers," and that "Code should remain subordinate to legislation."[^25] For an enacted rule I accept all three, and the file above is what accepting them looks like: a second text that quotes the first, checked against itself by a compiler and against the first by a reader. The compiler never reads the CFR. Only the reader does.

The neural tools now write these files. A benchmark at the Natural Legal Language Processing workshop in 2025 had GPT-4.1, shown sixteen worked examples, translate 89 French statute provisions — tax, family allowances, housing benefit, drawn from the example corpus of Catala, the Inria language for statutes that has been run against section 121 of the US Internal Revenue Code and the French family-benefits computation — and 88.8 percent of its output parsed, give or take 5.6 at 90 percent confidence; its similarity to the reference translation, on the paper's CodeBLEU score, was 52.2, give or take 6.0. A preprint of June 2026 proposes making a verifier's verdict on generated Catala the reward that trains the next model.[^21] Georgetown's Beeck Center, after a summer of experiments on food-assistance and Medicaid rules, found that large language models (LLMs) "still require external knowledge and human oversight within an iterative process for any policies containing complex logic."[^27] What a compiler adds to that pipeline is the refusal: a model cannot refuse its own output, and a checker can. What it cannot add is the refusal of a well-typed wrong norm, which is where the reader, and the test, come in.

The surface that lets the reader do that job is the subject of a paper in draft with Adam Wyner, planned for the workshop on Controlled Natural Language (CNL), "Syntactic Affordances for Isomorphic Legal Formalisation."[^28] The power as a typed operator, and the model checker that would search it — no verification backend exists in the tree today; `l4 prove` is a proposal with its first phase ruled and nothing built — are a section of "Deontics as Domination," in draft for JURIX, the conference on Legal Knowledge and Information Systems, into which the Hohfeld material was folded on 18 July 2026 after a prior-art pass that found the higher-order reading to be the received view.[^22] Neither is published, and neither will claim more than the crowdfunding file can show.
