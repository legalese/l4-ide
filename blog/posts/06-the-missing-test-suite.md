For a single person in France with a salary of €20,000 in 2012, the income-tax variable is expected to come out at −1,181, within half a euro. That sentence is a test. It sits in the documentation of OpenFisca, the open-source engine behind several countries' tax-and-benefit models, as seven lines of YAML, the plain-text format build tools use for structured data: a name, a period, an input, an expected output, a tolerance. The command `openfisca test` runs it, and every file like it, after each change to the rules. The page's advice to anyone writing a formula is the advice every programmer has heard: at least one test per formula, and better, cases at the boundaries, "thresholds for example."[^1]

A test suite — a set of cases, each an input paired with the answer its author expects, rerun after every edit — is the most ordinary thing in software. Law has had half of it for a century and a half. The Indian Penal Code of 1860, which the Straits Settlements, Singapore among them, re-enacted in 1871, follows its definition of culpable homicide with a heading, *Illustrations*, and a case: A lays sticks and turf over a pit, meaning to cause death; Z, believing the ground firm, treads on it, falls in and is killed; A has committed culpable homicide. That is an input paired with the answer the drafter expects. The US Treasury's tax regulations carry paragraphs headed *Example 1* by the thousand, each with its arithmetic worked.[^2] What none of them has is a runner. Nothing executes the illustration against the section, and when the section is amended nothing turns the illustration red. Statutes ship with worked cases and no way to run them, and the reason is plain: you cannot run prose.[^3]

Post 1 of this series told the story of New Zealand's rates rebate. In May 2019 it had thirteen test cases behind it, and a defect in its "excess income" term. That defect was found twice within a week, once by a maintainer reading the code and once by us charting an early encoding; the numbers and the caveats are in that post's notes.[^4] Thirteen is more than most encoded law has, and every one of the thirteen could be run.

At negotiation, each party writes down the dozen scenarios it cares about most. Those become tests. Every redraft, from either side, runs them, so that the latest edit has not broken anything — and when a test fails, the two sides are arguing about a case rather than a comma. The encoding can be regenerated and the tool can be replaced; the twelve cases each side wrote on the day they signed are a record, for their own later selves, of what each thought the deal was for. Whether a court would ever look at that record is a question for a planned post on what formalization cannot do, and nothing here depends on the answer. What a founder could do with the pieces that exist today is for the series' planned closing post.[^5]

We have come within one reading of it once, by accident, with one side in the room. In 2023 a team of an insurer's claims adjusters were assisting us with a pilot, encoding one of the insurer's own policies, and the payout formula, once we tried to write it down exactly, admitted two readings. The insurer had been paying the larger one. Two figures for the same claim, from one sentence, is a test nobody had written: a case on which two readings disagree. When we presented the finding at the top of the company we worded it so that the adjusters who had helped us were not the ones exposed for overpaying. The presentation is lost, and this is my recollection of it; post 1 carries the rest of that case.[^6]

## What one encoding carries

The corpus that has been through this most often is our encoding of the Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC) Regulation Crowdfunding, the rules under which an eligible company can raise up to $5,000,000 in any twelve months from the public, through a single intermediary. The encoding covers the issuer's side of the regulation — who may offer and how much, how much each investor may put in, what must be disclosed, what the intermediary must do, what may be advertised, what must be reported afterward, and when the shares may be resold — and leaves out the registration of the funding portals themselves, the detail of the bad-actor disqualifications, and three smaller items its README lists.[^7] The file `regcf.l4` runs to 1,444 lines and carries 83 assertions; a second file, the deployable face of the same rules, carries 29 more. Run on 14 September 2026, all 83 were satisfied. The first four read as follows, and the fixtures they name — a clean issuer, a foreign one, a delinquent filer, a blank-check company — are defined a few lines above:[^7]

```l4
#ASSERT     `issuer is eligible` `a clean issuer`
#ASSERT NOT `issuer is eligible` `a foreign issuer`
#ASSERT NOT `issuer is eligible` `a delinquent issuer`
#ASSERT NOT `issuer is eligible` `a blank-check issuer`
```

The corpus's README keeps a table of the boundaries, at least one case on each side of every threshold the corpus encodes: $5,000,000 is allowed and $5,000,001 is not; 21 days of availability passes and 20 fails; 299 holders of record is under the threshold and 300 is on it; a transfer on day 365 is permitted and one on day 364 is restricted.[^7] That is the OpenFisca page's advice, followed. No figure came from the wiki page the corpus was built to mirror; where the page and the Code of Federal Regulations disagree, the regulation governs and the README lists the disagreements.

An assertion has four outcomes. Two are verdicts, satisfied and failed. The other two are the evaluator declining to give one. *Could not be evaluated* means a fact the rule needs was never supplied — the assertion asks about x and nothing has said what x is — and it is a breakdown: `l4 run` exits non-zero on it. *Refused* means the rule itself declines the case as outside its domain: the regulation took effect in 2016, so an encoding of it has no figure for 2015 and says so.[^8]

A decision rule says whether something holds: is this issuer eligible, does this notice comply. A regulative rule puts a party under a duty with a clock on it — must file this form within so many days, must not transfer these shares before a year is out. Eight `#TRACE` directives play the duties out against events. An issuer that files its termination form on day 30, with five business days to do it, is reported in breach with the deadline named; the encoding counts those business days as plain days, a simplification the README lists first among the things it could not express. A buyer who resells a hundred days after issue to an ordinary investor is reported in breach of the one-year restriction. A buyer who resells to an accredited investor is not, because the rule excepts that sale, and the trace says no prohibition was left standing.[^7]

The same file yields more than tests. The decision rules lower — compile down — to a Decision Model and Notation (DMN) model, the business-rules standard for decision tables that a process team can hand to an engine they already run: 70 decisions in 12 tables. Two engines, Camunda's and Drools, evaluate that model over 23 committed cases and return, at every one of the 70 decision nodes, the value the L4 evaluator expected; the projection page reports this as 1,610 of 1,610, which is the 23 cases counted once per node.[^9] It is evidence that the lowering is faithful and both engines deterministic, and no evidence about the regulation, because the expected values were supplied by our evaluator. That is the house rule for every projection: the expected output in an engine's test file is populated from L4's own evaluator, never accepted from the engine, so the evaluator that runs the 83 assertions is the reference answer — the oracle, in the testing vocabulary — for all of them.[^10] The three regulative rules lower to three Business Process Model and Notation (BPMN) processes. The same `@export` annotation feeds five compilers out of L4: to docassemble, the guided-interview platform used across access-to-justice work; to OpenFisca; to Catala, the literate language for law with a proof assistant behind it; to Blawx, the blocks-based rules tool over the s(CASP) reasoner; and to DMN and BPMN. A deployed set of rules answers as a web API and as tools under the Model Context Protocol (MCP) that an AI client can call.[^11]

The house rule for which projection to want is on the export page: pick by what you need to do, "not by which is most sophisticated."[^11] Nobody wants an encoding.[^12] The benefits office wants its OpenFisca module, the clinic wants its interview, the process team wants a table its engine can run, and the tests are what lets one file be all three without saying three different things.

What runs today, from one line, is the replay of a committed encoding, end to end. The instruction is "⟨body of law⟩: go" — "SEC Regulation Crowdfunding: go" was the first — and it names a pipeline of ten stages. Typed for Regulation Crowdfunding, it checks the corpus, runs the assertions, emits the projections and runs the verifier, and its driver writes a receipt for every stage; the receipt writer refuses a status nicer than its evidence. The stages that would take a statute from source text to an encoding — fetch, sweep, encode, find the forks — do not fetch, search or encode. They validate what a person or a model has deposited in their place, and report SKIPPED when nothing is there. The publish stage prints what it would do and exits.[^13] Between the checks and the tests stands a gate: the assertions will not be run until a named person has read the encoding against the regulation section by section and signed, with their own key, a digest of the files they read. Edit the corpus afterward and the signature stops verifying.[^13]

One more artifact the pipeline keeps, because the tests need it. Where the words admit two readings — is a person born on the day an Act commenced born "after commencement"? — the encoder, person or model, is told not to guess silently but to record what our documents call a fork: the site, the readings, the one taken, and why. Regulation Crowdfunding has two encodings in the repository, the committed one and a second made from scratch on the pipeline's deposit path; the committed register holds 7 entries and the fresh one 26. The insurer's payout formula would have been one entry. The specification for the tests stage asks for two kinds of case against a register like that: properties that hold under every reading, and witness cases where two readings disagree, minimized and pinned — the pair we found by hand in 2023, searched for. That is the design; nothing in the repository searches for them yet.[^14]

## Who wrote tests for rules before us

Kent Beck's first step, in his 2023 restatement of his 2002 book on test-driven development (TDD), is to "write a list of the test scenarios you want to cover" before writing code; the dozen scenarios at negotiation are that list with two authors.[^15] Koen Claessen and John Hughes published QuickCheck at the International Conference on Functional Programming (ICFP) in 2000: properties "described as Haskell functions" and "automatically tested on random input," which is the second kind of test, a claim over a whole domain rather than a case.[^16] Gojko Adzic's *Specification by Example*, in 2011, made the shared list of examples a method for a business and its developers, in a notation that runs.[^17]

Running a statute against cases is older than any of that. In 1986 Marek Sergot, Robert Kowalski and four colleagues at Imperial College published a large part of the British Nationality Act 1981 as a logic program. They had shown it to Home Office officials in January 1984, and they said in print how far it had been tested: "a limited number of test applications, primarily to test the citizenship of various real and hypothetical individuals." The 1990s then had a literature, and a European symposium series, on validating and verifying knowledge-based systems, of which a statute in Prolog is one.[^18] Rules engines have had suites for years. Oracle Policy Modeling, whose users have included a national tax authority and an immigration department, defines its test cases in Excel; its manual says "test cases should always be created whenever rules are developed" and that "hundreds or even thousands" can sit in one project.[^19] New Zealand's Better Rules exercise ran over three weeks in 2018 with legislative drafters and software developers on one team, on two Acts, one of them the Rates Rebate Act; the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) wrote in 2020 that coded rules "could enable testing of individual scenarios, to ensure the intent of the policy meets the likely outcomes given a person's circumstances."[^20]

The closest thing to a statute shipping with a runnable suite is the French income tax. Denis Merigoux, Raphaël Monat and Jonathan Protzenko reverse-engineered the tax authority's own calculation, written in a custom language from 1990, and reported at the compiler-construction conference in 2021 that their replacement compiler, Mlang, "has been thoroughly validated against the private DGFiP test suite" — the Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP) being the authority — and that the authority was moving its production system onto it. Catala, the same group's language for law and one of the five targets above, uncovered a bug in an official implementation by encoding the text it was evaluated on.[^21] That is one computation in one administration, against a test suite the public cannot see, and it took a research team years. But it is the thing, and it exists.

The tax modelers check each other. PolicyEngine, a fork of OpenFisca's core, runs its US model on every release against the tax calculator the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) maintains and calls a result close when it is within $100.[^22] That is testing by comparison with another implementation, which needs one to exist. Where none does, Saeid Tizpaz-Niari's group, testing US tax-preparation software in 2023, named the barriers — "the unavailability of explicit specifications and the difficulty of obtaining oracles" — and got around them by comparing similar taxpayers, whose outcomes should move together.[^23] The DMN standard has a conformance kit of 3,391 tests against which ten vendors have posted results, the latest in June 2026; the contract side, from the Accord Project's template tests to the fuzzers for Ethereum contracts and the computable-contracts literature behind them, is in the notes.[^24]

So the line of descent is long. What I do not find in it is the adversarial pair: two parties keeping their own scenarios against one draft, with the failing test as the agenda for the next call. Specification by example and its cousin, behavior-driven development, put a business and its developers on the same executable examples; neither puts counterparties on opposite sides of them. Whether the negotiation-support literature — Gregory Kersten and Hsiangchu Lai surveyed it in 2007 — ever ran a draft against both sides' cases I could not check today, and nothing here rests on it.[^25]

## What a passing suite does not prove

"Program testing can be a very effective way to show the presence of bugs, but is hopelessly inadequate for showing their absence." That was Dijkstra's Turing lecture in 1972, and it applies to every number above.[^26] Eighty-three assertions satisfied is eighty-three cases someone thought of. The corpus's README still reports 70, from an older run, and the page that coordinates our backends warns in its own header that a figure is "the highest-risk thing a cell can hold," because it reads as evidence whether or not anyone re-ran it.[^7] A failed assertion does not even fail the run: `l4 run` exits zero on it, and a build server has to read the JSON output for the word false. A breakdown fails the build; a wrong answer does not.[^8]

The exhibit was chosen because it is the best-exercised, which is the survivorship point post 5 makes in its own words. Of the 26 encodings under `jl4/examples/legal`, 12 carry no assertions at all, and Regulation Crowdfunding's 83 is not the largest count: the Singapore succession cleanroom files carry 429, 404, 381, 352 and 341. Half the corpus has no cases, and nothing above says anything about that half.[^27]

Everything that runs today is example-based. QuickCheck's kind of test — a property over a generated domain — exists in our 2019 prototype as my recollection of what it made possible, and in the specification for the pipeline's tests stage as a plan. There is no generator over L4 rules in the repository, and the compiler's own suite is examples too: four golden files per corpus file, over some 300 files.[^28] The nearest thing to a property is a check that every one of those 300 files survives being printed, re-parsed and re-typechecked. That check was green while the printer was wrong. The pretty-printer had been dropping a bracket, so that `(TRUE OR FALSE) AND FALSE` and `TRUE OR (FALSE AND FALSE)` came out as the same text; the printed source parsed, type-checked, and evaluated to a different answer. A list in one test file went from `TRUE, TRUE, FALSE, FALSE` to `TRUE, TRUE, TRUE, TRUE`; an assertion in the Regulation Crowdfunding corpus went from satisfied to failed; a guard in the encoding of Tesla's CEO award re-associated. Every existing check stayed green throughout. Re-parsing is not re-meaning.[^28]

The verifier that ships, `l4 verify`, checks the and-or shape of each rule and never looks inside a comparison, so `x > 5 AND x < 3` is invisible to it, and a boundary assertion like the one at $5,000,001 is what catches an arithmetic mistake. On this corpus it reports zero findings, and the review page bounds what that is worth: 25 of the 42 rules it analyzed have exactly one condition, 111 of the corpus's 154 rules are outside the analysis because they do not return a yes-or-no answer, and 7 more are nested where it does not reach. "Findings are sound; silence is not a consistency proof."[^28]

And satisfied means satisfied under the readings we took. The committed encoding records 7 forks and the fresh one 26; every assertion holds relative to the reading chosen at each, and a different, equally defensible reading could turn assertions red with nothing in the encoding wrong. Until the divergence witnesses exist, no test would notice.[^14] One of the eight traces, too, explains its verdict in a sentence I cannot read as English: an issuer that publishes a non-conforming notice on day ten is reported to have "surpassed the deadline" of a duty it "had to do," where the duty is a prohibition. The verdict is the one the encoder's own comment predicts; the message was written for a *must* and reused for a *must not*, and a trace is only as good as its explanation.[^7]

Nothing in our record shows the negotiation-stage scenarios run with a party. Of our five engagements, the one that touched a commercial agreement — a payments company whose contracts were full of fee tables, which we encoded and served through what I described at the time as an SQL-like query interface — was an integration, and only one side was in the room; the insurer's two readings were found by us, not kept by a counterparty.[^5] Nothing in the repository implements two private suites against one draft; it would be a way of working with the tools that exist, and it is untested.

The stronger objection is sixty-three years old. Stewart Macaulay interviewed 68 businessmen and lawyers from 43 companies and six law firms, all but two of the companies with plants in Wisconsin, and reported in 1963 that "businessmen often fail to plan exchange relationships completely, and seldom use legal sanctions to adjust these relationships or to settle disputes"; the explanation he offered "emphasizes a considered choice not to plan in detail for all contingencies," though "at times it is clear that businessmen fail to plan because of a lack of sophistication."[^29] A dozen scenarios asks each party to do the planning Macaulay's respondents had decided against. Where he is right, the tests will be the ones the lawyers wrote for each other. Where the parties already plan — an insurer whose formula runs on every claim, a benefits agency, a regulator, and a statute, which has no counterparty to decline — the tests were always wanted, and the missing thing was something to run them against.

The neural tools are arriving at the same place from the other side. Tizpaz-Niari's group reported in 2025 an agent framework in which large language models (LLMs) write the tax code's implementation and a second agent generates metamorphic tests and searches for counterexamples; with a small model it reached a worst-case pass rate of 45 percent on their harder tasks, against 9 to 15 for two larger ones.[^23] Our design divides the labor the same way, with one difference: a model may propose the scenarios, and the L4 evaluator, running over an encoding a named person has signed, is the only thing that says whether they pass.

The argument in academic dress is a paper in draft for ProLaLa, the workshop on programming languages and the law held with the Symposium on Principles of Programming Languages (POPL); its sixth section is headed "The Missing Test Suite," and post 5 of this series, on the ladder of checks from the serial comma up to model checking — the exhaustive search of a system's states for one that breaks a stated property — is already drafted.[^3] The tests are the practitioner's end of that paper. A statute that ships with its cases is a statute someone can change without wondering what broke.
