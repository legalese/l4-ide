For a single person in France with a salary of €20,000 in 2012, the income tax is expected to come out at −1,181, within half a euro. The minus sign is the model's bookkeeping: OpenFisca-France writes tax owed as a negative number. That sentence is a test. It sits in the documentation of OpenFisca, the open-source engine behind several countries' tax-and-benefit models, as seven lines: a name, a period, an input, an expected output, a tolerance. The command `openfisca test` runs it, and every file like it, after each change to the rules. The page's advice to anyone writing a formula: at least one test per formula, and better, cases at the boundaries, "thresholds for example."[^1]

Law has had half of that for a century and a half. The Indian Penal Code of 1860, which the Straits Settlements, Singapore among them, re-enacted in 1871, follows its definition of culpable homicide with a heading, *Illustrations*, and a case. In the Indian text: A lays sticks and turf over a pit, intending to cause death or knowing death is likely; Z, believing the ground firm, treads on it, falls in and is killed; A has committed culpable homicide. That is an input paired with the answer the drafter expects. The US Treasury's tax regulations carry paragraphs headed *Example 1* by the thousand, 2,975 sections of Title 26 among them, and the ones I opened work the arithmetic through.[^2] A test is subordinate to the code: when they disagree, the test is presumed right and the code is the bug. An illustration is enacted with its section, and the Privy Council said in 1916 that a court's duty is to accept it "as being both of relevance and value in the construction of the text." When section and illustration seem to disagree, the answer is a question of construction, not a bug report.[^2] What no statute has is a runner: nothing executes the case against the section, and when the section is amended nothing turns the case red. You cannot run prose.[^3]

Post 1 of this series told the story of New Zealand's rates rebate, a discount on a homeowner's property rates that shrinks as income rises. In May 2019 it had thirteen test cases behind it and a defect in its "excess income" term, coded so that the excess could go negative and the rebate rise past the threshold; a maintainer reading the code and our chart of an early encoding — a body of rules rewritten in a language a machine can run — found it within a week of each other.[^4] Thirteen is more than most encoded law has, and every one of the thirteen could be run.

The idea I have carried since is for contracts. At negotiation, each party writes down the dozen scenarios it cares about most. Those become tests. Every redraft, from either side, runs them, so that the latest edit has not broken anything, and when a test fails the two sides are arguing about a case rather than a comma. The twelve can be written today, in English, by anyone with a draft in front of them, and they are worth having before anything can run them: a record, for each side's later self, of what it thought the deal was for. Like any contemporaneous note, the other side can ask to see it, and whether a court would ever look at it is a question for a planned post on what formalization cannot do.[^5]

We have come within one reading of it once, by accident, with one side in the room. In 2023 — this is my recollection, and the slides are lost — a team of an insurer's claims adjusters were assisting us with a pilot, encoding one of the insurer's own policies, and the payout formula, once we tried to write it down exactly, admitted two readings. The insurer had been paying the larger one. [NEEDS MENG: the shape of the two readings, names and figures filed off, or a ruling that confidentiality forbids even that.] Two figures for the same claim, from one sentence, is a test nobody had written: a case on which two readings disagree. When we presented the finding at the top of the company we worded it so that the adjusters who had helped us were not the ones who could be blamed for the larger payments; post 1 carries the rest of that case, including the rule of construction that reads an ambiguity against the insurer who wrote it.[^6]

## What one encoding carries

The corpus that has been through this most often is our encoding of the Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC) Regulation Crowdfunding: the regulation rewritten, clause by clause, in L4, the language we built so that rules can be run. Regulation Crowdfunding is the SEC's rules for the exemption that lets an eligible company sell up to $5,000,000 of securities to the public in any twelve months, through a single intermediary, without registering the offering. The encoding covers the issuer's side, from who qualifies to when the shares may be resold; its README lists what it leaves out.[^7] The file `regcf.l4` runs to 1,444 lines and carries 83 assertions; a second file, the deployable face of the same rules, carries 29 more. Run on 14 September 2026 with a binary built from the same checkout as the corpus, all 83 were satisfied. The first four read as follows; the fixtures they name are defined a few lines above them, and a blank-check company, in the regulation's words, is one that "has no specific business plan or has indicated that its business plan is to engage in a merger or acquisition with an unidentified company."[^7]

```l4
#ASSERT     `issuer is eligible` `a clean issuer`
#ASSERT NOT `issuer is eligible` `a foreign issuer`
#ASSERT NOT `issuer is eligible` `a delinquent issuer`
#ASSERT NOT `issuer is eligible` `a blank-check issuer`
```

The corpus's README keeps a table of boundaries, one case on each side of every threshold it encodes: $5,000,000 is allowed and $5,000,001 is not; 21 days of availability passes and 20 fails; 299 holders of record is under the threshold and 300 is on it; a transfer one year after issue, on day 365, is permitted and one on day 364 is restricted.[^7]

An assertion has four outcomes. Two are verdicts, satisfied and failed. The other two are the evaluator declining to give one. *Could not be evaluated* means a fact the rule needs was never supplied, and it is a breakdown: `l4 run` exits non-zero on it. *Refused* means the rule itself declines the case as outside its domain: the regulation took effect in 2016, so an encoding of it has no figure for 2015 and says so.[^8]

A decision rule says whether something holds: is this issuer eligible, does this notice comply. A regulative rule, the term of art for the other kind, puts a party under a duty or a prohibition, usually with a clock on it: must file this form within so many days, must not transfer these shares before a year is out. Eight `#TRACE` directives play the duties out against events, the compliance-checking shape Guido Governatori's group has run against business processes since 2006.[^20] An issuer that files its termination form on day 30, with five business days to do it, is reported in breach with the deadline named; the encoding counts business days as plain days, the first simplification its README lists. A buyer who resells a hundred days after issue to an ordinary investor is reported in breach of the one-year restriction. A buyer who resells to an accredited investor — in the SEC's definition, a person with a net worth above $1,000,000 or an income above $200,000 in each of the last two years, or an institution on a list — is not, because the rule excepts that sale, and the trace says no prohibition was left standing.[^7]

The same file feeds other engines. The decision rules compile down to a Decision Model and Notation (DMN) model, the business-rules standard for decision tables: 70 decisions in 12 tables. Two engines, Camunda's and Drools, evaluate it over 23 committed cases and agree with the L4 evaluator at every one of the 70 decision nodes; the projection page reports this as 1,610 of 1,610, the cases counted once per node.[^9] It is evidence that the lowering agrees with the evaluator on those cases and no evidence about the regulation, because the expected values came from our evaluator. A proof that the lowering preserves meaning on every input is the property one would want, and we do not have it. The house rule for every projection is that expected outputs come from L4's own evaluator and never from the engine, so the evaluator that runs the 83 assertions is the reference answer for all of them.[^10] The other targets are in the notes; none changes what the tests say.[^9]

Between the checks and the tests stands a gate. The assertions will not be run until a named person has read the encoding against the regulation section by section and signed, with their own key, a digest of the files they read. Edit the corpus afterward and the signature stops verifying. The gate sits inside a ten-stage pipeline whose one-line instruction, "⟨body of law⟩: go," is typed to a model working in the repository, which dispatches a shell driver; what the driver runs today, and what it only validates, is in the notes.[^11]

One more artifact the pipeline keeps, because the tests need it. Where the words admit two readings, the encoder is told not to guess silently but to record what our documents call a fork: the site, the readings, the one taken, and why. The example our review page gives is from the British Nationality Act: is a person born on the day the Act commenced born "after commencement"? That fork the Interpretation Act 1978 in fact closes, at section 4, under which an Act commences "at the beginning of that day"; the encoder took that reading and recorded the other as rejected. Knowing which forks the statute book has already closed is part of the job.[^12] Regulation Crowdfunding has two encodings in the repository, the committed one and a second made from scratch on the pipeline's deposit path; the committed register holds 7 entries and the fresh one 26. The insurer's payout formula would have been one entry. The specification for the tests stage asks for two kinds of case against a register like that: properties that hold under every reading, and witness cases where two readings disagree, minimized and pinned — the pair we found by hand in 2023, searched for. Nothing in the repository searches for them yet.[^12]

## Who wrote tests for rules before us

Someone has run statutes against cases for forty years. In 1986 Marek Sergot, Robert Kowalski and four colleagues at Imperial College published a large part of the British Nationality Act 1981 as a logic program, had shown it to Home Office officials in January 1984, and said in print how far it had been tested: "a limited number of test applications, primarily to test the citizenship of various real and hypothetical individuals."[^14] Rules engines and government rules-as-code programs have carried suites since then.[^15][^16] The software lineage, from Kent Beck's list of scenarios to QuickCheck's properties and specification by example, is in the notes.[^13]

Three projects come closest to a body of law that ships with a runnable suite. OpenFisca-France is public and anyone can run its tests, but it models the tax-and-benefit system rather than encoding the statute's text. Mlang is in production: Denis Merigoux, Raphaël Monat and Jonathan Protzenko reverse-engineered the French tax authority's calculation and reported in 2021 that their compiler "has been thoroughly validated against the private DGFiP test suite," the Direction générale des Finances publiques (DGFiP) being the authority; the suite is private. Catala, the same group's language for law, encoded the French family benefits and found a case the official simulator's OpenFisca code got wrong; its test cases were the authors' own, not the statute's.[^17] The exhibit above differs from all three narrowly: the suite lives with an encoding of the text, the projections are checked against one evaluator, and a signed human reading gates the run.

The tax modelers check each other. PolicyEngine, a fork of OpenFisca's core, runs its US model on every release against the calculator the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) maintains and calls a result close within $100. In July 2026 the Axiom Foundation announced more than 3,000 provisions of law encoded to its RuleSpec standard, every available one "cross-checked against external engines and datasets," PolicyEngine and NBER's calculator among them.[^18] At that scale, comparison with another implementation is the most direct present-day answer to the title above, and its reach is tax-and-benefit arithmetic; the duties, clocks and breach verdicts of the Reg CF traces have no second engine, so they are checked by cases and a signature instead. Where no second implementation exists, Saeid Tizpaz-Niari's group, testing US tax-preparation software in 2023, compared similar taxpayers instead, for want of "explicit specifications" and "oracles."[^19]

The contract strand has given contracts formal specifications, with analysis on top, for twenty years: Andersen, Henglein and colleagues published a compositional specification language for commercial contracts in 2006, Governatori and Milosevic formalized a business contract language the same year, and Symboleo, since 2020, specifies legal contracts for analysis. Procurement contracts annex acceptance tests, agreed cases run against a deliverable.[^20] Requirements engineering's viewpoints literature holds that two stakeholders' disagreement over one artifact is the useful thing.[^21] What I do not find in any of it is two privately held, adversarially maintained suites run against a live draft during negotiation, with the failing case as the agenda for the next call. Viewpoints are held by collaborators; acceptance tests are agreed, not kept apart; the contract languages analyze one settled model.

## What a passing suite does not prove

"Program testing can be a very effective way to show the presence of bugs, but is hopelessly inadequate for showing their absence." That was Dijkstra's Turing lecture in 1972, and it applies to every number above.[^22] Eighty-three assertions satisfied is eighty-three cases we thought of, scored against our own encoding, by the evaluator that is also the thing under test. Nothing outside the project supplied an expected value; the Penal Code's illustration, the Treasury's example and DGFiP's suite each have one. The corpus's README still reports 70 from an older run, and the page that coordinates our backends warns that a figure is "the highest-risk thing a cell can hold," because it reads as evidence whether or not anyone re-ran it.[^7] A failed assertion does not even fail the run: `l4 run` exits zero on it, and a build server has to read the JSON output for the word false. A breakdown fails the build; a wrong answer does not.[^8]

The exhibit was chosen because it is the best-exercised: the survivorship point Jim Woodcock and Peter Gorm Larsen make about the formal-methods case studies at Amazon applies to us first. Of the 26 files under `jl4/examples/legal`, 12 carry no assertions, some because their cases live in a sibling module, and Regulation Crowdfunding's 83 is not the largest count: the Singapore succession cleanroom files carry 429, 404, 381, 352 and 341. Nothing above says anything about the twelve.[^23]

Everything that runs today is example-based. A property over a generated domain, QuickCheck's kind of test, exists in our 2019 prototype as my recollection and in the tests-stage specification as a plan. There is no generator over L4 rules in the repository, and the compiler's own suite is examples too: four golden files per corpus file, over some 300 files.[^24] The nearest thing to a property is a check that every one of those files survives being printed, re-parsed and re-typechecked. That check was green while the printer was wrong. The pretty-printer had been dropping a bracket, so that `(TRUE OR FALSE) AND FALSE` and `TRUE OR (FALSE AND FALSE)` came out as the same text; the printed source parsed, type-checked, and evaluated to a different answer. A list in one test file went from `TRUE, TRUE, FALSE, FALSE` to `TRUE, TRUE, TRUE, TRUE`; an assertion in the Regulation Crowdfunding corpus went from satisfied to failed; a guard in the encoding of Tesla's CEO award re-associated. Every existing check stayed green. A rule answered one way before the round trip and the other way after, and nothing reported a problem.[^24]

The verifier that ships, `l4 verify`, checks the and-or shape of each rule and never looks inside a comparison, so `x > 5 AND x < 3` is invisible to it, and a boundary assertion like the one at $5,000,001 is what catches an arithmetic mistake. On this corpus it reports zero findings, and the review page bounds what that is worth with three numbers, given as the page gives them; they do not add up as a partition: 25 of the 42 rules it analyzed have exactly one condition, 111 of the corpus's 154 rules are outside the analysis for not returning a yes-or-no answer, and 7 more are nested where it does not reach. "Findings are sound; silence is not a consistency proof."[^24]

And satisfied means satisfied under the readings we took. The committed encoding records 7 forks and the fresh one 26; every assertion holds relative to the reading chosen at each, and a different, equally defensible reading could turn assertions red with nothing in the encoding wrong. Until the divergence witnesses exist, no test would notice.[^12] One of the eight traces, too, explains its verdict in a sentence I cannot read as English: an issuer that publishes a non-conforming notice on day ten is reported to have "surpassed the deadline" of a duty it "had to do," where the duty is a prohibition. The verdict is the one the encoder's own comment predicts; the message was written for a *must* and reused for a *must not*, and a trace is only as good as its explanation.[^7]

Nothing in our record shows the negotiation-stage scenarios run with a party. Of our five engagements, the one that touched a commercial agreement — a payments company's fee tables, which we encoded and served through what I described at the time as an SQL-like query interface — was an integration with one side in the room; the insurer's two readings were found by us, not kept by a counterparty.[^5] Nothing in the repository implements two private suites against one draft; it would be a way of working with tools that exist, and it is untested.

Stewart Macaulay interviewed 68 businessmen and lawyers from 43 companies and six law firms, most of them in Wisconsin, and reported in 1963 that "businessmen often fail to plan exchange relationships completely, and seldom use legal sanctions to adjust these relationships or to settle disputes"; his explanation "emphasizes a considered choice not to plan in detail for all contingencies," though "at times it is clear that businessmen fail to plan because of a lack of sophistication."[^25] A dozen scenarios asks each party to do the planning Macaulay's respondents had decided against. Where he is right, the tests will be the ones the lawyers wrote for each other. Where the parties already plan — an insurer whose formula runs on every claim, a benefits agency, a regulator, and a statute, which has no counterparty to decline — the tests were always wanted, and the missing thing was something to run them against.

The neural tools arrive at the same place from the other side: Tizpaz-Niari's group reported in 2025 an agent framework in which large language models (LLMs) write the tax code's implementation and a second agent generates metamorphic tests and hunts counterexamples.[^19] Our design divides the labor the same way, with one difference: a model may propose the scenarios, and only the L4 evaluator, over an encoding a named person has signed, says whether they pass.

The argument in academic dress is a paper in draft for ProLaLa, the workshop on programming languages and the law held with the Symposium on Principles of Programming Languages (POPL); its sixth section is headed "The Missing Test Suite."[^3] Post 5 of this series explains the rungs above testing, from the serial comma up to model checking; the tests are the practitioner's end of that ladder. A statute that ships with its cases is a statute someone can change without wondering what broke.
