A lays sticks and turf over a pit, meaning to cause a death, or knowing one is likely. Z, believing the ground firm, treads on it, falls in and is killed. A has committed culpable homicide.

That is the Indian Penal Code of 1860, directly under its definition of culpable homicide, beneath a heading that reads *Illustrations*; the Straits Settlements, Singapore among them, adopted the Code in substance in 1871, pit and all.[^2] It is an input paired with the answer the drafter expected, written by the drafter and enacted with the section. The US Treasury's tax regulations do the same by the thousand. Search Title 26 for the phrase "Example 1" and you get 2,975 hits; the one I opened, on an employer's chauffeured car, works the arithmetic through to $24,250.[^2]

A software test is not part of the program. Nobody ships it, nobody is bound by it, and when test and code disagree the code is what gets changed. An illustration is part of the law, not a check on it. What no statute has is a runner: nothing executes the pit against the section, and when the section is amended nothing turns the pit red. You cannot run prose.[^3]

Here is what the pit would look like if anyone could run it. OpenFisca is the open-source engine behind several countries' tax-and-benefit models, and its documentation carries a seven-line test in which the parts that matter are a name, a year, one input, one expected output and a tolerance. For a single person in France with a salary of €20,000 in 2012, income tax should come out at −1,181, within half a euro; the minus sign is bookkeeping, since OpenFisca-France writes tax owed as a negative number. The command `openfisca test` runs that case, and every file like it, after each change to the rules. The page's advice to anyone writing a formula: at least one test per formula, and better, cases at the boundaries, "thresholds for example."[^1]

Post 1 told the story of New Zealand's rates rebate, a discount on a homeowner's property rates that shrinks as income rises. In May 2019 the OpenFisca model of it had thirteen tests of that kind behind it, and a defect in its "excess income" term: below the allowable-income threshold the excess came out negative, and subtracting a negative pushed a low-income ratepayer's rebate up toward the $630 maximum. A maintainer reading the code found it. So, within a week and without either knowing of the other, did our chart of an early encoding — the rules rewritten in a language a machine can run.[^4] Thirteen cases, all runnable, sat beside the formula while the defect sat inside it.

We have produced one test of that kind ourselves, by accident, with one side in the room. In 2023 — this is my recollection; the slides are lost — a team of an insurer's claims adjusters were helping us encode one of the insurer's own policies, and the payout formula, once we tried to write it down exactly, admitted two readings. Two figures for the same claim, from one sentence. [NEEDS MENG: the shape of the two readings, names and figures filed off, or a ruling that confidentiality forbids even that.] The insurer went back through its files and found it had been paying the larger one; the gap, on my own estimate at the time, was potentially millions of dollars in claims. When we took the finding to the top of the company we worded it as a defect in the wording, so that the adjusters who had helped us could not be blamed for the larger payments, and put two court cases on the screen as the class of risk an ambiguous term carries. The executives talked about liability. Nobody said which figure future claims would be paid on. The fix went into the wording of the term, where the defect was, not into the code computing the payout.[^6] That pair of figures is a test nobody had written: a case on which two readings disagree.

The idea I have carried since is a proposal, for contracts, and nothing below tests it. At negotiation each party writes down the dozen scenarios it cares about most, and those become its suite — the whole set of tests, run together every time the draft changes. Each redraft, from either side, runs both suites, so the latest edit has not broken anything, and when a case fails the two sides are arguing about a case rather than a comma. Stewart Macaulay, interviewing 68 businessmen and lawyers in 1963, found that businessmen "often fail to plan exchange relationships completely," by considered choice, he thought, though sometimes for want of sophistication; the dozen asks for exactly the planning his respondents declined.[^25] The half that needs no tool is available now to anyone with a lease or a policy in front of them: write your twelve down, in English, before signing, as a record of what you thought the deal was for. The other side can ask to see that record, and whether a court would ever look at it is a question for a planned post on what formalization cannot do.[^5]

## What one encoding carries

The Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC) Regulation Crowdfunding is the rulebook for the exemption that lets an eligible company sell up to $5,000,000 of securities in any twelve months, through one intermediary, without registering the offering. Of the bodies of law we have encoded — rewritten, clause by clause, in L4, the language we built so that rules can be run — it is the one that has been through the most. The encoding covers the issuer's side, and its README lists what it leaves out.[^7] The main file runs to 1,444 lines and carries 83 assertions, each the OpenFisca test again, an input and the expected answer written into the rules file; a second file, which re-implements no law and exists to route plain-English inputs into the first file's rules for a form to render, carries 29 more. We ran the main file on 14 September 2026, with the tool built from the same version of the code as the rules, and all 83 came out as expected.[^7] The first four:

```l4
#ASSERT     `issuer is eligible` `a clean issuer`
#ASSERT NOT `issuer is eligible` `a foreign issuer`
#ASSERT NOT `issuer is eligible` `a delinquent issuer`
#ASSERT NOT `issuer is eligible` `a blank-check issuer`
```

The four issuers are made-up profiles defined a few lines above; the blank-check label is the encoder's, for Rule 100(b)(6)'s exclusion of an issuer that "has no specific business plan or has indicated that its business plan is to engage in a merger or acquisition with an unidentified company or companies."[^7] Boundaries get a case on each side, and the README keeps the table: $5,000,000 is allowed and $5,000,001 is not; 21 days of availability passes and 20 fails; 299 holders of record is under the threshold and 300 is on it; a transfer on day 365 after issue is permitted and one on day 364 is restricted.[^7] Ask the encoding for a figure from 2015, the year before the regulation took effect, and it does not fail. It refuses, because the rule declines the case as outside its domain. Leave out a fact the rule needs and it cannot evaluate at all; `l4 run` stops with an error.[^8]

Is this issuer eligible, does this notice comply: those are decision rules, and an assertion checks one. The other kind of rule puts a party under a duty or a prohibition, usually with a clock on it, and eight `#TRACE` directives play those out against a fixed sequence of events. An issuer that files its termination form on day 30, with five business days to do it, is reported in breach with the deadline named — the encoding counts business days as plain days, and its README says so. A buyer who resells a hundred days after issue to an ordinary investor is reported in breach of the one-year restriction. A buyer who resells to an accredited investor is not, because the rule excepts that sale. (Accredited investor is Rule 501(a)'s term for, broadly, high-net-worth or high-income individuals and institutions, on a definition with more conditions than this sentence carries.)[^7] Eight sequences, each replayed once; the compliance-checking literature this descends from is in the notes.[^20]

The same file feeds other engines. Its decision rules compile to Decision Model and Notation (DMN), a standard format for decision tables, and two DMN engines, run over 23 committed cases, agree with the L4 evaluator on every decision. That says the translation did not change the answers on those cases. It does not say we read the regulation right: the expected values came from our own evaluator, and the two engines are conformance-tested against the same standard test pack, so they are not two witnesses either. The property one would want is a proof that the translation preserves meaning on every input. We do not have it.[^9][^10]

All of this is designed to sit behind a gate. The pipeline meant to produce an encoding will not count an assertion as a verdict until a named person has read the encoding against the regulation, section by section, and signed for it, and if any file changes afterward the signature stops matching. Nobody has signed. No signer's key is enrolled, so the gate refuses, or is waived with a reason written down, and the 83 above were run with the command-line tool directly. Nor can the pipeline yet write an encoding. Typed one line — the name of a body of law, then "go" — it re-checks an encoding a human already wrote; the stages that would read a statute and produce one only inspect what a human deposited.[^11]

Where the words admit two readings, the encoder is told not to guess silently but to record a fork: the site, the readings, the one taken, and why. The example our review page gives is from the British Nationality Act. Is a person born on the day the Act commenced born "after commencement"? The encoder said yes, and recorded the other reading as rejected because it "would leave commencement-day births governed by neither the old law nor the new."[^12] Regulation Crowdfunding has two encodings in the repository, the committed one and a second made from scratch on the pipeline's deposit path. Their registers hold 7 forks and 26, and each accounts for its own count — the 7 as disclosures first argued in prose, the 26 as six inherited from an earlier stage plus twenty judgements the encoder made where the text left two readings standing — and both say, in their own words, that completeness cannot be claimed, since no procedure enumerates the ambiguities a statute contains.[^12] The insurer's payout formula would have been one entry. What the tests stage's specification asks for, against a register like that, is two kinds of case: properties that hold under every reading, and witness cases where two readings disagree, minimized and pinned — the pair we found by hand in 2023, searched for. That search is planned and unwritten.[^12]

## Who ran statutes against cases before us

Someone has done this for forty years. In 1986 Marek Sergot, Robert Kowalski and four colleagues at Imperial College published a large part of the British Nationality Act 1981 as a logic program. They had shown it to Home Office officials in January 1984, and they reported "a limited number of test applications, primarily to test the citizenship of various real and hypothetical individuals"; the idea of testing a statute before it takes force, they said, was Layman Allen's, in 1957.[^14] The rules engines, the government rules-as-code programs, the Internal Revenue Service's own test scenarios for e-filing software, the financial industry's shared trade models, the formal contract languages, and the software lineage from Kent Beck to QuickCheck are in the notes.[^13][^15][^16][^19][^20][^21]

Three projects come closest to a body of law that ships with a runnable suite.[^17] OpenFisca-France's tests are public, but it models the tax-and-benefit system rather than encoding the statute's text. Mlang, a reverse-engineering of the French tax authority's calculation, was "thoroughly validated against the private DGFiP test suite" — the Direction générale des finances publiques is the authority itself. Catala, the same group's language for law, assessed by the tax scholar Sarah Lawsky in 2022, found a case the official family-benefits simulator got wrong, with test cases its authors wrote themselves. The exhibit above differs from all three, narrowly: the suite lives with an encoding of the text, the projections are checked against one evaluator, and a signed reading is designed to gate the run. And it is weaker than one of them where it counts most. Mlang's expected values came from the tax authority. Every one of ours came from us. The tax modelers check each other — PolicyEngine against the National Bureau of Economic Research's calculator within $100 on every release, the Axiom Foundation's 3,000-odd RuleSpec provisions "cross-checked against external engines and datasets" — and that is the most direct check anyone runs on encoded law; its reach is tax-and-benefit arithmetic. The duties, clocks and breach verdicts of the Reg CF traces have no second engine.[^18]

## What a passing suite does not prove

"Program testing can be a very effective way to show the presence of bugs, but is hopelessly inadequate for showing their absence." That was Dijkstra's Turing lecture in 1972, and it applies to every number above.[^22] Eighty-three assertions satisfied is eighty-three cases we thought of, scored against our own encoding, by the evaluator that is also the thing under test. The corpus's README still reports 70 from an older run, and a figure reads as evidence whether or not anyone re-ran it.[^7] A failed assertion does not even fail the run: the tool prints the wrong answer and exits as if nothing were wrong, so a build server has to read the output for the word `false`. A breakdown stops the build. A wrong answer does not.[^8]

The suite is uneven across our own corpus. Of the 26 files under `jl4/examples/legal`, 12 carry no assertions, some because their cases live in a sibling file, and Regulation Crowdfunding's 83 is not the largest count: the Singapore succession files carry 429, 404, 381, 352 and 341. The twelve are covered by nothing above. Jim Woodcock and Peter Gorm Larsen, reviewing the formal-methods case studies at Amazon, found success stories with "little systematic data about neutral or unsuccessful adoption attempts," and that applies to us before it applies to Amazon. Every engagement this series describes produced a finding. The repository keeps no count of encodings begun and abandoned, of pilots that ended with nothing to report, or of assertions that failed and were weakened rather than fixed.[^23]

Everything that runs today is example-based. A property over a generated domain, QuickCheck's kind of test, exists in our 2019 prototype as my recollection and in the tests-stage specification as a plan. The compiler — the program that turns L4 into something that runs — has its own suite, and it is examples too: four golden files per corpus file, saved copies of what the tool printed last time, compared on every change, over some 300 files.[^24] The nearest thing to a property is a check that every one of those files survives being printed, re-parsed and re-typechecked. That check was green while the printer was wrong. The pretty-printer had been dropping a bracket, so that `(TRUE OR FALSE) AND FALSE` and `TRUE OR (FALSE AND FALSE)` came out as the same text. A list in one test file went from `TRUE, TRUE, FALSE, FALSE` to `TRUE, TRUE, TRUE, TRUE`. An assertion in the Regulation Crowdfunding corpus went from satisfied to failed. A guard in the encoding of Tesla's CEO award re-associated. Every existing check stayed green.[^24]

The verifier that ships, `l4 verify`, checks the and-or shape of each rule and never looks inside a comparison, so `x > 5 AND x < 3` is invisible to it. On this corpus it reports zero findings. It reaches a minority of the rules — most return something other than yes or no and are outside it altogether — and of the 42 it analyzed, 25 have a single condition, which leaves on the order of seventeen with any shape it could find fault in. Its own page says "Findings are sound; silence is not a consistency proof," where sound means every finding it reports is real, not that it finds everything.[^24]

And satisfied means satisfied under the readings we took. Every assertion holds relative to the reading chosen at each fork, and a different, equally defensible reading could turn assertions red with nothing in the encoding wrong; until the divergence witnesses exist, no test would notice.[^12] One of the eight traces explains its verdict in a sentence I cannot read as English. An issuer that publishes a non-conforming notice on day ten is reported to have "surpassed the deadline" of a duty it "had to do," where the duty is a prohibition. The verdict is the one the encoder's comment predicts; the message was written for a *must* and reused for a *must not*.[^7]

The dozen scenarios at negotiation remain untested. Of the five engagements post 1 counts — a government agency, an insurer, a drafting office, a payments company and the New Zealand exercise — the one that touched a commercial agreement was the payments company's fee tables, which we encoded and served through what I described at the time as an SQL-like query interface: an integration, with one side in the room. The insurer's two readings were found by us; no counterparty kept them.[^5] Two private suites run against one live draft is a way of working with tools that exist, and the repository holds no instance of it.

The neural tools arrive at the same division of labor from the other side. In 2025 Saeid Tizpaz-Niari's group had large language models (LLMs) write a tax implementation while a second agent hunted counterexamples,[^19] and ours differs in one place: a model may propose the scenarios, and only the L4 evaluator says whether they pass. The argument in academic dress is a paper in draft for ProLaLa, the workshop on programming languages and the law, whose sixth section carries this title. Post 5 sets a model checker — a program that tries every path through a rule looking for one that breaks it — loose on a rule to find what a bad-faith reader would; the tests here are the cheaper end of that work.[^3]

Where the parties already plan — an insurer whose formula runs on every claim, a benefits agency, a statute, which has no counterparty to decline — the tests were always wanted; what was missing was something to run them against. Regulation Crowdfunding now has its runner and 83 cases, scored by us against ourselves, with nobody's signature on the reading. The Indian Penal Code's drafters wrote theirs into the Act in 1860, and the pit has been waiting for its runner since.
