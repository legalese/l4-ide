In 2016 six students sat Singapore's O-Level examinations with help. Accomplices at a tuition centre had registered as private candidates; the questions were streamed out of the hall by video, and answers came back through hidden phones, wireless receivers and earpieces, until an invigilator caught one of the students. The centre's principal, Poh Yuan Nie, and a teacher, Poh Min Fiona, were convicted of abetting cheating and took the case to the Court of Appeal, which decided it on 21 November 2022. They did not dispute the facts. They argued that what they had done was, as a matter of law, not cheating.[^1]

The argument ran through the definitions. Cheating under section 415 of the Penal Code requires deceiving someone. Explanation 1 to the section adds that "a dishonest concealment of facts is a deception within the meaning of this section." Section 24 defines "dishonestly" as doing a thing with the intention of causing wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another — gain and loss of property. No property changed hands at the tuition centre, so, the applicants said, no concealment there was "dishonest" in the defined sense, so there was no deception, so there was no cheating. Substitute the definition, simplify, walk free.[^1]

Oliver Wendell Holmes described the person making that argument in "The Path of the Law," which the Harvard Law Review printed in 1897. "If you want to know the law and nothing else, you must look at it as a bad man, who cares only for the material consequences which such knowledge enables him to predict, not as a good one, who finds his reasons for conduct, whether inside the law or outside of it, in the vaguer sanctions of conscience."[^2] Jurisprudence has read the Bad Man as a claim about what it is to know the law. A programmer reads him as a threat model: an adversary who takes the text as given and searches it for an input the drafter did not anticipate. Security researchers have a name for the person who runs that search and files a report instead of an exploit — a white hat — and the tool is the same in both hands. The letter of the law is the model; the spirit of the law is the property the drafter meant the model to have; a loophole is a counterexample, a run of the model on which the letter is satisfied and the spirit is not. The only thing the search leaves open is who runs it first.

## What the court did, and what a solver does

The Court of Appeal's answer to the applicants was mechanical in three of its four moves, and the three can be rerun. First it enumerated: paragraph 18 of the judgment lists four readings of "dishonest" in Explanation 1, from the applicants', on which only a property-minded concealment counts, to the one the court adopted, on which "dishonest" names the intention to deceive and nothing more. Second it built a counterexample, at paragraph 28: a candidate who submits a forged university degree to win an unpaid internship. No property moves; any reasonable layperson would say the employer was cheated; on the applicants' reading the forger walks. Third it showed that the applicants' reading left Explanation 1 with no work to do — "otiose" is the judges' word — since with no property in play a concealment could never change the outcome. The fourth move was to call the forger's acquittal absurd, and that one is a judgment about what the section is for.[^1]

The three mechanical moves are, in a compiler's vocabulary, a case split, a failing test and dead-code elimination, and an essay in our repository writes the case down that way. Section 415 becomes eleven Boolean atoms — "by deceiving any person," "dishonest concealment of facts," "intent to cause wrongful property gain or loss," and eight more — and the four readings become a four-way enumerated type over which the section is evaluated.[^3] This morning I ran the three checks that sit beside that model. The L4 evaluator convicts the forger under three readings, acquits him under the applicants', and passes its assertions. Z3, a satisfiability solver, proves that the applicants' reading can only ever acquit where the court's would convict; proves that across every no-property fact pattern the concealment atom is a don't-care under their reading; and, asked for any case the two readings classify differently, returns the forged-degree class unprompted: concealment, inducement, harm to mind, no property. Espresso, the Berkeley circuit minimizer, deletes the literal — eight product terms under the court's reading, four of them mentioning concealment; four under the applicants', none of them.[^3] The presumption against surplusage, which lawyers learn as a canon of construction, and dead-code elimination, which compiler writers learn as an optimization, are one computation run for opposite ends. The minimizer deletes the clause. The court deletes the reading that killed it.

What the solver cannot do is decide that letting the forger keep his internship offends the purpose of the section. The essay puts it as a slogan — a Boolean argument is a contradiction-detector, never a contradiction-resolver — and Parliament's own patch shows the boundary from the other side. In 2020 it added section 6A to the Penal Code, switching off the cognate-forms rule for "dishonestly" and "fraudulently" across the Code; the court noted that the amendment "was made out of an abundance of caution and was not intended to change the law."[^1] A clarifying commit with no change in behavior, and it took an Act and an appeal to establish that none was needed.

## The ladder

Boolean logic is the second rung of a ladder on which each rung is a bug class with a tool that finds it. The first is grammar: a sentence with two parse trees. Maine's overtime law exempted "canning, processing, preserving, freezing, drying, marketing, storing, packing for shipment or distribution of" perishable food, and in March 2017 the First Circuit held that the missing comma before "or distribution" left open whether distribution was an exempt activity on its own, and read the clause for the dairy's delivery drivers.[^4] A parser counts derivations and flags more than one. The third rung is types: a term used and never defined, a cross-reference to a section that no longer exists, a formula that adds a rate to a principal. The fourth is time. Post 1 of this series described a regulation in which, under one interleaving of the parties' permitted steps, one clause obliged an act that another prohibited: a race condition, found by exhausting a state space.[^5] An earlier study from our group had put the data-breach sections of Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) through Uppaal, a model checker for systems with clocks, as three interacting timed automata, and found a trace on which an organization is required to notify the regulator while still forbidden to, and a deadline that deadlocks [UNVERIFIED: the paper's repository copy was bot-walled today; the description is the one our own specifications record].[^5] The fifth rung is strategy. The question there is whether one party can force a bad outcome whatever the others do; Alur, Henzinger and Kupferman's alternating-time temporal logic (ATL), from 2002, is the formalism, a cap table with side letters accumulating over funding rounds is the case, and nothing on that rung is built.[^6]

Every rung has a past. Layman Allen proposed symbolic logic as a drafting tool in the Yale Law Journal in 1957, and post 1 told the story of the British Nationality Act in Prolog in 1986. Model checking was proposed by Edmund Clarke and Allen Emerson in 1981 and by Jean-Pierre Queille and Joseph Sifakis in 1982, and the three shared the 2007 Turing Award for it.[^7] Pace, Prisacariu and Schneider model-checked a contract language for conflicts in 2007, at a verification conference rather than a law one, and Symboleo, a specification language for contracts, gained a property checker in 2024.[^8] The black-hat search has its own precedent: in 2015 a group at MIT working with the Mitre Corporation modeled partnership-tax schemes and audit policies in one framework and let the schemes evolve against the audits, on the observation that closing a known scheme breeds a more profitable one; the paper took the award for best innovative application at the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Law (ICAIL) that June.[^9] Sarah Lawsky argued in the Florida Tax Review in 2017 that statutory reasoning is defeasible — rules with exceptions — and wants default logic rather than classical logic.[^10]

## What Amazon found, what it cost, and what the record does not show

The people who have run this kind of search longest on systems with money in them are at Amazon Web Services (AWS), and their decade has three beats.

What they found. Engineers at AWS began writing specifications in Leslie Lamport's TLA+ in 2011, and in a 2015 report in Communications of the ACM Chris Newcombe and five colleagues published the table: an 804-line PlusCal specification of a fault-tolerant network algorithm in S3, two bugs; a 939-line TLA+ specification of DynamoDB's replication and group membership, three bugs, "some requiring traces of 35 steps"; 102 lines for Elastic Block Store (EBS) volume management, three bugs.[^11] One author's first specification was of an algorithm with a known bug that "had passed unnoticed through multiple design reviews and code reviews, and had only surfaced after months of testing." He spent two weeks learning the language and writing the spec; the model checker found the bug in seconds; he added the team's reviewed fix, and the checker found the problem again by a different trace.[^11] Six years later Bornholt and eleven co-authors reported on ShardStore, a new S3 storage node of more than 40,000 lines of Rust, validated with executable reference models, property-based tests and stateless model checking rather than proofs: "Our work has prevented 16 issues from reaching production."[^12] James Somers's 2017 piece in The Atlantic is the way into this material for a reader who has not met TLA+, and Tim Bray's reply — that the single biggest boost to software quality since 2000 was unit testing — is the way to read it with the right skepticism; both are on-ramps.[^13]

What it cost, and who could do it. Newcombe's report says engineers from entry level to principal learned TLA+ "from scratch and get useful results in 2 to 3 weeks," some on evenings and weekends without help.[^11] The ShardStore paper's measure of adoption is a git-blame count: "18% of the lines of code in the test harnesses were last edited by a non-formal-methods expert," after two experts had built the harnesses over nine months and handed them to a team none of whom had formal-methods experience.[^12] Woodcock and Larsen's phrase for that is training-by-doing.[^14] In April 2023 the Linux Foundation stood up a TLA+ Foundation with AWS, Oracle and Microsoft as inaugural members.[^15]

What the record does not show. Jim Woodcock and Peter Gorm Larsen, two formal-methods researchers at York and Aarhus, published a critical evaluation of the whole public AWS record in Formal Aspects of Computing in June 2026, and their verdict is the one to carry. The case studies are success stories; there is "little systematic data about neutral or unsuccessful adoption attempts." No public source compares formal methods against fuzzing, static analysis, runtime monitoring or a targeted code review on the same system, so a return on the investment cannot be established from what has been published. Attribution is confounded, because the teams that write specifications also review, test and monitor. And keeping a specification true as the system under it changes is acknowledged as costly, with, in their words, "limited systematic evidence about long-term maintenance effort."[^14] Their own test for a practitioner, in section 3.5.3, is that formal methods are "most compelling when design-level defects are expensive, when systems are highly concurrent or fault-tolerant, and when teams can sustain the modelling and maintenance effort alongside conventional assurance."[^14]

Then there is the night of 19 October 2025. At 11:48 p.m. Pacific time, in AWS's Northern Virginia region, two copies of an automated component called the DNS Enactor — DNS being the Domain Name System, the directory that turns a service name into an address — raced. One had been unusually delayed; it applied a much older plan over a newer one, and the other's clean-up then deleted the older plan because it was many generations old, and DynamoDB's regional endpoint stopped resolving. AWS's own summary calls it "the race condition," and the disruption ended at 2:20 p.m. the next day.[^16] On 5 November Murat Demirbas posted a TLA+ model of the Planner and the Enactors on his blog, built on a Promela model by Waqas Younas and translated, he says, by asking ChatGPT; splitting the Enactor's action into receive, apply and clean up "exposed the race condition clearly."[^16] Nothing public says whether that automation had ever been specified. What the outage shows is the narrower thing Woodcock and Larsen say: a method covers what someone chose to model, and fourteen years of TLA+ in a company do not put a model under every component.

## Law against the test

Score law against the practitioner's three conditions. Design-level defects are expensive: a defect in a statute costs an amendment cycle, and section 6A took an Act of Parliament and then an appeal to confirm it changed nothing. Concurrency is the ordinary condition: a multi-party contract is a set of processes that step independently, with deadlines, no scheduler, and at least one participant reading the text the way the Bad Man reads it. A distributed system has race conditions between its nodes; a multi-party contract has race conditions between its parties; the two are the same bug class, and the Enactor that applied a stale plan is a cousin of the operator on day thirty-one in post 1. The third condition — whether the team can sustain the model as the system changes — is the open one, and for law it is the whole question, because statutes are amended, contracts are redlined, and a specification that was true of last year's draft is a specification of nothing. That is the maintenance cost Woodcock and Larsen say nobody has measured. Two of three and one open is a stronger position than "AWS does it, so lawyers should," and a weaker one than a pitch.

The neural tools are arriving at the same place from the other side. In January 2026 Yung-Shen Hsia, Fang Yu and Jie-Hong Roland Jiang had a large language model (LLM) read financial regulations and enforcement cases from Taiwan's Financial Supervisory Commission (FSC) and emit constraints for a satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) solver — 87 cases, 86.2 percent of the generated constraint code correct — with the solver deciding consistency.[^17] In June 2026 Armin Heydari at Harvard and Torben Leowald at Columbia proposed an architecture in which the model autoformalizes into a calculus extending Catala and a verification kernel checks it, under the slogan "LLM proposes, verifier disposes."[^17] Our version of that division of labor is the one the section 415 model already shows: the model proposes the readings and the suspicious hypothetical, the solver runs the search, and what sits between them is a typed encoding a lawyer can read beside the statute. The search runs over the encoding rather than the prose, which is why its silence means something within the encoding and nothing outside it.

What a reader can run today is the light end. The section 415 model type-checks and evaluates with the shipped `l4` binary, and the Z3 and Espresso scripts sit beside it in the repository under a reproduction manifest.[^3] `l4 verify` reads a decision rule as its propositional skeleton, reports what can never be true and what can never fire, and says in its own help text that its silence is not a consistency proof; `l4 state-graph` draws a rule set's obligations as a state machine.[^18] The model checker that would search that graph for the operator's double bind is a specification with rulings and no prover, and the specification's header says so.[^18] On the temporal and strategic rungs the white-hat Bad Man is a design.

The academic version of this argument is a working paper for the formal-methods facet of our series, aimed at ProLaLa, the workshop on programming languages and the law that meets alongside the Principles of Programming Languages (POPL) conference; it carries the full catalog of bug class against legal pathology against tool, and the cap-table case in detail.[^19] The next post in the series is planned to take up what the ladder implies for a contract under negotiation — that it ships with no tests — and it is not written.

Holmes insisted that the distinction he was drawing was a practical one: "a bad man has as much reason as a good one for wishing to avoid an encounter with the public force."[^2] The drafter who plays him in simulation, over every draft, before he turns up in person, is doing the good man's work with the bad man's method.
