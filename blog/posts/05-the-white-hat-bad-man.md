In 2016 six students sat Singapore's O-Level examinations with help. Accomplices at a tuition center had registered as private candidates; the questions were streamed out of the hall by video, and answers came back through hidden phones, wireless receivers and earpieces, until invigilators caught one of the students. The center's principal, Poh Yuan Nie, and a teacher, Poh Min Fiona, were convicted of abetting the cheating by conspiracy and lost their appeals in the High Court. Then they obtained leave to refer a question of law of public interest to the Court of Appeal, which answered it on 21 November 2022. They did not dispute the facts. They argued that what they had done was, as a matter of law, not cheating.[^1]

The argument ran through the definitions. Cheating under section 415 of the Penal Code requires deceiving someone. Explanation 1 to the section, one of the clarifying notes the Code prints under a section, adds that "a dishonest concealment of facts is a deception within the meaning of this section." Section 24 defines "dishonestly" as doing a thing with the intention of causing wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another, and the gain and loss there are of property. No property changed hands at the tuition center, so, the applicants said, no concealment there was "dishonest" in the defined sense, so there was no deception, so there was no cheating. Substitute the definition, simplify, walk free.[^1]

Oliver Wendell Holmes described the person making that argument in "The Path of the Law," which the Harvard Law Review printed in 1897. "If you want to know the law and nothing else, you must look at it as a bad man, who cares only for the material consequences which such knowledge enables him to predict, not as a good one, who finds his reasons for conduct, whether inside the law or outside of it, in the vaguer sanctions of conscience."[^2] A programmer reads him as a threat model: an adversary who takes the text as given and searches it for an input the drafter did not anticipate. Security researchers have a name for the person who runs that search and files a report instead of an exploit, a white hat, and the tool is the same in both hands.[^9] The applicants ran the search on section 415 and took what they found to court.

## What the court did, and what a machine can redo

Three of the four moves in the Court of Appeal's answer can be rerun by a solver once a person has written the readings down. A solver is a program that, handed a formula, either finds values that make it true or proves that none exist. The first move, enumeration, is the human one: paragraph 18 of the judgment lists four readings of "dishonest" in Explanation 1, from the applicants', on which only a concealment aimed at property counts, through the amicus's (other concealments count too) and the prosecution's (the ordinary meaning), to the court's, on which "dishonest" names the intention to deceive and nothing more. Second, the court built a counterexample, at paragraph 28: a candidate who submits a forged university degree to win an unpaid internship. No property moves; any reasonable layperson would say the employer was cheated; on the applicants' reading the forger walks. Third, it showed what their reading did to the section's width: with no property in play a concealment could never change the outcome, so the second limb of the offense would shrink to property damage and Explanation 1 would do no work there. The fourth move was to call the forger's acquittal "an absurd state of affairs," and that one is a judgment about what the section is for. The answer to the question was no, and the convictions stood.[^1]

The three redoable moves are, in a compiler's vocabulary, a case split, a failing test and dead-code elimination, and an essay in our repository writes the case down that way. Section 415 becomes eleven Boolean atoms, each a phrase of the section: "by deceiving any person," "dishonest concealment of facts," "intent to cause wrongful property gain or loss," and eight more. The four readings become a four-way enumerated type, and the section is evaluated under each.[^3] Three encodings of those eleven atoms sit beside the essay, all written by hand after the judgment was read: a model in L4, the language our group at Singapore Management University has been building for six years; a script for Z3, a solver; a script for Espresso, a circuit minimizer from Berkeley. Nothing lowers one to another. Nothing checks that they agree except that they do; that is the weakness.

This morning I ran all three; together they took a quarter of a second. What the applicants' reading does to the statute can be said before any tool is named. In every case with no property in it, on their reading, the concealment clause can never change the outcome: put a concealment in or take it out and the verdict is the same, so wherever property is absent Explanation 1 is dead text. Two of the programs prove that independently, and the third exhibits it. The L4 evaluator convicts the forger under three readings, acquits him under the applicants', and passes its assertions. Z3 proves two theorems: that the applicants' reading can never convict where the court's would acquit, so every disagreement between the two runs the other way; and that across every no-property fact pattern the concealment atom is irrelevant under their reading.[^3]

The script's last query asks the solver for a case the two readings classify differently, restricted to fact patterns with no property in them. The restriction is the modeler's, written into the query. The solver returned four atoms set true: concealment, inducement, harm, and harm to mind, "mind" being one of the four kinds of harm the section lists beside body, reputation and property. Paragraph 28: a forged degree, an unpaid internship. The script's own banner says where the class came from, "GD [28] was handed to us," and that label is right. The solver reproduced the court's hypothetical from an encoding built after the court had written it. That validates the encoding against a known answer. Whether the search would have found the forger first, it does not say.[^3]

Espresso takes a Boolean function and returns a near-minimal sum of cases that computes it; the script checks its answer against an exact minimizer, Quine–McCluskey, and the two agree.[^3] Fed the offense in the no-property world under the applicants' reading, it returns four cases, and concealment appears in none of them. Under the court's reading it returns eight, and concealment is in four. Law has a name for what the minimizer just did to the applicants: the presumption against surplusage, the canon that a legislature wastes no words, so a reading that leaves a clause idle is presumptively wrong. The canon and dead-code elimination are the same computation put to opposite uses. The minimizer deletes the clause. The court deletes the reading that killed it. One difference: the minimizer's deletion is a theorem, and the canon's is a presumption a court may rebut. Canons have been formal objects in AI and Law for a decade; the notes have the papers.[^3] The count, four cases under their reading and eight under the court's, is the part I have not seen done elsewhere.

The same division shows up one level down, in grammar. "For want of a comma, we have this case," the First Circuit opened in March 2017. Maine's overtime law exempts "canning, processing, preserving, freezing, drying, marketing, storing, packing for shipment or distribution of" agricultural produce, meat and fish products, and perishable foods. Maine's drafting manual tells drafters to leave out the comma before the last item of a series, and they had, so "packing for shipment or distribution" reads as one activity or as two. A dairy's delivery drivers distribute and do not pack: exempt from overtime on one parse, owed it on the other. The court held the clause ambiguous and then did what no parser does. It applied Maine's rule that an ambiguity in its wage laws is read in the workers' favor, and the drivers won.[^4] A parser counts derivations; the choice between them was a canon's.

What the solver cannot do is decide that letting the forger keep his internship offends the purpose of the section. The essay beside the model says as much: Boolean reasoning can show that two readings disagree and prove that one of them kills a clause; which reading the section was for is not a question it takes. Parliament's own patch shows the boundary from the other side. The Criminal Law Reform Act 2019, in force from 1 January 2020, gave section 24 a second limb, so that "dishonestly" now also covers an act "dishonest by the ordinary standards of reasonable and honest persons," and added a section 6A that applies the Code's definitions Code-wide while expressly leaving out "dishonestly" and "fraudulently." Of the first the court said the amendment "was made out of an abundance of caution and was not intended to change the law." Of the second, not yet in force in 2016, it said that it "clarifies the underlying legislative intent."[^1] A clarifying commit with no change in behavior, and, on the court's own account, words that do no work: belt and suspenders, which is ordinary drafting, and the reason the canon against surplusage is only a presumption.

The forged degree is the shape of every loophole. The letter of the section is the model; the spirit is the invariant the drafters meant it to keep, here that deceiving someone is cheating whether or not property moves; the loophole is a counterexample, a run on which the letter holds and the invariant fails. What the verification vocabulary adds to that two-layer picture, which AI and Law has had for three decades, is that the lower layer can be searched, and that the search has a cost somebody has measured.[^2]

## What it cost at Amazon, and what the record does not show

The people who have run this kind of search longest on systems with money in them are at Amazon Web Services (AWS), where engineers began writing specifications in TLA+, Leslie Lamport's language for a system's states and steps, in 2011.[^13] Chris Newcombe and five colleagues reported on it in 2014, and the figure in their report that is about a person rather than a system is two weeks. One author's first specification was of an algorithm with a known bug that "had passed unnoticed through multiple design reviews and code reviews, and had only surfaced after months of testing." He spent two weeks learning the language and writing the spec; the model checker found the bug in seconds; he added the team's reviewed fix, and the checker found the problem again by a different trace. Engineers from entry level to principal, the report says, learned TLA+ "from scratch and get useful results in 2 to 3 weeks," some on evenings and weekends without help. The line counts are in the note.[^11] Seven years later a team of twelve described ShardStore, a new S3 storage node of more than 40,000 lines of Rust, checked with reference models and property-based tests: "Our work has prevented 16 issues from reaching production." The harnesses were built by two formal-methods experts working full-time for nine months and a third who joined for three. Since then most of the work on them has been done by the ShardStore engineers, none with prior formal-methods experience, and by git blame "18% of the lines of code in the test harnesses were last edited by a non-formal-methods expert."[^12]

Jim Woodcock and Peter Gorm Larsen, formal-methods researchers at York and Aarhus, published a critical evaluation of that public record in Formal Aspects of Computing in June 2026. I read its seventy pages for the number that would say what such a search costs a team that is not Amazon, and it is not there; they say why. The case studies are success stories, and there is "little systematic data about neutral or unsuccessful adoption attempts." Nothing public compares formal methods with fuzzing, static analysis or code review on the same system, so no return on the investment can be established. Keeping a specification true as the system under it changes is acknowledged as costly, with "limited systematic evidence about long-term maintenance effort." Their test for a practitioner: formal methods are "most compelling when design-level defects are expensive, when systems are highly concurrent or fault-tolerant, and when teams can sustain the modelling and maintenance effort alongside conventional assurance."[^14]

Then there is the night of 19 October 2025. At 11:48 p.m. Pacific time, in AWS's Northern Virginia region, two copies of an automated component called the DNS Enactor, which rewrites the records that turn DynamoDB's service name into an address, raced: one, unusually delayed, applied a much older plan over a newer one, and the other's clean-up then deleted the older plan because it was many generations old. The regional endpoint stopped resolving, and the disruption ended at 2:20 the next afternoon; AWS's own summary calls it "the race condition."[^16] On 5 November Murat Demirbas posted a TLA+ model of the component; splitting its action into receive, apply and clean up "exposed the race condition clearly."[^16] Neither AWS's summary nor Demirbas's post says whether that automation was ever specified. A method covers what someone chose to model, and fourteen years of TLA+ in a company do not put a model under every component. Demirbas's model is a retrodiction, built after the postmortem said what the race was. So are the section 415 encodings.

## Law against the test

A multi-party contract is a set of processes that step independently, with deadlines, no scheduler, and at least one participant reading the text the way the Bad Man reads it. That is a distributed system's shape, and the search carries over: enumerate the interleavings, look for a state where two obligations collide. Post 1 of this series described a statutory obligation with that defect in it. Under one interleaving of the parties' permitted steps, an organization that had found a data breach was obliged by one clause to notify the people affected and, from the same reachable state, instructed not to. A model checker found it. A model checker is a program that walks every order in which the parties' steps could happen and reports the orders that reach a state you said must never be reached. The model it walked was built by hand, in the timed-automata checker from Uppsala and Aalborg (UPPAAL), from the fragment of the Act as we understood it; post 1 says how, and what the guidance beside the Act did with the case.[^5] The Enactor that applied a stale plan is that organization's cousin. Contract languages have been model-checked for conflicts since Pace, Prisacariu and Schneider in 2007; the search itself is not new.[^7]

What does not carry over is what happens next. A machine's race is real the moment it runs. A contract's is a hypothesis about the text until a lawyer has checked how the encoding treats provisos and priorities, and asked whether the doctrine already absorbs the interleaving: a later instruction overriding an earlier duty, waiver, estoppel. Law is defeasible. Sarah Lawsky argued in 2017 that at least some statutory reasoning yields conclusions later information can defeat, and post 1 says as much of its own finding: the checker alone would not have persuaded us the conflict was in the text; the guidance beside the Act did.[^10] So concurrency is law's ordinary condition, and a model's findings there arrive as hypotheses for a person to sort into defects and cases the doctrine already handles. That sorting is a cost the AWS numbers do not include.

Design-level defects are expensive in law. A defect in a statute costs an amendment cycle, and the 2020 amendment to section 24 took an Act of Parliament and then a criminal reference to establish that it changed nothing. The third of Woodcock and Larsen's conditions, whether the team can sustain the model as the system changes, is the open one, and for law it is the whole question. Statutes are amended, contracts are redlined, and a specification that was true of last year's draft is a specification of nothing. Nobody has measured that cost for AWS, they say, and nobody has measured it for us.

Their survivorship point applies to us before it applies to Amazon. Every result in this series is a hit. Post 1's three findings came from three searches, out of five engagements, and the exhibit above reproduced a decided case. Nothing counts the encodings that found nothing, or the reported conflicts that were artifacts of the encoding, a category post 1 concedes exists; the group that typed the British Nationality Act into Prolog in 1983 found fewer ambiguities than they expected, and that is probably the common case.[^8] Four case studies and no denominator is the shape of evidence I just criticized.[^15]

The neural tools, large language models (LLMs), are arriving at the same place from the other side, under a slogan Armin Heydari and Torben Leowald borrowed from mathematical AI in June 2026: "LLM proposes, verifier disposes."[^17] Our version of that division of labor is the section 415 model's: a person proposes the readings and the suspicious hypothetical, the solver runs the search, and between them sits a typed encoding whose atoms are the statute's own phrases. The hinge of the case, the ten lines that say when a concealment counts as a deception:[^3]

```l4
GIVEN s IS A `Cheating Scenario`
      i IS AN Interpretation
GIVETH A BOOLEAN
`dishonest concealment counts as a deception` s i MEANS
    CONSIDER i
    WHEN `Second Interpretation` THEN
            s's `dishonest concealment of facts`
        AND `the s 24 requirement is met` s
    OTHERWISE
        s's `dishonest concealment of facts`
```

Whether a lawyer can read that beside Explanation 1 is for a lawyer to say. Catala, from Inria and Microsoft Research, and Kowalski and Datoo's Logical English are the nearest neighbors on the encoding half, and post 1 describes the rules-as-code programs and policy-automation engines beside them.[^17] The search half, an adversary walking the encoding, runs over the encoding rather than the prose, so its silence means something within the encoding and nothing outside it.

What a reader can run today is the light end: the section 415 model and its two scripts, under a reproduction manifest.[^3] `l4 verify` reads a decision rule as its propositional skeleton, reports what can never be true and what can never fire, and says in its own help text that its silence is not a consistency proof. `l4 state-graph` draws a rule set's obligations as a state machine.[^18] The model checker that would search that graph for post 1's double bind is a specification with rulings and no prover, and its header says so.[^18] A lease has defects of a third kind: a defined term, "the Premises," that appears once as "the Property"; a cross-reference to a schedule the last redline deleted; a rent-review formula that adds a percentage to a sum of dollars. The first two are unbound names, which L4's type checker refuses today with "I could not find a definition for the identifier"; the third is a units error, and L4 has no notion of units: a percentage is typed as a number.[^6] Whether one party can force a bad outcome whatever the others do, the cap-table question, wants a game solver, and none is built. The white-hat Bad Man there is a design document.

The academic version of this argument is a working paper aimed at ProLaLa, the workshop on programming languages and the law that meets alongside the Principles of Programming Languages (POPL) conference. It carries the full catalog of bug class against tool, and the cap-table case.[^19] Two later posts are planned: one on what the compiler checks, which is where the lease belongs, and one on what all of this implies for a contract under negotiation, that it ships with no tests. Neither is written.

Holmes insisted that the distinction he was drawing was a practical one: "a bad man has as much reason as a good one for wishing to avoid an encounter with the public force."[^2] The drafter who plays him in simulation, over every draft, before he turns up in person, is doing the good man's work with the bad man's method.
