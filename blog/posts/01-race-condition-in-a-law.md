A notice is served on an operator: remediate within thirty days. A second clause says that while an assessment is pending, the operator must not remediate until it is complete. The assessment runs long. On day thirty-one the operator is in breach of the first clause by doing nothing and in breach of the second by doing anything, and every step that led there was permitted. The person standing on day thirty-one asks: which one do I break?

That fragment is from our own design notes, with the names filed off, and it is the shape of what we found, not the text.[^2] The text belongs to a government agency that, in one of L4's early pilots, asked us to turn one of its regulations into software. The instrument was secondary legislation: rules made under a power that a statute delegates, usually to a minister or a statutory body, the kind that decides whether a business has to file a particular thing by a particular day. We encoded it in L4, the language our group at Singapore Management University has been building for six years, and generated a web wizard from the encoding — a member of the public answers questions about their situation and gets back what they have to do, each answer citing the clause it came from.[^1] Interviews of that kind are a mature product; Oracle Policy Automation, which Oracle bought with an Australian company called RuleBurst in December 2008, has generated them for tax and immigration agencies for years, and docassemble does it free.[^5] The second pass was the new part. We asked the encoding the question a model checker asks — is there any reachable state in which the rules say two things that cannot both be done? — and there was one. Under an unusual interleaving of the parties' steps, each step permitted, one clause obliged an act that another prohibited.[^2]

A programmer calls that a race condition: two processes, each fine on its own, wrong in one ordering of their steps. A deontic logician — deontic logic being the branch von Wright founded in 1951 to deal in obligation, permission and prohibition, the MUST, MAY and SHANT of an L4 rule — has been picking at conflicts of this shape since Chisholm's contrary-to-duty paradox of 1963; our paper calls it a contradictory conflict.[^4] If "model checker" is a phrase you have met only in a job advertisement, James Somers's 2017 piece in The Atlantic on Leslie Lamport and TLA+ is the way in, and nothing below depends on it.[^6]

What ran that search is the one thing this account cannot name. The pilot record says the conflict was found automatically, the paper says so too, and the model-checking backend in the repository a reader can build today is designed, not built, so the search is not one you can rerun from the tree.[^2] And the guidance the agency publishes beside the regulation, it turned out, already covers the case. Someone had seen the corner before we did and written a way out of it. A reader working from the regulation alone does not have that note.[^3]

## Two more

The second finding was about money, and it is the one closest to anyone who has signed a policy without reading it. A major insurer — millions of customers; it stays unnamed — had us formalize one of its policies, which means writing each clause precisely enough that a machine can compute a payout from it. The payout formula was not that precise. It had two readings, so two customers with the same claim could be paid different amounts depending on who read the sentence. The paper puts the gap as material financial exposure; our estimate at the time ran to millions of dollars in claims on the more generous reading, and an ambiguity in a policy the insurer wrote is, by the usual rule of construction, read against the insurer. We reported it. What the insurer did with the sentence is not on record here.[^7]

The third was earlier, informal, and about tests. A set of New Zealand entitlement rules had been written in OpenFisca, an open-source engine for tax-and-benefit rules that began in 2011 inside a French government policy unit; the rules were Python, and they carried a handful of tests.[^8] We wrote a parser for the Python, pulled the rules into Haskell, and drove them from HSpec, a Haskell test framework, with generated scenarios: many claimants, each run through the rules, the outputs read for where the numbers bent the wrong way. A miscalculation showed up in the generated suite. Which scheme, how many scenarios, how the generator bounded the space and what the outputs were judged against are not on record here, and the parser is not in the repository. The claim is the small one: a handful of hand-written tests missed what a generated suite caught.[^9]

None of the three was a one-off contract. Each was a rule that runs many times — a regulation every regulated business must comply with, a formula applied to every claim, a calculator applied to every claimant — and that is where a defect in a rule stops being a lawyer's problem and becomes an engineering one. Three findings are also three anecdotes. We have five engagements on the record; the other two — a payments company's fee tables served through a query API, a drafting office's look at the language — were not searches for defects, so the denominator is three of three, which is no denominator at all. Woodcock, Larsen, Bicarregui and Fitzgerald said of their own 2009 survey of sixty-two industrial formal-methods projects that people who saw no value in the method would be "under-represented among the responses"; the same bias applies to us, and the Imperial group's experience below, fewer ambiguities than they expected, is probably the common case.[^10]

## Somebody has been here before

Layman Allen, in the Yale Law Journal in 1957: "A large amount of the litigation based on written instruments—whether statute, contract, will, conveyance or regulation—can be traced to the draftsman's failure to convey his meaning clearly."[^11] His remedy was symbolic logic, used as a drafting tool. Thirty-seven years later, with Charles Saxon, he narrowed the diagnosis: nearly all the ambiguity in legal writing is ambiguity in the language that expresses logical structure, and nearly all of it is inadvertent.[^12] The *and*, the *or* and the *unless* of a clause, rather than the meaning of its nouns.

In the summer of 1983 a group at Imperial College London typed a large part of the British Nationality Act 1981 into Prolog; in January 1984 they showed it to Home Office officials who had been involved in drafting the act; in May 1986 they published it in Communications of the ACM, the journal of the Association for Computing Machinery.[^13] Two findings carry forward. They expected more imprecision and ambiguity than they found — "fewer such examples than we originally expected" — and where it did exist "it was usually possible to identify the intended interpretation with little difficulty." The second finding is a bug, and it came from writing a rule down. Section 1(2) of the act says that a newborn found abandoned in the United Kingdom shall, unless the contrary is shown, be deemed to have been born there to a parent who was a British citizen or settled. Two negations sit in that clause: "unless the contrary is shown," and inside it "the contrary" — that the infant was *not* born to such a parent. Prolog's *not* means *cannot be proved*. Read both negations that way and the pair collapses into a requirement to prove that the parent qualifies, which an infant of unknown parents cannot meet, so the machine declines to make it a citizen — "exactly the opposite of what is intended by the act." The authors' fix was to read the inner *not* as ordinary negation. No tool reported that. Formalizing forced a choice the English never had to make, and the obvious choice went the wrong way. Their conclusion was Allen's: a logical representation "can also help to derive logical consequences of the rules and therefore test them before they are put into force." Their last sentence ran the other way: "the accumulated experience of managing complex systems of law may teach us something about the maintenance of large bodies of complex software."

The rebuttal arrived the same year. Philip Leith, in the Computer Journal, argued that the Imperial team had a mistaken picture of the legal process and of what logic programming could do inside it, and set out to dispute the power of logic programming as such.[^14] Law is not only its text — there is the guidance, the practice, the tribunal, the discretion. Leith was right about that, and the agency's guidance is the same point made by a drafter instead of a critic.

## Rules as code, then and now

The idea went into government under a new name. In 2018 a New Zealand team spent three weeks — legislative drafters, policy specialists, business analysts and software developers in one room — trying to code two acts, the Rates Rebate Act and the Holidays Act. They came out with a method they called Better Rules, a phrase for what the current process makes of lawyers once a law is enacted — "lawyers as modems," translating text into business rules — and a finding: "it is difficult to produce machine-consumable rules if the policy and legislation has not been developed with this outcome in mind."[^15] That year OpenFisca's product manager moved to New Zealand, and the rates rebate calculator became the first OpenFisca service in production outside Europe.[^8] The OECD picked the movement up in 2020, in a working paper by James Mohun and Alex Roberts, under the name Rules as Code. Their definition is a machine-consumable version of some rules "to exist alongside the existing natural language counterpart," and their sentence on limits is that the point "is not necessarily to eliminate this challenge or ambiguity, but instead to make it visible to both implementers and policy makers." Their example is a grant for children aged four and a half to eighteen, and the question, raised by a Jersey legislative drafter named Waddington, of what "half" means to a computer counting from the hour of birth.[^15]

In 2021 Denis Merigoux, Nicolas Chataing and Jonathan Protzenko, at Inria and Microsoft Research, published Catala, a language meant as a shared medium for lawyers and programmers, with the code following the legal text it implements; they tried it on French family benefits and reported that "in doing so, we uncover a bug in the official implementation."[^16] The movement has kept moving since — Dutch officials are taught a structured reading of legislation for implementation, Norway's digitalization agency keeps guidance on writing rules that automate, and the 2026 OpenFisca conference met in Canberra in March — and it has its critics.[^17] Mireille Hildebrandt, in 2020: "What code-driven law does is to fold enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke, collapsing the distance between legislator, executive and court." A New Zealand Law Foundation report the next year, from inside the Better Rules jurisdiction, put it as a headline — legislation should not be code — because enacting law as code "undermines the separation of powers and the role of the Judiciary."[^18] Both objections are to code that replaces the instrument. A checker that shows a drafter where the instrument contradicts itself, guidance and all, is a different thing, and it is the thing on offer here.

Then there is the failure case. Australia's Robodebt scheme ran from 2015 to 2019. It took a welfare recipient's annual income from the tax office, averaged it into fortnightly amounts, compared those to what the person had declared, and hundreds of thousands of people were told they owed money on the strength of the difference. The Federal Court, approving the class-action settlement in 2021, put the numbers this way: debts totaling at least $1.763 billion unlawfully asserted against about 433,000 people, about $751 million recovered from 381,000 of them, and $112 million paid on top in the settlement — which is why the figure reported for "the settlement" varies with which of those a writer adds up. The Royal Commission that reported in July 2023 — three volumes, 990 pages, 57 recommendations — found that income averaging used that way "was in fact inconsistent with social security legislation" and called the scheme "a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal."[^19] That is the bug class the rules-as-code programs mostly talk about: the implementation disagreeing with the law. The check was in fact made, by lawyers, before the scheme started — the social-services department was advised in November 2014 that the averaging "did not accord with legislation" — and the Commission found no evidence the advice reached the department running the system. The finding lived in a memo. The code ran for four years.

The double bind is a third class. Waddington's half a year is one clause with two readings. Robodebt and Catala's family benefits are code disagreeing with text. The pilot's finding is two clauses, each clear, each faithfully implemented, that cannot both be obeyed from a state the rules themselves let you reach. Reading harder does not find it; searching does — enumerate the states, look for one in which an obligation and a prohibition coincide. That search has a literature. Giovanni Sartor wrote on normative conflicts in legal reasoning in 1992; Pace, Prisacariu and Schneider model-checked a formal contract language for conflicts in 2007, and with Fenech built a contract analyzer (CLAN) that found obligation–prohibition conflicts automatically in 2009; Guido Governatori's Regorous checked business processes for compliance in 2013; Symboleo, a specification language for legal contracts with deontic operators, dates from 2020; and Harry Surden gave the law reviews "computable contracts" in 2012.[^20] What our pilot adds is narrow. The obligations ran as timed processes, so the conflict surfaced as an interleaving — a sequence of permitted steps with a late assessment in it — rather than as a clash in a static rule set, and the encoding it ran over is one a non-programmer can read beside the source text.

A conflict a checker reports is only as real as the encoding's treatment of exceptions and priorities. Law is defeasible: a proviso carves a case out of a general duty, a clause that opens "subject to section 12" yields to section 12, a regulation cannot override the act it is made under — and an encoding that forgets any of that reports conflicts that are artifacts of the encoding.[^21] The checker does not persuade me that this one is in the text rather than in our encoding. The guidance does: someone reading the instrument without us saw the same corner and wrote a way out of it. That check was made by people, and a machine does not make it alone.

## The tooling gap

Programmers have languages, compilers, type checkers, version control, test frameworks, model checkers, and a culture in which "we found a race condition" is a normal thing to say on a Tuesday. Graphic designers have Adobe: Illustrator, InDesign, Photoshop, a tool for every niche in the craft. Lawyers have Microsoft Word — and, in fairness to forty years of legal software, HotDocs, which has assembled documents since 1993, the policy-automation engines that generate eligibility interviews, and docassemble, which has done the same free since 2015 and which L4 itself compiles to.[^5] Those are generators and determination engines. None of them tells you the instrument contradicts itself. The asymmetry is odd because the work is not that different: a corporate lawyer and a programmer both take a tangle of business goals and turn it into a textual artifact that has to anticipate the behavior of a complicated system with many moving parts and at least one adversarial participant.[^22] I spent a year at Harvard's Berkman Center and a year at Stanford's CodeX asking why the lawyer's stack stops at the word processor. Stanford's own index of the industry, the CodeX TechIndex, lists 5,761 legal technology companies today, 389 of them in contract management and 898 in document management and automation.[^23] Those are categories for storing documents, moving documents and generating documents. Contract-review tools built on large language models will flag a clause as possibly inconsistent with another; a flag is a guess a reader has to check, where a search that enumerates the states and returns the one that breaks can say that nothing was missed, within the encoding it was given. The index has no category for the second kind of tool.

The binary a reader builds from the repository today cannot ask the pilot's question, and says so. `l4 verify` reads each decision rule as its logical skeleton — the ands, ors and nots, with every leaf an opaque atom — and reports rules that cannot be satisfied, branches that cannot be reached and guards that do nothing. It sees no numbers and no dates; its findings are sound with respect to the encoding it is given, which says nothing about whether the encoding is faithful to the law; and its silence is not a consistency proof. `l4 state-graph` draws the state machine of a set of obligations. The timed model checking that would ask the pilot's question over that graph is designed, not built.[^24] If you run a small company and signed a lease this year without reading it, there is nothing here for you yet. The checker is for people who write rules that run many times, and the part that found the double bind is not in the shipped binary.

## The guidance, and the offer

The double bind is documented in the guidance that accompanies the regulation, and in practice the guidance handles it. So what our method found from the text alone, someone had already found. But a reader without the guidance is caught, and the fix sits in a document that does not amend the instrument, so the instrument still carries the contradiction. Found at drafting time, the same finding could have gone into the regulation itself. The offer is to the drafters as much as to the reader, and one drafting office has already looked at L4 for future legislation with that in mind.[^3]

The academic version of this argument is a paper in draft for ICAIL, the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Law: constitutive rules as typed total functions, regulative rules as timed concurrent processes, and the double bind as a reachability query over the product of the parties' processes.[^25] A later post in this series is planned to run the ladder from the serial comma up through model checking; it is not written.

In January 1984 the Imperial group showed their Prolog to Home Office officials who had helped draft the act, and the officials made the observation the authors matched to Allen's of 1957: this could help with drafting the rules in the first place. Forty-two years later that is still the offer.
