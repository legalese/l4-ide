An organization finds that it has been breached, and the breach is assessed as the kind the regulation calls notifiable. From that point the rule is plain: notify the people whose data was exposed, and notify the regulator, as soon as possible. The organization starts doing both. Some time later the regulator comes back with advice: do not tell the affected parties. The organization is now obliged by one clause to notify its users and instructed, under the same rules, not to. It got there without breaking anything. Whoever is holding the phone has to choose which one to break.

That is the shape of one regulation, with the names filed off, as I recall it; the instrument stays unnamed.[^2] Why a regulator would say such a thing, the text does not explain. I can guess — a state actor behind the breach, some need for disaster management — but that is a guess from general knowledge, and nothing here turns on it.

The regulation belongs to a government agency that, in one of L4's early pilots, asked us to turn it into software. It was secondary legislation — rules made under a power that a statute delegates, usually to a minister or a statutory body, the kind that tells a business what it must report, to whom, and how soon — and we encoded it in an early version of L4. Our group at Singapore Management University has been building the language for six years. From the encoding we generated a web wizard: a member of the public answers questions about their situation and gets back what they have to do, each answer citing the clause it came from.[^1] Interviews of that kind are a mature product. Oracle Policy Automation, which Oracle bought with an Australian company called RuleBurst in December 2008, has generated them for tax and immigration agencies for years, and docassemble does it free.[^5]

The second pass was the new part. Once we understood the fragment — the two duties to notify, the regulator's later advice, the clock running under "as soon as possible" — we built a model of it by hand in the timed-automata model checker from Uppsala and Aalborg (UPPAAL), a tool that takes processes with clocks and searches their joint behavior for a state you name. The state we named was one in which the rules say two things that cannot both be done. It found one. Under an unusual interleaving of the parties' steps, each step permitted, one clause obliged an act that another prohibited.[^2] [NEEDS MENG: the year, and what UPPAAL put on the screen when it found the state — a trace, a diagram — if it can be shown.]

A programmer would call that a race condition. A deontic logician has been picking at conflicts of this shape since Chisholm's contrary-to-duty paradox of 1963 — deontic logic being the branch von Wright founded in 1951 to deal in obligation, permission and prohibition, the MUST, MAY and SHANT of an L4 rule — and our paper calls it a contradictory conflict.[^4] If "model checker" is a phrase you have met only in a job advertisement, James Somers's 2017 piece in The Atlantic on Leslie Lamport and TLA+ is the way in, and nothing below depends on it.[^6]

No compiler emitted that model. We built it by hand from the fragment as we understood it, so the fidelity between regulation and model was a person's, checked by a person, and what the tool found is what its builder put in. Nor can a reader rerun the search from the repository. The fragment can be written in today's L4, but the lowering from L4 to a timed model checker, which would let the search run from the encoding itself, is a written proposal with nothing built behind it.[^2] And the guidance the agency publishes beside the regulation, it turned out, already covers the case. Someone had seen the corner before we did and written a way out of it. A reader working from the regulation alone does not have that note.[^3]

## Two more

The second finding was about money, and it is the one closest to anyone who has signed a policy without reading it. A major insurer — millions of customers; it stays unnamed — had us formalize one of its policies, which means writing each clause precisely enough that a machine can compute a payout from it. The payout formula was not that precise. It had two readings, so two customers with the same claim could be paid different amounts depending on who read the sentence. The paper calls the gap material financial exposure; my own estimate at the time, from memory, ran to millions of dollars in claims on the more generous reading. And an ambiguity in a policy the insurer wrote is, by the usual rule of construction, read against the insurer. Singapore's Court of Appeal has said that rule "is particularly pertinent in insurance policies because these policies are invariably drafted and/or vetted by experts for the benefit of insurers so as to protect the latter's interest."[^7]

We reported it. The insurer reviewed what it had been doing and found it had been making the larger payout. That made the write-up delicate: the adjusters who had been helping us through the policy were the people who could be penalized for overpaying, so the finding was presented as what it was, a defect in the wording. When it reached the executives, the conversation was about liability. Nobody said whether future claims would be paid on the stricter reading or the generous one. What one of them did name was a 2023 judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union, *Ocidental v LP*, as the liability the ambiguity might have exposed them to, and clarifying the term as what would save them from it. That case is about a limiting term in a group policy that the insured was never given to read, and the transparency a consumer contract owes; it sits beside the point rather than on it. The remark is reported here as the executives' reading of their own risk. The fix went into the wording of the term, where the defect was, and not into the code that computed the payout.[^8]

The third was earlier, informal, and about tests; the record of it is my recollection and one paragraph in a working paper. A set of New Zealand entitlement rules had been written in OpenFisca, an open-source engine for tax-and-benefit rules that began in 2011 inside a French government policy unit; the rules were Python, and they carried a handful of tests.[^9] We wrote a parser for the Python, pulled the rules into Haskell, and drove them from HSpec with generated scenarios: many claimants, each run through the rules, the outputs read for where the numbers bent the wrong way. A miscalculation showed up in the generated suite. [NEEDS MENG: which scheme; what generated the cases and over what bound; what the outputs were judged against.] The parser is not in the repository. A handful of hand-written tests had missed what a generated suite caught, and that is all the episode shows.[^10]

None of the three was a one-off contract. Each was a rule that runs many times: a regulation every regulated business must comply with, a formula applied to every claim, a calculator applied to every claimant. Three findings are also three anecdotes. We have five engagements on the record; the other two — a payments company's fee tables served through a query API, a drafting office's look at the language — were not searches for defects, so the denominator is three of three, which is no denominator at all. Woodcock, Larsen, Bicarregui and Fitzgerald, in a 2009 survey of sixty-two industrial formal-methods projects, warned that people who saw no value in the method would be "under-represented among the responses." A formalization that finds nothing tends not to get written up either — our impression; they did not measure it. The Imperial group's experience below, fewer ambiguities than they expected, is probably the common case.[^11]

## Somebody has been here before

Layman Allen, in the Yale Law Journal in 1957: "A large amount of the litigation based on written instruments—whether statute, contract, will, conveyance or regulation—can be traced to the draftsman's failure to convey his meaning clearly."[^12] His remedy was symbolic logic, used as a drafting tool. Thirty-seven years later, with Charles Saxon, he narrowed the diagnosis: nearly all the ambiguity in legal writing is ambiguity in the language that expresses logical structure, and nearly all of it is inadvertent.[^13] The *and*, the *or* and the *unless* of a clause are where it goes wrong.

In the summer of 1983 a group at Imperial College London typed a large part of the British Nationality Act 1981 into Prolog; in January 1984 they showed it to Home Office officials who had been involved in drafting the act; in May 1986 they published it in Communications of the ACM.[^14] They expected more imprecision and ambiguity than they found — "fewer such examples than we originally expected" — and where it did exist "it was usually possible to identify the intended interpretation with little difficulty." What they did find was a bug, and it came from writing a rule down. Section 1(2) of the act says that a newborn found abandoned in the United Kingdom shall, unless the contrary is shown, be deemed to have been born there to a parent who was a British citizen or settled. Two negations sit in that clause: "unless the contrary is shown," and inside it "the contrary" — that the infant was *not* born to such a parent. Prolog's *not* means *cannot be proved*. Read both negations that way and the pair collapses into a requirement to prove that the parent qualifies, which an infant of unknown parents cannot meet, so the machine declines to make it a citizen — "exactly the opposite of what is intended by the act." The authors' fix was to read the inner *not* as ordinary negation. No tool reported that. Formalizing forced a choice the English never had to make, and the obvious choice went the wrong way.

Their conclusion was Allen's: a logical representation "can also help to derive logical consequences of the rules and therefore test them before they are put into force." Philip Leith answered them in the Computer Journal the same year: the team had a mistaken picture of the legal process, and law is not only its text — there is the guidance, the practice, the tribunal, the discretion.[^15] The agency's guidance is Leith's point, made by a drafter instead of a critic.

The idea went into government anyway, under the name rules as code: a New Zealand team in 2018, three weeks in one room coding two acts, came out with the phrase "lawyers as modems" for what the current process makes of the profession once a law is enacted; the OECD, the intergovernmental body of mostly rich countries, wrote the movement up in 2020; Catala, from Inria and Microsoft Research in 2021, tried its language on French family benefits and found "a bug in the official implementation."[^16] The work has continued since, in universities and in governments, and the newer of it is in the notes.[^17] The movement has its critics, whose objection is to code that replaces the instrument.[^18] A checker that shows a drafter where the instrument contradicts itself, guidance and all, is a different thing, and it is the thing on offer here.

## Three kinds of bug

Then there is the failure case. Australia's Robodebt scheme ran from 2015 to 2019. It took a welfare recipient's annual income from the tax office, averaged it into fortnightly amounts, compared those to what the person had declared, and hundreds of thousands of people were told they owed money on the strength of the difference. The Federal Court, approving the class-action settlement in 2021, put the numbers this way: debts totaling at least $1.763 billion unlawfully asserted against about 433,000 people; about $751 million recovered from 381,000 of them; and $112 million paid on top in the settlement. The figure reported for "the settlement" varies with which of those a writer adds up. The Royal Commission that reported in July 2023 — three volumes, 990 pages, 57 recommendations — found that income averaging used that way "was in fact inconsistent with social security legislation" and called the scheme "a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal."[^19] That is the bug the rules-as-code programs mostly talk about: the implementation disagreeing with the law. The check was in fact made, by lawyers, before the scheme started — the social-services department was advised in November 2014 that the averaging "did not accord with legislation" — and the Commission found no evidence the advice reached the department running the system. The finding lived in a memo. The code ran for four years.

A Jersey legislative drafter named Waddington asked, in the OECD's paper, what a grant for children "aged four and a half to eighteen" means to a computer counting from the hour of birth: that is one clause with two readings.[^16] Robodebt and Catala's family benefits are code disagreeing with text. The breach regulation's bug is neither of those. The duty to notify and the advice not to are two clauses, each clear, each faithfully carried out, that cannot both be obeyed from a state the rules themselves let you reach. You find it by enumerating the states and looking for one in which an obligation and a prohibition coincide. That search has a literature going back to Giovanni Sartor's 1992 paper on normative conflicts, and it has been automated before: Fenech, Pace and Schneider's contract analyzer (CLAN) found obligation–prohibition conflicts in formal contracts in 2009, and Guido Governatori's Regorous checked business processes for compliance in 2013.[^20] What the pilot adds is that the duties ran against a clock, so the conflict arrived as a timing — a sequence of permitted steps with a late piece of advice in it — rather than as a clash in a static rule set.

A conflict a checker reports is only as real as the encoding's treatment of exceptions and priorities. Law is defeasible: a proviso carves a case out of a general duty, a clause that opens "subject to section 12" yields to section 12, a regulation cannot override the act it is made under — and an encoding that forgets any of that reports conflicts that are artifacts of the encoding.[^21] The obvious priority here is that the regulator's later word overrides the earlier duty; read that way, the organization stops notifying the moment the advice arrives, and a model that carries the priority goes quiet. But by then the organization has told the regulator, and perhaps some of its users, that a breach was found. Whatever the silence was meant to protect is already partly gone; the conflict the checker reports disappears and the thing the rules were for is still unmet. The checker alone would not persuade me that the conflict is in the text. The guidance does: someone reading the instrument without us saw the same corner and wrote a way out of it. That check was made by people, and a machine does not make it alone.

## The tooling gap

Programmers have languages, compilers, type checkers, version control, test frameworks, model checkers, and a culture in which "we found a race condition" is a normal thing to say on a Tuesday. Graphic designers have Adobe: Illustrator, InDesign, Photoshop, a tool for every niche in the craft. Lawyers have Microsoft Word — and, in fairness to thirty-odd years of legal software, HotDocs, which has assembled documents since 1993; the policy-automation engines that generate eligibility interviews; and docassemble, which has done the same free since 2015 and which L4 itself compiles to.[^5] Those are generators and determination engines. None of them tells you the instrument contradicts itself.

The asymmetry is odd because the work is not that different. A corporate lawyer and a programmer both take a tangle of business goals and turn it into a textual artifact that has to anticipate the behavior of a complicated system with many moving parts and at least one adversarial participant.[^22] Stanford's index of the industry, the CodeX TechIndex, lists 5,761 legal technology companies today, 389 of them in contract management and 898 in document management and automation.[^23] Those are categories for storing documents, moving documents and generating documents. Contract-review tools built on large language models will flag a clause as possibly inconsistent with another. A flag is a guess a reader has to check; a search that enumerates the states and returns the one that breaks can say that nothing was missed, within the model it was given. The index has no category for the second kind of tool.

The binary a reader builds from the repository today cannot ask the pilot's question, and says so. `l4 verify` reads each decision rule as its logical skeleton — the ands, ors and nots, with every leaf an opaque atom — and reports rules that cannot be satisfied, branches that cannot be reached and guards that do nothing. It sees no numbers and no dates; its findings are sound with respect to the encoding it is given, which says nothing about whether the encoding is faithful to the law; and its silence is not a consistency proof. `l4 state-graph` draws the state machine of a set of obligations. The timed model checking that would ask the pilot's question over that graph is designed, not built.[^24]

If you run a small company and signed a lease this year without reading it, there is nothing here for you yet. The checker is for people who write rules that run many times, and the part that found the double bind was a model built by hand. The argument in academic dress is a paper in draft for ICAIL, the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Law: constitutive rules as typed total functions, regulative rules as timed concurrent processes, and the double bind as a reachability query over the product of the parties' processes. A later post in this series, planned and not yet written, will take the ladder from the serial comma up through model checking.[^25]

## The guidance, and the offer

The double bind is documented in the guidance that accompanies the regulation, and in practice the guidance handles it. So what our method found from the text alone, someone had already found. But a reader without the guidance is caught, and the fix sits in a document that does not amend the instrument, so the instrument still carries the contradiction. Found at drafting time, the same finding could have gone into the regulation itself. The offer is to the drafters as much as to the reader, and one drafting office has already looked at L4 for future legislation with that in mind.[^3]

In January 1984 the Imperial group showed their Prolog to Home Office officials who had helped draft the act. The officials made the observation the authors matched to Allen's of 1957: this could help with drafting the rules in the first place. Forty-two years later that is still the offer: find the corner before anyone is holding the phone, and write the way out into the regulation rather than into the note beside it.
