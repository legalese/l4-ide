Some time before May 2024 a law clerk in the Eleventh Circuit typed a question into ChatGPT because his judge had wondered aloud what it would say: "What is the ordinary meaning of 'landscaping'?"[^1]

The case behind the question was small. James Snell ran a landscaping business in Fairhope, Alabama, under the name Outdoor Expressions. A family hired him for a job that ran to tree pruning, shrubs, sod and a sprinkler system, and one thing more: a trampoline, sunk into the lawn rather than standing on it, with a retaining wall and a decorative wooden cap around the rim, because an in-ground trampoline looks better and stands firmer than one on legs. A child fell off it and struck her face on the board. Her family sued Snell. His insurer refused to defend him. An endorsement to the policy described the covered work in three words, "Insured performs landscaping," and a trampoline, the insurer said, is not landscaping. The district court, reading the parties' dictionaries, agreed, and on appeal both sides spent their briefs on the one word.[^1]

Judge Kevin Newsom joined the opinion and wrote separately to say what he had done. Photographs of the finished job had moved him; he could not say why; and deciding on a gut feeling "has always given me the willies." So, "on what can only be described as a lark," he had a clerk ask the model. The answer: "'Landscaping' refers to the process of altering the visible features of an area of land, typically a yard, garden or outdoor space, for aesthetic or practical purposes." Asked whether an in-ground trampoline was landscaping, it said yes. The concurrence ends with a proposal that judges who take ordinary meaning as their rule "consider—consider—whether and how" large language models "might—might—inform the interpretive analysis."[^1]

Formalization — writing a rule down precisely enough that a machine can run it — has an answer to Newsom's question, and the answer is a shrug. Encode Snell's policy and the clause that matters computes coverage from a handful of facts. The insurer owed Snell two duties with two tests: whether it had to defend him turned on what the family's complaint alleged; whether it would have to pay turned on what actually happened. Both pass through one fact, whether the work was landscaping. The encoding names that fact, types it true-or-false, and computes everything downstream. What it does not do, and cannot, is say whether a trampoline sunk into a lawn is landscaping. Someone outside the encoding supplies the value.[^1]

## The word left open, 1983 and 2026

In the summer of 1983 a group at Imperial College London translated a large part of the British Nationality Act 1981 into Prolog — a language in which you write down rules and let the machine chase their consequences — and their 1986 paper in Communications of the ACM lists what they expected to trip over: "such vague phrases as 'being a good character,' 'having reasonable excuse,' and 'having sufficient knowledge of English.'" They did not try to define them. "The simplest way to handle vagueness is to assume that the vague concepts always apply and to use this assumption to generate qualified answers. For example, Peter is a citizen, if he is of good character."[^2] The machine computed everything it could and handed the rest back as a condition.

Forty-three years later our own encoding of section 1 of the same Act does the same thing, and writes down that it is doing it. At twelve places the encoding faced a choice the text did not settle — eleven in the words of section 1, and one about where the Act's good-character restriction belongs — and each is recorded as a fork: the site, the readings, the one taken, and why.[^3] Two sit on the foundling provision, which deems a newborn found abandoned in the United Kingdom to have been born there, after the Act's commencement, to a parent then a British citizen or settled, "unless the contrary is shown." Here is the first, from the file, unchanged:[^3]

```
-- AMBIGUITY: "new-born infant" is undefined by the Act. Reading (i): an
-- infant abandoned within days or weeks of birth, as assessed by the
-- decision-maker (taken here — the boolean input field asserts
-- new-born-ness as a found fact, leaving the vague boundary to the
-- fact-supplier). Reading (ii): any abandoned child of apparently
-- qualifying age (rejected: it reads "new-born" out of the text).
```

The input field it refers to is declared higher up the same file, one line in a record of facts about a person:

```
    `found abandoned as a new-born infant in the United Kingdom`                                           IS A BOOLEAN
```

"Leaving the vague boundary to the fact-supplier" is the Imperial group's "if he is of good character," typed out as a field. The others have the same shape. Does a birth on 1 January 1983, the day the Act commenced, count as "after commencement"? Taken: yes; commencement is an instant. Good character itself, in the current Act, qualifies the grant of citizenship rather than the entitlement, so the encoding keeps it in a separate gate.[^3] What the machine does with these words has not changed since 1983. What has changed is where the choice lives: at the site, with the losing reading beside it, so that a reviewer can disagree.

Lawyers had a name for the thing before either group met it. H. L. A. Hart called it the "open texture" of rules: a rule forbidding vehicles in the park is plain for "motor-cars, buses, and motor-cycles" and stops being plain for an electrically propelled toy car. When such a case arrives, Hart said, we choose between the competing interests, and the choice settles what the word means for that rule.[^4] Lon Fuller answered with a truck: patriots mount a working military truck on a pedestal in the park as a war memorial, and whether the rule reaches it cannot be known from the words without knowing what the rule is for.[^5]

Vehicle, landscaping, new-born, good character: an encoding takes each as a value it did not compute. One tradition in artificial intelligence and law tried to compute with the word itself, arguing a new case from decided ones and hypotheticals, and forty years on it has produced better-structured argument and a place for a person to stand.[^6] Someone still says which factors the case presents, and that someone is doing Hart's choosing and Fuller's reaching past the word to the purpose.

## Four kinds of case, and what the machine does in each

In November 2022 Singapore's Court of Appeal wrote down four readings of one word. The case, *Poh Yuan Nie*, was about exam cheating. The applicants argued that the Penal Code's definition of "dishonestly" governed the undefined word "dishonest" too, and so imported a property requirement into a cheating charge with no property in it. The court followed their reading to its consequence — a concealment offense could never be made out without a transfer of property — called that "an absurd state of affairs," and produced a man who wins an unpaid internship with a forged degree to show it. It measured the damage to the section, and rejected another reading because it would leave a clause with nothing to do.[^8] Once a person had written the four readings down, three of those moves could be redone in a fraction of a second, and were: by an encoding in our repository, by a solver, which searches for facts that satisfy a formula, and by a minimizer, which shrinks a Boolean function. "Absurd" is a judgment about what the section is for. The solver applies whatever value ordering it is handed. Supplying one was the court's work.[^8]

A design note in our repository, drafted in June 2026 for a paper not yet written, sorts decisions like that one into four kinds by how much of the decision an encoding reproduces. Deductive: the outcome follows from the rules and the found facts. Resolved: exceptions and priorities were needed, but they are stated or derivable. Forked: the rules admit more than one reading with different outcomes, and the court closed the fork — "detect-but-don't-resolve" in the note's words. Discretionary: no rule entails the outcome. *Poh Yuan Nie* sits on the line between resolved and forked. The scheme is proposed, not found.[^7]

| stratum | what the court did | what the machine does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **deductive** | the outcome follows from the rules and the found facts | reproduces it |
| **resolved** | exceptions and priorities were needed, but they are stated or derivable | reproduces it, once the priorities are encoded |
| **forked** | the rules admit more than one reading with different outcomes; the court closed the fork by a value choice, a rule of construction, or a patch | finds the fork and the case where it bites; cannot close it |
| **discretionary** | no rule entails the outcome: proportionality, a standard, a balance struck | nothing |

At a fork the labor divides: the encoder records it, a solver can show where the readings part and produce the fact pattern where it bites, and a person chooses. Our own pilots went the same way.

In 2022 we suspected a double bind in the breach-notification duty of Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act. Once a breach is assessed as notifiable, tell the people whose data was exposed, and tell the regulator, as soon as possible. The regulator may later direct you not to tell the people. We named the state we were afraid of — the individual told, after the regulator had forbidden telling them — and built a model of the rules by hand in UPPAAL, a model checker that searches processes with clocks in them for a state you name. It found a run and printed it: the organization notifies the regulator; the regulator prohibits; the organization, which in the model cannot know when a prohibition might arrive, has already told the individual. A second query found the other jaw: past the three-day deadline for notifying the regulator, no move remains. The one path that appears to satisfy both duties — notify the regulator, then stall on the individuals until a direction arrives — does so by breaching the immediacy requirement in the interim. A static read of the text misses that. A model with clocks does not.[^9] Nobody in our group resolved the bind, and nobody asked us to. The model's job was to show that the two duties could collide, and where.

In 2023, encoding an insurer's policy, one of us hit two readings of the payout formula. The insurer had been paying the larger one. That made the finding awkward to deliver: the adjusters who had walked us through the policy could be penalized for overpaying if it landed as "you have been paying too much." As I recall it — it was our presentation, and the slides are lost — we put the case to management as a class of risk, with two court decisions, one from Singapore and one from the European Court of Justice, in which an unclear term in a policy had come back on the insurer that wrote it. Management's reply was about that liability. Nobody said which reading future claims would be paid on. The fix went into the wording of the term, at the level of the logic and not the payout algorithm.[^9]

The reviewing guide in our documentation makes that ending a rule. "Real law under-determines," it says. "When our encoders meet an ambiguity they are instructed *not* to guess silently … Instead they record a fork: the site, the plausible readings, which was taken provisionally, and why." The encoder, in our pipeline, is a model; the reviewer is a person. Whether an encoding says what the law says is a judgment only a named human expert may make — a gate the pipeline calls HG1, human gate one — and the reviewer signs the exact files they read, so that any later edit breaks the signature.[^10] The stage that checks a register of forks says in its header what it cannot do — "What this stage cannot do is find a fork" — and that no procedure establishes that every ambiguity was found.[^10]

Louis Kaplow, in 1992, defined the difference between a rule and a standard as timing: a rule says fifty-five miles an hour; a standard says an excessive speed, and leaves the number to the adjudicator, later.[^11] An encoding of "excessive speed" is an input named *the speed was excessive*, with its content supplied exactly as late as the law left it. What the encoding adds is a map of where the specification ends and who is standing there when it does — what a concept note in our documentation calls converting surprise discretion into designed discretion.[^12]

## The strongest form of the objection

There is a school that would say the register is the statute. Critical Legal Studies, the movement of the 1970s American law schools, is remembered for an indeterminacy thesis; at its strongest — stronger than most of its members put it — it says that legal materials never determine outcomes: for any case the doctrine can be made to yield either result, and what decides is politics.[^13] Push the thesis to its limit and every case is discretionary. The deductive and resolved strata are empty; the forked stratum is a story the court tells about a choice it had already made; and an encoding that reports twelve forks in a section has miscounted, because every input in it is a fork. Snell's policy, on that reading, is open all the way down.

Taken at that strength the thesis would overturn the argument here. The argument does not need it to be false everywhere; it needs the weaker thing the forked stratum asserts, that in a given case the materials rule out some results and leave more than one standing.[^14] The design note's answer is to measure. Encode a corpus of appellate judgments, run each encoding on its found facts, and count how many dispositions fall in each stratum. If the first two bins are empty, the strong thesis was right, and what we call a register of what the text left open was only ever a record of what we chose. If they are not, the frontier has a location. The note warns against its own instrument: appellate judgments are selected for difficulty, so the corpus will overstate the last two strata, and a judgment in which no skeleton of rules and facts was found is weak evidence that none exists.[^7]

Mireille Hildebrandt's objection to rules as code comes from a different direction. Code-driven law, she wrote in 2020, "fold[s] enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke, collapsing the distance between legislator, executive and court," and what text affords that code does not is contestability, which she takes to be a core tenet of the rule of law.[^15] The fork register is a refusal to fold; the reading taken sits beside the reading rejected, and a superseded reading is "demoted, never deleted."[^10] But the refusal holds only as long as somebody reads the register. An encoding deployed as a service applies the reading it was given, to every query, with the losing reading in a comment nobody sees. So her point survives our tooling: the register makes the interpretation contestable to the reviewer, and the person on the receiving end of an answer can contest it only if the answer carries the fork with it.

## What the neural tools changed

Newsom did it again that September. In *United States v. Deleon*, on whether a robber who pointed a gun at a cashier had "physically restrained" him under the Sentencing Guidelines, he queried three models ten times apiece in fresh chats — "Not perfectly scientific, I know, but better than a rifle shot" — and reported that the answers "all tended to emphasize" physical force, physical means or physical barriers, though Gemini did not always use the words.[^16] His Snell concurrence had preferred the models to surveys and to corpus linguistics.[^1]

The pushback was quick. James Grimmelmann, Benjamin Sobel and David Stein put Newsom's two questions back to ChatGPT-3.5 and got the opposite answer. The model's verdict on the trampoline depended on the wording it had happened to pick for its definition a moment earlier; a list of examples ending in "other features" made it three times likelier to count the trampoline in. That reduced the question "to a roll of the dice."[^18] The larger measurements agree in shape — one of them 42,500 queries against a survey of 2,835 people,[^17] another finding that reformatting the question changed the answer.[^19] So the open-textured word has a new claimant, one that supplies the value fluently and not the same way twice.

What we ask a model for is the reverse: in the pipeline the encoder is told to record a fork rather than guess silently, and the register is checked for consistency before a human signs it.[^10] Where the model has collapsed a fork into one reading anyway, the register is where the collapse becomes visible and reversible — when it is read.

## Where the encoding is wrong

Jim Woodcock and Peter Gorm Larsen, reviewing Amazon's decade of formal methods this June, reach for George Box — "All models are wrong, but some are useful" — and cite Box's 1976 paper for it, whose own sentence is sharper: "Since all models are wrong the scientist must be alert to what is importantly wrong."[^20] An encoding of a statute is wrong wherever the statute's words were doing work its inputs do not carry — the words whose values the encoding takes from outside — and the register is the list of where one encoder thought so. The same review says of its own evidence what is true of ours: the case studies that reach print are the ones that worked.[^20]

Three things would overturn this argument, and none of them is settled. If a corpus measurement put nearly all adjudication in the discretionary stratum, the machine would be finding forks in a text that is all fork, and the strong thesis would have been right. If the forks our encoders, the models, record turned out, on review, to be mostly artifacts of the encoding rather than of the text, then the register would be measuring us, and nothing yet counts those. And if the choice at the fork were being made silently by the model that wrote the encoding, and the reviewer signed without reading, the gate would be a signature over a guess. The instrument for the first is a paper, planned and not yet drafted, for a journal issue on computational legal studies that closes at the end of June 2027: encode each judgment in a Singapore appellate corpus, run it, and count.[^7] The instrument for the second is the register itself, once there are enough of them to sum. The third has no instrument but a reviewer.

Snell's trampoline is in the discretionary stratum, or the forked one; the encoding does not know which. It knows that coverage turns on the word, that the word is an input, and that somebody had better be standing there. For Snell that somebody was his insurer, and an encoding of his policy would have told him so before the claim: one undefined word, and the other side filling it in.

The court never filled it in. Alabama treats an insurance application as part of the policy it precedes, and on his application Snell had answered "No" to whether he did "any recreational or playground equipment construction or erection." A trampoline is recreational equipment; nobody disputed that. So the court took "landscaping" as ambiguous, read it in Snell's favor, and held that he had disclaimed the work anyway.[^1]

The word the briefs were about did not decide the case, and that is the same point one level up: an encoding shows you where somebody else gets to fill in a blank. Snell had two. The one that cost him was on a form he had signed before the policy existed, and an encoding that folded the application into the policy, as Alabama law does, could have shown him both. The judge with a gut feeling he distrusted, a stack of dictionaries and a clerk with a browser open got his answer from the form.[^1]
