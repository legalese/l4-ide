In May 2024 a law clerk in the Eleventh Circuit typed a question into ChatGPT because his judge had asked him to: "What is the ordinary meaning of 'landscaping'?"[^1]

The case behind the question was small. James Snell ran a landscaping business in Alabama under the name Outdoor Expressions. A family hired him to put a trampoline into their backyard — not on the grass but sunk into it, with a retaining wall and a decorative wooden cap around the rim, because an in-ground trampoline looks better and stands firmer than one on legs. A child fell off it and struck her face on the board. Her family sued Snell. His insurer refused to defend him, because the application he had signed described his business in three words, "Insured performs landscaping," and a trampoline, the insurer said, is not landscaping. The district court read the dictionaries the parties supplied and agreed. On appeal both sides spent their briefs on the one word.[^1]

Judge Kevin Newsom joined the court's opinion and wrote separately to say what he had done with that word. The dictionaries had not settled it. Photographs of the finished job had moved him, and he could not say why, and deciding on a gut feeling "has always given me the willies." So, "on what can only be described as a lark," he had a clerk ask the model. The answer came back: "'Landscaping' refers to the process of altering the visible features of an area of land, typically a yard, garden or outdoor space, for aesthetic or practical purposes." Asked the narrower question — "Is installing an in-ground trampoline 'landscaping'?" — ChatGPT said yes. Google's Bard, asked the same, "was more equivocal." The court decided the appeal on other grounds, chiefly what Newsom calls a quirk of Alabama law that folds an insurance application into the policy it precedes; his concurrence ends with a proposal that judges who take ordinary meaning as their rule "consider—consider—whether and how" large language models "might—might—inform the interpretive analysis."[^1]

Formalization — writing a rule down precisely enough that a machine can run it, which is what our group at Singapore Management University has spent six years building a language for — has an answer to Newsom's question, and the answer is a shrug. Encode Snell's policy and the clause that matters computes coverage from a handful of facts, one of which is whether the work was landscaping. That fact is an input. The encoding names it, types it as true-or-false, and computes everything downstream of it; it does not, and cannot, say whether a trampoline sunk into a lawn is landscaping. Someone outside the encoding supplies the value. The whole of what formalization cannot do is in that one word, and the rest of this argument is about where such words live, how many of them there are, and who fills them in.

## The word left open, 1983 and 2026

The first people to put a statute into a computer met the same word in a different coat. In the summer of 1983 a group at Imperial College London translated most of the British Nationality Act 1981 into Prolog, and their 1986 write-up in Communications of the ACM lists what they expected to trip over: "such vague phrases as 'being a good character,' 'having reasonable excuse,' and 'having sufficient knowledge of English.'" They did not try to define them. "The simplest way to handle vagueness is to assume that the vague concepts always apply and to use this assumption to generate qualified answers. For example, Peter is a citizen, if he is of good character."[^2] The machine computed everything it could and handed the rest back as a condition.

Forty-three years later our own encoding of section 1 of the same Act does the same thing, and writes down that it is doing it. The encoding carries twelve places where the text admitted more than one reading; at each, a comment records both readings, the one taken, and why. Two of them sit on the foundling provision — the subsection that deems an abandoned newborn to have been born in the United Kingdom to a citizen parent, "unless the contrary is shown." Here is the first, from the file, with nothing changed:[^3]

> AMBIGUITY: "new-born infant" is undefined by the Act. Reading (i): an infant abandoned within days or weeks of birth, as assessed by the decision-maker (taken here — the boolean input field asserts new-born-ness as a found fact, leaving the vague boundary to the fact-supplier). Reading (ii): any abandoned child of apparently qualifying age (rejected: it reads "new-born" out of the text).

"Leaving the vague boundary to the fact-supplier" is the Imperial group's "if he is of good character," typed out as a field. The twelfth entry in the register is good character itself, which in the current Act qualifies the grant rather than the entitlement, so the encoding keeps it in a separate gate. What the machine does with these words has not changed since 1983. What has changed is that the choice is now recorded at the site, with the losing reading beside it, so that a reviewer can disagree.

Philosophers of language had a name for the thing before the computer scientists met it. In 1945 Friedrich Waismann, arguing against the idea that a statement's meaning is its method of verification, observed that even a precise empirical term can be thrown into doubt by a case nobody imagined — a cat that grows to gigantic size, or dies and comes back — and called the property "Die Porosität der Begriffe," the porosity of concepts, until W. B. Kneale suggested the English "open texture." Waismann was careful that open texture is not vagueness: a vague term is uncertain over cases we can already think of; an open-textured one is uncertain over cases we cannot.[^4] H. L. A. Hart borrowed the phrase for law in 1961 and blurred that line a little — rules, he wrote, have "a fringe of vagueness or 'open texture'" — and gave the example every law student meets: a rule forbidding vehicles in the park is plain for "motor-cars, buses, and motor-cycles" and stops being plain for an electrically propelled toy car. When such a case arrives, Hart said, we "settle the question by choosing between the competing interests in the way that best satisfies us. In doing so we shall have rendered more determinate our initial aim, and shall incidentally have settled a question as to the meaning, for purposes of this rule, of a general word."[^4] Three years earlier, in the Harvard Law Review exchange that made the example famous, Lon Fuller had answered Hart's picture of a clear "core" and an uncertain "penumbra" with a truck: a group of patriots mounts a working military truck on a pedestal in the park as a war memorial. In ordinary talk it is a vehicle. Whether the rule reaches it, Fuller said, cannot be known from the words without knowing what the rule is for.[^5]

Every one of these examples is an input. Vehicle, landscaping, new-born, good character: a formal encoding of the rule takes each as a value it did not compute. Hart's answer — decide, and in deciding make the aim more determinate — is a description of what the person supplying the value does. Fuller's truck is the case where the person has to reach past the word to the purpose. Neither is a description of anything a checker does, and a checker that pretended otherwise would be lying about its inputs.

## Four kinds of case, and what the machine does in each

A design note in our repository — drafted in June 2026 for a paper not yet written, so read it as a classification proposed and not a result found — sorts a court's decision into four strata by how much of it a formal skeleton reproduces.[^6] In the first, the outcome follows from the extracted rules and the found facts: deduction, formalism's home ground. In the second, the rule needs exceptions and priorities, but they are stated or derivable, and one model of the case survives. In the third, the formal structure admits more than one model with different outcomes, and the court closes the fork by a value choice, a canon of construction, or a patch. In the fourth, no skeleton entails the outcome: proportionality, a standard, a balance struck. The note's phrase for the third stratum is "detect-but-don't-resolve," and its argument is that the interesting seam is the one between the second and the third — the point where a formal model can find a problem it cannot settle.

The same note cuts the third and fourth strata another way, by what made the case hard. Some interpretive disputes are about the parse: comma scope, which items a proviso governs, whether an infinitive states a purpose or a result. Some are about a definition. And some are about applying a value-laden standard — reasonable, dishonest, proportionate — to facts. The first kind is avoidable at drafting time; the third is not, and the note says so: "No formalism removes it (and none should)."[^6]

Post 5 of this series walked through a case on the seam. In *Poh Yuan Nie*, decided by Singapore's Court of Appeal on 21 November 2022, the applicants argued that a defined adverb, "dishonestly," governed an undefined adjective, "dishonest," and so imported a property requirement into a cheating charge that involved no property. The court laid out four readings of the word, showed that the applicants' reading would acquit a man who wins an unpaid internship with a forged degree, called that "an absurd state of affairs," and held that another reading would make a clause of the section do nothing. Three of those moves — enumerate the readings, find the case that separates them, prove a clause dead — an encoding in our tree and two solvers reproduce in a fraction of a second. The fourth, the word "absurd," is a judgment about what the section is for, and no solver has one.[^7]

So the division of labor in the third stratum is settled, and it is the division this whole series rests on. The machine finds the fork and, where it can, the fact pattern that makes the fork matter. A person chooses. That is what happened in our pilots too, though post 1 told them as findings rather than as choices. The double bind in the Personal Data Protection Act's breach-notification duty — notify the affected people at once; the regulator may later direct you not to — was found by a timed model in 2022 and resolved by nobody in our group: the guidance beside the Act had already found the same corner and written a way out.[^8] The two readings of an insurer's payout formula in 2023 were found by our encoding and settled by the insurer's management, in the wording of the term, after a conversation about liability rather than about which reading to pay.[^8] In each case the encoding's contribution ended at the fork.

The reviewing guide in our documentation makes that ending a rule. "Real law under-determines," it says. "When our encoders meet an ambiguity they are instructed *not* to guess silently — the opposite of most automation practice. Instead they record a fork: the site, the plausible readings, which was taken provisionally, and why." And whether an encoding says what the law says "is a judgement, and only a named human expert may make it" — a gate the pipeline calls HG1, which is a detached SSH signature over a digest of the files the reviewer read, so that an edit after signing makes the signature stop verifying.[^9] What the guide describes as fork machinery — each fork an enumerated type, the whole set of a corpus's forks one record, the favored reading a default — is designed; the one hand-encoded statute section in the tree records its twelve forks as comments, and its own report says the ruling that would give them a machine-checkable syntax is "deliberately unruled."[^3] The register is a convention today. It broke once, when one of the twelve markers drifted from the exact form and a scan found eleven.[^3]

Economists have a name for the choice the register makes visible. Louis Kaplow, in 1992, defined the difference between a rule and a standard as nothing more than timing: "the only distinction between rules and standards is the extent to which efforts to give content to the law are undertaken before or after individuals act." A rule says fifty-five miles an hour; a standard says an excessive speed, and leaves the number to the adjudicator, later.[^10] Formalization does not convert standards into rules. An encoding of "excessive speed" is an input named *the speed was excessive*, and the encoding is exactly as ex post as the law was. What it adds is a map of which parts of an instrument were rules and which were standards — where the specification ends, and who is standing there when it does. A concept note in our documentation calls that converting surprise discretion into designed discretion: the encoding cannot shrink the discretion, and the note says the aim is not to, but it can say on whose desk it lands.[^11]

## The strongest form of the objection

There is a school that would say the register is the statute. Critical Legal Studies (CLS), the movement that came out of American law schools in the 1970s, held in its strongest form that legal materials do not determine outcomes: that for any case, the doctrine can be made to yield either result, and what decides is politics. Duncan Kennedy's 1976 article on form and substance is the founding text on the choice this post is circling. A standard, he wrote, "refers directly to one of the substantive objectives of the legal order. Some examples are good faith, due care, fairness, unconscionability, unjust enrichment, and reasonableness. The application of a standard requires the judge both to discover the facts of a particular situation and to assess them in terms of the purposes or social values embodied in the standard." The virtues claimed for rules — "the restraint of official arbitrariness and certainty" — were, in his account, one side of a political argument, and the choice between rule and standard was itself a substantive choice dressed as a formal one.[^12] Push the thesis to its limit and every case is in the fourth stratum. The first and second are empty; the third is a story the court tells about a choice it had already made; and an encoding that reports twelve forks in a section has miscounted, because every atom in it is a fork.

Taken at that strength the thesis would overturn this series. Lawrence Solum's 1987 reply put the strong claim as the assertion that any conceivable result can be squared with the existing materials, and argued that it is false while weaker versions are true — the law is underdetermined in many cases, in the sense that the materials rule out some results and leave several standing.[^13] That weaker version is the one a checker can live with, because it is what the third stratum says: more than one model survives, fewer than all. Whether the strong version holds is not a matter for argument, on the design note's view; it is a distribution to measure. Encode a corpus of appellate judgments, run each skeleton on its found facts, and count how many dispositions follow from the rules, how many follow once exceptions are ordered, how many fork, and how many have no skeleton at all. If the first two bins are empty, the critics were right, and the register was a brief. If they are not, the frontier has a location, and a share of adjudication is deduction whatever anyone's politics. The note's own caution is that appellate judgments are selected for difficulty, so the corpus is an adversarial test of formalism and will overstate the third and fourth strata; and that "no skeleton found" is weak evidence, since the extractor may have missed one.[^6]

Mireille Hildebrandt's objection to rules as code is the same objection from a different direction, and it is the one a drafting office will actually raise. Code-driven law, she wrote in 2020, "fold[s] enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke, collapsing the distance between legislator, executive and court." What text affords, and code does not, is contestability: the open-textured concepts of a legal norm — she lists reasonableness, equity, force majeure, foreseeability — generate "a normativity of contestability," which she takes to be a core tenet of the rule of law.[^14] The fork register is a refusal to fold — the reading taken sits beside the reading rejected, and the guide says a superseded reading is "demoted, never deleted"[^9] — but the refusal holds only as long as somebody reads the register. An encoding deployed as a service applies the reading it was given, to every query, with the losing reading in a comment nobody sees. Hildebrandt's point survives contact with our tooling in that form: the register makes interpretation contestable to the reviewer, and does nothing for the person on the receiving end of the answer unless the answer carries the fork with it.

## What the neural tools changed

Newsom did it again that September. In *United States v. Deleon*, on whether a robber who pointed a gun at a cashier had "physically restrained" him within the meaning of the Sentencing Guidelines, he queried three models ten times apiece, opening a fresh chat each time — "Not perfectly scientific, I know, but better than a rifle shot" — and reported that the thirty answers converged on physical force, physical means, physical barriers. "I continue to believe—perhaps more so with each interaction—that LLMs have something to contribute to the ordinary-meaning endeavor."[^15] The scholars had got there ahead of him. Christoph Engel and Richard McAdams put the vehicles-in-the-park question to GPT-3.5 Turbo in seventeen forms, over twenty-five candidate objects, a hundred times each, 42,500 requests, against a survey of 2,835 people who had been asked the same thing; the model told obvious cases from debatable ones, "but overall, it discriminates much less than human participants," and their conclusion was two-handed: "Legal actors have good reason to be cautious, but LLMs have the potential to radically facilitate and improve legal tasks, including the interpretation of statutes."[^16]

The pushback arrived in 2026, and it is specific. Brandon Waldon, Nathan Schneider, Ethan Wilcox, Amir Zeldes and Kevin Tobia, in the Georgetown Law Journal, take Newsom's two concurrences as their occasion — "in 2024, a U.S. judge queried LLM chatbots to interpret a disputed insurance contract and the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines" — and argue that practitioners "run the risk of inappropriately relying on LLMs" to settle questions of meaning.[^17] James Grimmelmann, Benjamin Sobel and David Stein, in the Harvard Journal on Legislation, report that the trampoline question comes back with opposite answers from different versions of the same model; their line is that "the superficial fluency of LLM-generated text conceals fundamental gaps between what these models are currently capable of and what legal interpretation requires."[^18] A group including two of the Georgetown authors ran controlled tests and found that reformatting a question changed the models' conclusions.[^19] So the open-textured word has a new claimant: a model that will supply the value the encoding leaves blank, fluently, and not the same way twice.

What we do with a model is the reverse. In our pipeline the model is asked to widen, not to settle: to emit the readings a clause can bear, so that the formal layer can test whether they differ in consequence and a person can choose among the ones that do. Where a model writes the encoding itself, the register exists so that the model's collapse of a fork into one reading is visible and reversible. The failure mode the 2026 papers document — an answer that depends on the version and the formatting — is the failure mode a register is built to catch, when it is read.

## Where the model is wrong

Jim Woodcock and Peter Gorm Larsen, reviewing Amazon's decade of formal methods this June, reach for George Box: "All models are wrong, but some are useful," which they attribute to Box's 1976 paper in the Journal of the American Statistical Association.[^20] The sentence in that paper is less quotable and more useful. "Since all models are wrong the scientist cannot obtain a 'correct' one by excessive elaboration," Box wrote, and, a line later, "Since all models are wrong the scientist must be alert to what is importantly wrong." The famous form is from a 1987 book.[^20] The 1976 form is the guide. An encoding of a statute is wrong wherever the statute's words were doing work the atoms do not carry, and the places where that happens are exactly the words this argument has been about: the ones whose values the encoding takes from outside. The design note's third and fourth strata are a map of where the model of a law is importantly wrong, and the fork register is the working list of where one encoder thought so.

Three things would overturn the argument of this series, and none of them is settled. If a corpus measurement put nearly all adjudication in the fourth stratum, the machine would be finding forks in a text that is all fork, and the critics of 1976 would have been right about the tool as well as the law. If the forks our encoders record turned out, on review, to be mostly artifacts of the encoding rather than of the text — post 5 conceded that nothing yet counts those — then the register would be measuring us. And if the choice at the fork were being made silently by the model that wrote the encoding, and the reviewer signed without reading, the gate would be a signature over a guess. The instrument that would decide the first is a paper planned, and not yet drafted, for a themed issue on computational legal studies at the Cambridge Forum on AI: Law and Governance, whose call asks how computational methods might "empirically test legal theories" and closes at the end of June 2027: extract the skeleton of each judgment in a Singapore appellate corpus, run it, and count the strata.[^6] The instrument for the second is the register itself, once there are enough of them to sum. The third has no instrument but a reviewer.

Snell's trampoline is in the fourth stratum, or the third; the point is that the encoding does not know which. It knows that coverage turns on the word, and that the word is an input, and that somebody had better be standing there. In May 2024 that was a judge with a gut feeling he distrusted, a stack of dictionaries, and a clerk with a browser open. The court, in the end, decided on the application form.
