Two developers stand at a small round table, one holding a mug, the other bent over a laptop. The one with the mug says that some day we won't even need coders: we'll just write the specification and the program will write itself. The other looks up, delighted — a comprehensive and precise spec, and bam, no more programmers. "Exactly!" says the first, and drinks. Third panel: and do you know the industry term for a project specification that is comprehensive and precise enough to generate a program? Uh… no. Fourth panel: "Code. It's called code." The mug tips and the coffee goes across the table. CommitStrip ran it on 25 August 2016 as "A very comprehensive and precise spec," and the image file on their server still carries the French title it was drawn under: *Les specs c'est du code*.[^1]

The joke was already old. In 1978 Edsger Dijkstra wrote a short note, EWD667, "On the foolishness of 'natural language programming'," about the wish — as old as computing, he said — to be relieved of the care and accuracy that any formal symbolism demands. Formal notations were the reason "school children can learn to do what in earlier days only genius could achieve," and his closing gut feeling was that machines programmed in Dutch or English or Swahili "are as damned difficult to make as they would be to use."[^2] Six years later Donald Knuth proposed the opposite discipline under the name literate programming — "let us concentrate rather on explaining to human beings what we want a computer to do" — which keeps the formal text and wraps it in prose rather than replacing it.[^3] And in January 1985 Bertrand Meyer took a short specification of a text-formatting problem, written in careful English by careful people, and catalogued what was wrong with it: noise, silence, overspecification, contradiction, ambiguity, forward reference, wishful thinking. The seven sins of the specifier. His remedy was a formal one.[^4]

Law got there a generation earlier. Layman Allen opened a 1957 article in the *Yale Law Journal* with the observation that a large amount of litigation over written instruments "can be traced to the draftsman's failure to convey his meaning clearly," and proposed symbolic logic as the drafting tool.[^5] When a group at Imperial College turned the British Nationality Act 1981 into Prolog in 1983 and wrote it up in 1986, they said what the exercise had been good for: an executable representation of rules "can be used not only to apply the rules, but to aid the process of drafting and redrafting the rules in the first place — a point that was made by Layman Allen as long ago as 1957."[^6] Bench-Capon and Coenen gave the design principle its name in 1992 — *isomorphism*, "the well defined correspondence of the knowledge base to the source texts" — and opened with Michael Jackson's line that a program which works is not necessarily right.[^7] A comprehensive and precise specification of a statute is a program. The people who noticed were lawyers and logicians, decades before the comic.

## A hundred languages in a cloud

Between English and code there is a whole population of languages that tried to be both. Tobias Kuhn counted them in 2014: a hundred English-based controlled natural languages (CNLs) — a CNL being a constructed language "based on a certain natural language, being more restrictive concerning lexicon, syntax, and/or semantics while preserving most of its natural properties" — from Ogden's Basic English in 1930 to the Semantic Web, and together they "form a single scattered cloud filling the conceptual space between natural languages such as English on the one end and formal languages such as propositional logic on the other."[^8] Some of the hundred are law. The Massachusetts Senate's legislative drafting language of 2003 is about a hundred rules and close to ninety forbidden words. Attempto Controlled English (ACE), which the Attempto project at the University of Zurich began in 1995 and which translates unambiguously into a variant of first-order logic, "was first presented in 1996 as a language for software specifications." The hundred include COBOL, which John Sowa had called a controlled natural language in 2000. And Robert Kowalski, of the 1986 Imperial group, describes Logical English with three colleagues in 2023 as "syntactic sugar for logic programming languages such as pure Prolog," with coding legal rules as one of its two applications.[^9]

Which is where Matthew Waddington comes in. Waddington is a legislative drafter who leads the Computer-Readable Legislation Project at Jersey's Legislative Drafting Office, and his project has been working with our group at Singapore Management University.[^10] On 12 September he put to Meng the question drafters will keep putting: why leave natural language at all? If what a drafter wants is an editor that flags every ambiguity and every drafting error as the draft is typed, why not build that as language processing over the English, and keep pushing Logical English and its cousins in that direction? Meng's reply, in his own account of the exchange, was that a prototype editor evolved far enough to do what Waddington wants — "allowing a realtime editor component to flag every ambiguity and every drafting error that you want drafters to learn to avoid" — ends somewhere specific: "you will end up with a CNL: a natural language restricted sufficiently to allow that degree of rigour; and between that language and L4 there is no daylight."[^11] [NEEDS MENG: whether Waddington's side of the exchange may be paraphrased here, and whether the date and the medium are right.] That is the comic's third panel, spoken to a drafting office. A checker that catches every error a drafter could make has, by construction, a grammar of what it accepts; a natural language with a grammar of what it accepts is a controlled natural language; and a controlled natural language for rules with a checker behind it is what a programmer calls a specification language. L4 is built on that bet. The draft of our paper for ICAIL, the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Law, describes the surface as "deliberately engineered as a controlled natural language": uppercase keywords for the language and backticked lowercase phrases for the law's own vocabulary, the convention by which COBOL and SQL set their keywords apart from the programmer's names; indentation for precedence rather than parentheses; three dots for an unspoken *and* and two for an unspoken *or*, so a statute's list of limbs can be quoted with its own punctuation.[^12]

## Intent is the source of truth

The software industry arrived at the same panel from the other side, and fast. In February 2025 Andrej Karpathy posted that there was "a new kind of coding I call 'vibe coding', where you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists."[^13] On 14 July Amazon launched Kiro, an editor whose selling point is that before its agent writes code it writes three documents — requirements, a design, a task list.[^14] On 2 September GitHub open-sourced Spec Kit, and the announcement put the thesis in one line: "We're moving from 'code is the source of truth' to 'intent is the source of truth.'"[^15] Thoughtworks put spec-driven development on its November Technology Radar to assess, with a warning attached: "We may be relearning a bitter lesson — that handcrafting detailed rules for AI ultimately doesn't scale."[^16] On 6 November Collins made *vibe coding* its word of the year.[^17] And on 3 September 2026 David Fowler, a distinguished engineer at Microsoft, posted four words — "Typing code is absolutely over" — which are the whole of the statement on record; the paragraphs about specification and system design that followed in the coverage were the writers' own.[^18]

None of this is new either, and the people who say so most sharply work in the field that promised it last time. Jordi Cabot, placing low-code inside model-driven engineering in 2020, traced the lineage back through computer-aided software engineering (CASE) tools to the first CAiSE conference in 1991, where a paper already promised that "given the final model, the complete computerized information system can be automatically generated."[^19] Myers, Hudson and Pausch, surveying user-interface tools in 2000, gave the recurring outcome its vocabulary: a tool has a *threshold*, how hard it is to learn, and a *ceiling*, how much it can do, and "the most successful current systems seem to be either low threshold and low ceiling, or high threshold and high ceiling."[^20] Low-code lowered the threshold. The customers who needed the ceiling wrote code.

The nearest thing to a controlled experiment on the legal version was posted to arXiv on 14 August 2026 by James Wiles, who implemented a deontic formalism in Wolfram Language and then asked GPT-4 to translate English legal statements into it. Four statements after a warm-up; three of the four were "accepted as correct; actually wrong." His Statement A was a disjunctive condition. The generated function misused a pattern operator outside any pattern, "so its two conditions never evaluate to anything," and its final comparison "always fails, so the function returns True for every input." It ran. It read plausibly. It permitted everything. His abstract's phrase for the class is "code that runs, reads plausibly, but silently encodes the wrong norm," and his proposed fix is an authoring surface that works "the way Copilot and grammar checkers work today": formal building blocks carrying English renderings, suggested inline as the lawyer drafts.[^21]

That proposal is the halfway house, and the vibe-coding reading of it is the trap. Copilot's suggestion is the artifact: same buffer, same language, the thing that ships. In Wiles's sketch the English draft conditions a translation into a different representation, so the draft is a prompt, and re-editing it re-runs a fallible translator with nothing standing between the drafter and a guard that returns True for every input. What made vibe coding survivable for the developers who tried it was the harness around the prompt — a durable test suite, a type checker, a reviewer who reads the diff — and the field's measurement of the prompt without the harness is a caution: a randomized trial published in July 2025 by the research group METR had sixteen experienced open-source developers work 246 real issues in their own repositories, and with AI tools allowed "they take 19% longer than without," while believing afterwards that they had been sped up by 20 percent.[^22] The neural tools translate; they are good at it, and our own architecture uses a large language model for exactly that, to draft a first formalization from the source text and to verbalize an evaluation trace back to the reader. What differs is where the draft lands: in a language a compiler checks and a lawyer can read beside the source. The ICAIL draft's claim — made from engagements, not from a study — is that business stakeholders who are not programmers have read an L4 ruleset and signed off on it as the rules they intend.[^12] [NEEDS MENG: which engagement the sign-off finding comes from, how many stakeholders, and what "sign off" consisted of.]

## A specification in my own context window

The document that configures the agent writing these words is a specification in prose. Meng's `~/CLAUDE.md` runs to 6,891 words over 501 lines. It opens with a 2025 conversation about L4 in thirteen turns, carries a backup log with drive sizes and rsync flags, sets a commit-trailer convention, and ends with seven numbered rules on keeping written claims true, each followed by a block headed **Why** that names the incident which produced it — a commit, `7fd0b96`, whose message reads "correct my own status header, which asserted a migration that has not happened"; a pull request, #375, that shipped without the trailer the file requires. Rule five carries a retraction in place: "An earlier version of this file asserted the title was unreadable and told you to always ask. That was wrong, and it cost a question every session."[^23] The vendor's documentation says what such a file is and is not: Claude "treats them as context, not enforced configuration," instructions should be "concrete enough to verify," and the recommended size is under 200 lines.[^24]

Read as an exhibit, the file has acquired the discipline of a controlled language without the mechanism. Every ambiguity that cost a session produced a scope qualifier — "State this rule with its scope," the repository's sibling file says, after an unqualified sentence sent a session hunting a trap that did not exist. Every misreading produced a Why block, which is a regression test written in prose because there was nowhere to write it as code. Rule five, which tells the reader to run the cheapest check that could falsify a claim before writing it down, is a lint pass executed by a human. The file even records a reader who parsed it wrong: on 8 September a session read a line of the harness's own boilerplate as overriding the convention, stripped a trailer from a commit, and pushed. The fix was another paragraph. A grammar and a checker would have made it a compile error. This is one file, written by one person for one reader, so it proves nothing; it is what a specification looks like in 2026 when the reader is a machine and the language is still English.

## What does not transfer

Three things stop the analogy short of the comic's fourth panel, and the paper has to say all three.

In software the code is a build output and the spec may outlive it; the Tessl framework's bet, as the Thoughtworks radar describes it, is that "the specification itself becomes the maintained artifact, rather than the code."[^16] In law the text is the operative artifact. A signed contract is not regenerated from a spec, and a court reads the words. So "spec durable, code ephemeral" does not carry over, and L4's insistence that the formal text track the source word for word is what that costs: the formal layer has to be the binding text or a faithful projection of it, and the isomorphism Bench-Capon and Coenen asked for in 1992 is what makes the projection checkable.[^7] Model-driven engineering lost its last round partly because models went stale — nobody had to read them, and the code was what shipped. Law's structural advantage is that the prose must be read. The drift returns the moment a statute is amended and the encoding is not, which is why the drafting office — the one persona that could write the rule once, in one artifact — matters more to this program than its headcount suggests, and why Waddington's question deserved a better answer than "use ours."

The affordances are designed, and they have not been user-tested. The ICAIL draft names "a controlled study of authorability" as future work; the design note for the CNL paper says in terms that the affordances are "designed, not yet user-tested."[^25] Kuhn's hundred languages are a warning as well as a map: a scattered cloud is what a field looks like when no one has yet made a controlled language that people write in unprompted, and the sign-off finding above is an anecdote until it is a study.

And the critics of the wider program are right about the thing they are right about. Waddington, in a February 2025 interview: "There's very little that you can completely automate in fact. Or I might even say there's nothing you can completely automate."[^10] Mireille Hildebrandt's objection to code-driven law is that it collapses the distance between the legislator who enacts, the official who applies and the court that interprets. [UNVERIFIED: the chapter's publisher PDF and its VUB, SSRN and Bloomsbury copies were all blocked today; the paraphrase follows the reading recorded for post 1 on 2026-09-13, and no sentence is quoted.][^26] A specification language a drafter writes in, with a checker that shows where the draft contradicts itself, leaves the court exactly where it was. Nothing in the fourth panel says who reads the code. The paper says it has to be the lawyer, and sets out the syntax that lets them.

That paper is in draft for the workshop on Controlled Natural Language, with Adam Wyner, and evaluates L4's surface — the two registers, the indentation, the asyndeton — through Green and Petre's Cognitive Dimensions of Notations, the vocabulary the notation-design field uses to say what a syntax affords and what it costs.[^25] The next post in the series is planned to ask what the compiler checks once the text is in.
