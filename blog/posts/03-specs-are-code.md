A four-panel comic from 2016. One developer tells another that some day we won't even need coders any more: write a comprehensive and precise specification, and the program writes itself. Does he know the industry term for a specification that precise? Two balloons. "Code." "It's called code." The mug tips and the drink spills. CommitStrip published it in English as "A very comprehensive and precise spec," and the image file on their server still carries the French title it was drawn under: *Les specs c'est du code*.[^1]

We have spent six years running the joke backwards, on documents people had signed. An insurance policy is a specification: it says, in English, what is paid and when. In 2023 a major insurer — millions of customers; it stays unnamed — had our group at Singapore Management University write one of its policies in L4, the programming language for law we build. The insurer's own claims adjusters walked us through it. The payout formula turned out to have two readings, and the adjusters had been paying the larger one. Reporting that was delicate, since the adjusters were the people who could be penalized for overpaying, so we presented it upstairs as a defect in the wording. When it reached the executives the conversation was about liability. As I recall it — the slides are lost — nobody said which reading future claims would be paid on, and the fix went into the wording of the term.[^2]

A commercial agreement is a specification too. For a payments company we took contracts dense with fee tables, wrote them in L4, and served them to the company's own systems through a query interface, so that the contract itself answered "what is the fee on this transaction."[^2] Neither engagement generated a program from a specification. Each found that the specification, read precisely enough to execute, had been a program all along — in one case with a bug.

The joke was old in 2016. In January 1985 Bertrand Meyer catalogued the faults of a specification written in careful English by careful people: seven kinds, of which two are the ones a policyholder meets. *Ambiguity* is a sentence with two readings, which is what the payout formula had. *Contradiction* is two sentences that cannot both be obeyed, which is what a data-breach regulation had when one of our early pilots found a state in which a firm was obliged to notify its users and advised not to.[^3] Meyer's remedy was a formal notation.

In the summer of 1983 a group at Imperial College London typed a large part of the British Nationality Act 1981 into Prolog, a language whose programs are lists of if-then rules the machine can be asked questions about. In January 1984 they showed it to Home Office officials involved in drafting the act. Their 1986 paper says an executable representation "can be used not only to apply the rules, but to aid the process of drafting and redrafting the rules in the first place — a point that was made by Layman Allen as long ago as 1957."[^4] It records the demonstration and no adoption.[^5] Law got there first. Forty years on, the drafter's tool is still a word processor.

## Why leave English

Rules as code — the OECD's 2020 name for "a machine-consumable version of some types of government rules, to exist alongside the existing natural language counterpart" — is the government version of the wish. Its drafting-office arm in Jersey is Matthew Waddington's Computer-Readable Legislation Project, which has been working with our group.[^6] In an interview in February 2025 he said what such a tool is for — a logical error the computer finds in a draft: "I can now fix it before that draft becomes final and gets enacted." And what it is not. Hand the job to a large language model (LLM) instead of a human, and "you have to accept that you will have no way of being sure whether it has done it right."[^6]

The question drafting offices keep putting to us is why leave English at all. If the goal is an editor that flags every ambiguity and every drafting error as the draft is typed, why not build it as language processing over the English the drafter already writes? Why not push Logical English — Prolog that reads as English sentences, from Robert Kowalski of the 1983 Imperial group and three colleagues — further in that direction?[^7] My answer, on 12 September, was that a prototype editor evolved "far enough to achieve your goals of allowing a realtime editor component to flag every ambiguity and every drafting error that you want drafters to learn to avoid" ends somewhere specific: "you will end up with a CNL: a natural language restricted sufficiently to allow that degree of rigour; and between that language and L4 there is no daylight."[^8]

That is a bet, and the clause in the middle carries it: the errors "you want drafters to learn to avoid." A checker that only flags things restricts nothing — a spell-checker underlines a word and imposes no vocabulary on you; a form that will not submit until the date is written one way does — so what matters is which errors are on the list. The ones a drafter wants caught are the ones it took a formalization to find: two limbs that cannot both hold, a term used before it is defined, a proviso that binds nothing, an "and" a court could read as "or." Each is a fact about the sentence's logical structure, which a checker must first recover, and a parser recovering structure from unrestricted legal English will sometimes be unsure. Then it either guesses, which is the silent failure, or asks the drafter to rewrite until it is sure. A drafter who rewrites until the parser is sure is writing in the language the parser is sure about, and that language has a grammar.

A natural language restricted until a checker can always be sure of it is a controlled natural language (CNL). Tobias Kuhn counted a hundred of them in 2014, "a single scattered cloud filling the conceptual space between natural languages such as English on the one end and formal languages such as propositional logic on the other."[^9] A specification precise enough to check is the comic's fourth panel. What would falsify the bet is a checker that catches a deontic contradiction — deontic being the logician's word for the logic of must and may; here, an obligation and a prohibition that fire on the same facts — over unrestricted enacted English, reliably enough to draft against. Nobody has shown one. We would like to be shown.

Where in the cloud you land is the second half of the bet. The argument to a drafting office is for the class; for the instance, here is section 1(5A) of the British Nationality Act 1981, from the encoding in our repository, beside the words it encodes.[^10] The statute:

> (5A) Those requirements are that on the date on which the order is made or the Convention adoption is effected (as the case may be)—
> (a) the adopter or, in the case of a joint adoption, one of the adopters is a British citizen; and
> (b) in a case within subsection (5)(b), the adopter or, in the case of a joint adoption, both of the adopters are habitually resident in the United Kingdom or in a designated territory.

The L4:

```
@ref British Nationality Act 1981 s 1(5A)
GIVEN adoption IS AN AdoptionCase
GIVETH A BOOLEAN
`1(5A) — the requirements are met on the date of the order or adoption` adoption MEANS
        "(5A) Those requirements are that on the date on which the order is made or the Convention adoption is effected (as the case may be)—"
    ... `1(5A)(a) — the adopter, or one joint adopter, a British citizen` adoption
    AND `1(5A)(b) — habitual residence, in a Convention case` adoption
```

The uppercase words are the language's own — the rule's input (GIVEN), the type of its answer (GIVETH), its definition (MEANS), the connective (AND) — and everything in backticks is the law's vocabulary, a whole phrase used as a name. Indentation carries the structure; there are no parentheses. The line in quotation marks is the statute's own opening words, pasted in, and the machine reads past them: a quoted sentence sitting where a true-or-false belongs counts for nothing, so the statute's connecting prose can sit inside the rule without changing what it decides. The three dots are an unspoken *and*, three dots for three letters, so that the limbs line up in a column as a drafter lists them. Two dots are an unspoken *or*; the (b) limb, a few lines up in the same file, uses them to render its proviso as "not a Convention case, or habitually resident."[^10]

The difference from Logical English is what happens to a mistake. An L4 rule has a type the compiler checks before anything runs, so a rule that applies to the wrong kind of thing is refused at that point; Logical English runs as Prolog, which has no such step, so the rule runs and comes back with no answer. Catala, a language for statutes from Inria and Microsoft Research, interleaves each article of law with a rule in its own code syntax, so the lawyer reads the article and then, beneath it, a program. L4 makes the rule the text the lawyer reads, with the statute's words inside it, and compiles out — to Catala, among others, and to Blawx, a visual tool in which people who know the law and do not write code assemble rules from blocks.[^11]

The person on the other end of the payout formula had signed the policy without reading it, which is most of us. The first reader I had in mind was that person — someone unsure of his position under the law, or under a handful of insurance policies he signed without understanding them — and the lawyer came later. The data-breach pilot ended in a web wizard for exactly him: enter the facts of your situation, get back what you must do, each answer citing the clause it came from; it is still online.[^12] What he cannot do yet is upload his lease. Every encoding in these pilots was written by hand. The surface above is what lets someone who is not a programmer check a hand-written encoding, and the last post in the series, planned, is about who else could run the encoding and at what cost.[^12]

## Intent is the source of truth

The software industry reached the comic's third panel from the other side, and fast. In February 2025 Andrej Karpathy named "a new kind of coding I call 'vibe coding', where you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists."[^13] By September GitHub had an open-source toolkit for it and a thesis in a line: "We're moving from 'code is the source of truth' to 'intent is the source of truth.'"[^14] The promise has been made before, since at least 1991, by tools that were easy to start with and hit a wall — low threshold, low ceiling, in the vocabulary of a 2000 paper on user-interface toolkits — and the customers who needed the ceiling wrote code.[^15]

Machine translation from statute to logic has had a benchmark since 2020, on which GPT-3 in 2023 "performs poorly."[^16] James Wiles, in a preprint of August 2026, implemented a deontic formalism in Wolfram Language and asked GPT-4 to translate four English legal statements into it. Three of the four were "accepted as correct; actually wrong." Statement A was a rule with a disjunctive condition: anyone not wearing a tie, or blond, must leave the room. The generated checking function misused a pattern operator outside any pattern, "so its two conditions never evaluate to anything," and its final comparison "always fails, so the function returns True for every input." Three of the four tests — the examples with known right answers that get run against the code — expected True, so the suite looked healthy. It ran. It read plausibly. It ordered everyone out of the room. "Neither GPT-4 nor I noticed the bug at the time," Wiles writes. His name for the class is "code that runs, reads plausibly, but silently encodes the wrong norm"; his fix is formal building blocks with English renderings, suggested inline as the lawyer drafts, "the way Copilot and grammar checkers work today."[^17]

Neural tools translate well enough that our own architecture uses a large language model for exactly that — to draft a first formalization, and to explain a result in prose.[^18] The difference is where the draft lands. In Wiles's sketch the English draft is a prompt, and re-editing it re-runs a fallible translator with nothing to catch a guard that returns True for every input. In Copilot's the suggestion is the artifact, in the file that ships, and what made that survivable was everything around the prompt: a test suite, a type checker, a reviewer who reads the diff. Even so, a randomized trial by the research group Model Evaluation and Threat Research (METR), published in July 2025, had sixteen experienced open-source developers work 246 real issues in their own repositories, tests and reviewers included. With AI tools allowed "they take 19% longer than without," while believing afterwards that they had been sped up by 20 percent.[^19]

So what does a language a compiler checks buy against a wrong norm that is well-formed? Less than the word "compiler" suggests. Type-checking catches the ill-formed, a name used and never defined. It cannot catch a rule that is well-typed and wrong, which is Wiles's class. Our own draft for the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Law (ICAIL) says so: the gap "between an isomorphic formalisation and a *faithful* one … is not something the language can close on its own; it remains, as it should, a matter for human legal judgement, now better supported by tools."[^18] What catches the wrong norm is a reader with the two texts side by side, and the surface above is engineered so that the reader can be the lawyer. The draft's claim, from the engagements rather than from a study, is that non-technical business stakeholders "are able to *read* an L4 ruleset and *sign off* on it as a faithful statement of the rules they intend." It names no engagement and gives no count.[^18] Tests are the other half, and the subject of a later post, planned.

## What does not transfer

In software the code is a build output and the specification may outlive it; one framework's bet, as the Thoughtworks radar describes it, is that "the specification itself becomes the maintained artifact, rather than the code."[^14] In law the text is the operative artifact. A signed contract is not regenerated from a specification, and a court reads the words.[^20] The price is L4's insistence that the formal text track the structure of its source clause by clause — *isomorphism*, in the word Bench-Capon and Coenen argued for in 1992, "the well defined correspondence of the knowledge base to the source texts."[^21] The formal layer is a projection of the binding text, and isomorphism is what lets a reader audit it — a reader, and only a reader. Nothing in our tree checks isomorphism. Our own appendix says a translation "earns the word *isomorphic* only if a reader can lay the two texts side by side and check them clause by clause."[^18] Law's structural advantage is that the prose must be read; the encoding is what goes stale.

Oracle has sold a rules engine of that kind since 2008: an analyst writes rules "in plain English" in Microsoft Word, styled to compile for a determinations engine, and Britain's tax authority used it for its employment-status tool.[^22] It neither died nor won, and the reason is where it sits. It is bought by the agency that administers a law after enactment, a second artifact beside the statute that the office which wrote the statute never sees, and it drifts the moment the statute is amended and the rulebase is not. That is why the drafting office, which could write the rule once, in one artifact, matters out of proportion to its headcount.

The affordances are designed and have not been user-tested. The ICAIL draft names "a controlled study of authorability" as future work; the design note for the planned paper on the surface says in terms that the affordances are "designed, not yet user-tested."[^23] Kuhn's scattered cloud is what a field looks like when no one has yet made a controlled language that people write in unprompted, and the sign-off above is an anecdote until it is a study. The METR developers believed they had been sped up. A stakeholder who signs off believes the ruleset says what they intend, and that belief is what a study would have to test.

Mireille Hildebrandt wrote in 2020: "What code-driven law does is to fold enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke, collapsing the distance between legislator, executive and court. It has to foresee all potential scenarios and develop sub-rules that hopefully cover all future interactions."[^24] Her objection is to code that replaces the instrument, and a compile error a drafter fixes on Tuesday binds no court on Thursday. Her second sentence lands on us anyway. An encoding beside a statute will be the version that gets consulted, and what it did not foresee will look like what the statute did not provide for. Isomorphism is our answer to that. It is a discipline kept by a reader, and it guarantees nothing. Nothing in the comic's fourth panel says who reads the code. The paper planned with Adam Wyner for the workshop on Controlled Natural Language puts the lawyer in that seat, and proposes to evaluate the surface above through Green and Petre's Cognitive Dimensions of Notations, a vocabulary for what a notation affords and what it costs.[^23] The drafter who asked why leave English is the reader that paper is for.
