**STATUS 2026-09-14: DRAFT — machine-drafted, persona-reviewed twice, revised against both rounds and a live fact-check; not yet read by Meng. Citations checked live 2026-09-14. One [NEEDS MENG] marker stands, in note 19, on the replication's blinding protocol; voice pass 2026-09-14.**

Query five of nine reads, in full: "Assuming all other conditions are met and no other exclusions apply … will my policy apply if I was hospitalized for punching my own face to show off for my friends and I did not commit fraud or misrepresentation?" The policy is a page and a half — a hospital cash policy, the kind that pays a fixed sum for each day you spend in a ward, between CODEX INSURANCE LIMITED and a blank line. It is printed as an appendix to a paper posted to arXiv on 24 February 2025 by six authors, three of them at Stanford, who call it the Simplified Chubb policy. The gold answer, the one the authors decided was correct and scored every model against, is "No." The best of the seven models they tested was OpenAI's o1-preview, a reasoning model trained, in the words of its system card, "to reason using chain of thought" before it answers. Asked cold, it got query five wrong in nine trials of ten. Asked first to translate the policy into Prolog, a programming language built for stating rules and having a machine apply them, and then to put the question to the program, it got the same question wrong in nine of ten again.[^1]

A lawyer, asked why, would go looking for the clause that makes the cause of the hospital stay a condition of payment. In the policy as printed there is no such clause. Clause 1.1 conditions payment on the policy being in effect "at the time of the hospitalization for sickness or accidental injury on which the claim for such benefit is premised"; clause 2.1 lists five exclusions, from skydiving to being eighty. The policy is adapted from an illustration Oliver Goodenough and Preston Carlson published in 2024 under an open license, and theirs has a Benefits section saying what is paid and for what: confinement "as a result of sickness or accidental Injury." The printed version does not. Two of its clauses still refer to a "Section 5 below" that is no longer below anything.[^2] The paper reads clause 1.1 as requiring that the stay result from sickness or accidental injury, and explains the misses by saying the cause of the claim is "addressed indirectly in the contract." So the page grants no benefit and conditions none on cause, and every reading of it — the paper's, the models', ours — supplies something the page does not say. A ninety-percent miss rate on that question measures, in some part, what a model, or a person, will supply to a page that is silent.

Every serious approach to legal AI — software that answers a legal question about a set of facts — works on that terrain. A text, a question, a gold answer that depends on a reading, and a score that looks like a measurement of reasoning. There are three bets on how to build the software. They turn out to be bets on different parts of the job.

## The three bets

The first bet is that the model should be trained on law. The academic line runs from 2020;[^3] the commercial version is Harvey, and Harvey's three years are the bet in motion. By April 2024 it was describing a custom case-law model built with OpenAI; fine-tuning and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), which fetches relevant documents into the prompt, had on its account not been enough. Lawyers at ten large firms preferred that model's output to GPT-4's 97 percent of the time.[^4] Thirteen months later Harvey announced that "seven models" from three vendors "now outperform the originally benchmarked Harvey system."[^5] In August 2026 it changed course again, to Harvey Tenet: a model whose weights a Chinese lab had published for anyone to use, trained further on legal work.[^6]

The second bet is that the model is already good enough and the craft is in how it is used: the retrieval, the prompts, the tools it may call and, since o1-preview in September 2024, how long it is allowed to think before it answers.[^7] The bet has scoreboards, on which Harvey does well,[^9] and it has critics. Varun Magesh, Daniel Ho and colleagues at Stanford tested the research tools of LexisNexis and Thomson Reuters in 2024, after LexisNexis had guaranteed "hallucination-free" legal citations. Each tool hallucinated — answered incorrectly, or cited a source that does not support the answer — between 17 and 33 percent of the time.[^10] Andrew Blair-Stanek and Benjamin Van Durme put 500 questions from real cases to three models, twenty times each at temperature zero, the setting meant to make a model answer the same way every time. They got contradictory verdicts on between 10.6 and 50.4 percent of them. "In none of them was the legal analysis unsound. They simply weighed arguments or interests differently in different runs."[^11]

The third bet is that the answer should come from something that is not a model: a program, written from the rule, that a machine runs the same way every time. The model does the parts programs are bad at — reading the facts, drafting the program, explaining the result — and the program makes the decision. The word for the family is neurosymbolic.[^12] In law the idea is forty years old. A large part of the British Nationality Act 1981 was running as a Prolog program at Imperial College in 1986, and the shelf since then runs through national tax-and-benefit engines to the languages of the last five years, including ours.[^13][^14] Having the model write the program is a 2022 idea.[^15] The February 2025 paper is that idea applied to insurance; four of its six authors had reported in October 2024 that o1-preview could encode a health-insurance contract where GPT-4o could not.[^8] Its guided condition, the model given a fixed schema of facts to encode against, scored 1.00 for both GPT-4o and o1, on nine items over five encodings each, with DeepSeek-R1 at 0.73 in the same table.[^1]

Two 2026 arrivals show how far the bet spreads. Oskar von Cossel, at the Max Planck Institute in Hamburg, benchmarked Rulemapping — a tree of and, or and not that a lawyer draws, with a language model confined to the leaves — on 868 social-media posts under the German hate-speech statute. Precision, the share of flagged posts that were in fact illegal, went from 0.34–0.49 under prose prompting to 0.80–0.86; Rulemapping's own recall, the share of illegal posts it caught, ran 0.82–0.89.[^16]

In July 2026 the Axiom Foundation launched with "over 3,000 provisions" of US tax and benefit law, encoded by an AI pipeline, every value citing its statute. Its check on an encoding is a cross-check against engines that already exist, chief among them PolicyEngine, which Max Ghenis, Axiom's founder and chief executive, co-founded on a core forked from OpenFisca, the French state's tax-and-benefit engine. Disagreements get flagged.[^17]

L4 is in the third camp, on a particular corner of it. It is the language we have built for writing legal rules as typed, executable code, with a checker that rejects an ill-formed encoding and an evaluator that runs a well-formed one. Meng's phrase for the target, in the project's working record, is "a reasoner API to support traceable, audit-grade tool calling by LLM AIs" — the model is the "right brain" that talks, the evaluator is the "left brain" that decides. The model is not in the reasoning loop. A rule marked `@export` in an L4 file becomes a web endpoint and a tool under the Model Context Protocol (MCP), the standard through which assistants like Claude call external tools. The endpoint returns a decision and, on request, the full evaluation trace; the MCP tool, as the codebase stands today, returns the decision alone.[^18]

Axiom's cross-check and L4's type checker buy different things. The cross-check is an empirical signal of fidelity, and it inherits the errors of the engine it checks against. The type checker gives totality — every set of facts the rule admits gets an answer, so no fact-pattern falls through. It gives exhaustive case analysis — every branch of a choice is handled, so a case the encoder forgot is an error rather than a silent default. That rules out a class of encoding defects. It says nothing about whether the encoding reads clause 1.1 the way a court would; neither check tests that, and the guided encodings that disagreed about the punch all ran.[^17]

## What the replication showed

The February paper prints no Prolog encoding of the policy, and says in a footnote that encodings which "failed to run" were scored by "manually" reasoning through them.[^1] So we ran it again, on 31 August and 1 September 2026, from one Claude Code session. What follows is from the working notes; the encodings and harness sit on a development branch that is still unpushed, so a reader cannot open them yet.[^19]

The encoders were not people. Each trial was a language-model agent — claude-sonnet-5, on every launch — dropped into a sandbox with the policy, the nine questions and one of the paper's three conditions: answer cold, translate the policy into Prolog or L4 unguided, or translate it against the paper's schema of facts. Ten trials a cell, five cells. The agents were told nothing of the paper's scores and were forbidden to open the answer key or search for the paper. A harness ran every encoding instead of reading it, with a Prolog interpreter installed so both languages executed; an encoding that would not run failed its trial.

"Blind" means that and no more. The paper has been on arXiv since February 2025, so a model may carry its reading of clause 1.1 in its weights. And the notes record a channel of our own: a line in the agents' shared memory that named the deleted section, which two trials disclosed seeing. It was left in place for the whole arm on the printed policy and removed for the restored arm. The one argument against contamination is in the results: neither unguided encoder reproduced the paper's reading.

The first thing that failed was ours. The L4 schema we handed to the guided encoders did not compile: two field names needed backticks, and a helper was written in a syntax L4 does not have. Both L4 encoders in the pilot found the fault, repaired it, and wrote the repair up in their notes, without asking anyone. Two Prolog agents died on API timeouts having written nothing, and were relaunched into clean sandboxes. And one agent, asked the questions cold, reached unprompted for "the usual insurance-law reading that looks at whether the result (getting hurt) was intended, not just whether the underlying act (throwing the punch) was voluntary" — the distinction the courts have argued about for ninety years, and one the paper's reading of the policy never mentions.[^19]

Seven of the nine questions turned out to be mechanical — firefighter, seventy-eight years old, skydiving. In the arm that used the policy as printed, every trial in every condition got all seven right: 350 items of 350, from forty encodings and ten prompted answers. In the arm with the Benefits section restored, one encoding in 350 missed query nine, so "mechanical" has a thin edge. Every other miss sat on query five and on query four, which asks about "a fall while traveling abroad" after a wellness visit "8 months after the policy's effective date" and never says when the hospitalization happened — the moment clause 1.1 turns on.

On query five, in the pilot, all four unguided encodings — both languages, reading the policy as printed — built no field for the cause of the stay at all: coverage turned on the policy being in effect and no exclusion applying. That is one reading of a text that grants nothing in terms; the paper's is another; each supplies a premise. The encoding shows what it supplied, as a field that is there or is not, where a prose answer buries it. Over the ten-trial cells the unguided encoders reached the gold answer on query five in 3 trials of 10 in Prolog and 2 of 10 in L4. Given the paper's guided schema — supplied in both languages — the L4 encoder wrote the self-inflicted punch down as neither sickness nor accidental injury in seven trials of ten and as an accidental injury in three; the Prolog encoder, six and four. Every guided trial that missed, in either language, missed on that one field. The expert vocabulary got both encoders to the point where the question could be asked. It did not decide the answer.

The scores — 0.900 to 0.967 across the five cells, most trials at 8 of 9 — sit level with the paper's best unguided cell and are no evidence that L4 does better. Ten trials a cell, language and encoder and protocol all changed at once, and one item in nine is eleven points, where the paper reports one guided cell with a standard error of 0.17. With the Benefits section restored — an arm whose predictions were written down before any trial ran, in our own notes and no public registry — unguided encoders got query five right in 14 of 20 trials instead of 5 of 20, and the plain-prompt condition rose from 1 in 10 to 5 in 10. Query four did not move. No condition reached the ceiling on query five, even with the clause and the schema both supplied.[^19]

## Where the variance went

Read the three bets against that instance. Whether a deliberate punch is an "accidental injury" is a question of construction — the meaning a court would give the words on these facts, in this jurisdiction — and no dictionary settles it. Since Landress in 1934, when Cardozo warned in dissent that distinguishing accidental results from accidental means "will plunge this branch of the law into a Serbonian Bog," the cases have divided on whether it is the act or the injury that must be unintended.[^20]

What the first bet buys is familiarity with the authorities; two encoders given the same schema still split on the ruling. Turning facts into a filled-in schema and a page of policy into a first-draft program is drafting under a budget, and drafting is what the second bet buys. Whether facts plus rule yield "No" is the inference, and the third bet makes that step reproducible given an encoding: the same program on the same facts returns the same answer, which needs no trials to establish. The 350 agreeing answers show that fifty trials agreed on seven easy items, and nothing more. What the replication measured is where the variance went once inference was fixed: into the reading and the drafting. A deterministic core does nothing about those. It moves them to a place where they can be seen and argued about, one fact at a time.

Rulemapping is the same bet from a different corner — a visual tree a lawyer draws, where L4 is a text a lawyer reads — measured from outside our camp. Von Cossel's tree took an expert about three hours to draw and four to five more to refine against fifty pilot cases. The model at the leaves more than doubled precision, and the named failure the tree prevents is "scope drift," a model mistaking a post it finds offensive for one that is illegal.[^16] The Stanford hallucination study points the same way, with the authors' caveat attached. "Reasoning error" — the documents were retrieved and relevant, and the tool still drew the wrong conclusion — is coded on 61 percent of Westlaw's hallucinations against 28 percent of Lexis's. But the causes were assigned by inspection, they overlap, and "Because these are closed systems, we are not able to clearly identify a single point of failure for each hallucination."[^10] That is suggestive, and it is no controlled comparison of architectures.

## What the evidence does not show

The third bet has been tried at scale before, and mostly did not take. Legal expert systems were built and sold through the 1980s and 1990s, and the field wrote its own post-mortem: Philip Leith asked in 2010 why, "despite their failure," the "appeal of commoditising legal expertise continues to allure the unwary."[^21] One lineage never stopped shipping. The Australian rule engine Oracle bought in 2008, sold today as Oracle Intelligent Advisor, runs eligibility tools for tax, benefit and immigration agencies — and it is proprietary, with the closed-box problem the Stanford group met at Westlaw and Lexis.[^21]

Rich Sutton's essay of March 2019 is the standing objection from outside the field: "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin," and building in what we think we know tends to lose to search and learning over a long enough run.[^22] An encoding is human knowledge built in. The reply available here is that the encoding is the rule, not a heuristic about the rule, so it does not become less true as models improve. The reply has a cost the essay would recognize: von Cossel's seven or eight expert hours bought one paragraph of one code.

His own critique lands on languages like ours. Sentence-based systems such as PROLEG, Ken Satoh's Prolog system for Japanese civil cases, require, he writes, "legal knowledge to be authored in a programming formalism, reintroducing the knowledge-acquisition bottleneck that visualisation aimed to mitigate."[^16] L4 is a programming formalism. The answer on offer: the encoding reads beside the source, clause for clause — what Trevor Bench-Capon and Frans Coenen named isomorphism in 1992 — so a lawyer can sign it off. And a model can be driven against the type checker until its draft compiles and its tests pass.[^23] The first is a design goal that pilot participants have reported meeting, a report and not a measurement. The second is described in the paper and is not yet an automated pipeline; what exists is the checker and an authoring skill for a coding agent.[^18] A clause-for-clause encoding also inherits the text's amendments one at a time, and nobody here has measured what that costs.

Every third-bet number cited here — Rulemapping's 0.80 to 0.86, the paper's guided 1.00, our own cells — was reported by the people who built the system, on a benchmark they chose, and nobody publishes the encoding that failed to pay for itself. That includes us: nine items on one policy, from a branch that is not yet public.

The February paper's own data also cut against a careless version of the bet. Asked to write Prolog without a schema, six of the seven models did worse than when simply asked the question. Five had scored 0.78 with no variance, and their programs' "frequent ambiguities" pulled them under it, because the deterministic core evaluates whatever it is given, and a bad program is exactly as deterministic as a good one.[^1] Nine binary items, two of them contested, decided by a hundred trials, is a case study. The Stanford hallucination study is motivating context and no more: it has no encoding or solver condition, and only its thirty factual-recall queries have an answer a machine could check.[^10]

The academic version of the position is a paper in draft for ICAIL, the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Law. Its subject is constitutive rules — the ones that define what counts as what, a covered stay, a qualifying child, rather than commanding conduct — as typed total functions, with an evaluator whose trace is the explanation and the language model bounded to ingestion on one side and explanation on the other.[^24]

For a reader with a lease and a policy in a drawer, signed and never read closely, the offer is narrower than "ask the AI," and it is what the replication did fifty times over on one policy. The policy becomes a program you can put your own scenarios to. The dozen scenarios each party would name at negotiation and run on every redraft are Meng's proposal, in the working record, out of a New Zealand benefits calculator written in OpenFisca that had "only a handful of tests"; the post on that is planned and not written.[^24] The one judgment the program cannot make for you — was the punch an accident? — stands in a field where you can see it and argue about it, rather than buried in a sentence.
