**STATUS 2026-09-14: DRAFT — machine-drafted and persona-reviewed; not yet read by Meng. Citations checked live on 2026-09-14.**

Query five of nine reads, in full: "Assuming all other conditions are met and no other exclusions apply … will my policy apply if I was hospitalized for punching my own face to show off for my friends and I did not commit fraud or misrepresentation?" The policy is a page and a half. It is a hospital cash policy, the kind that pays a fixed sum for each day you spend in a ward, written for a fictional insurer and printed as an appendix to a paper that six authors, four of them at Stanford, posted to arXiv on 24 February 2025. The gold answer is "No." OpenAI's o1-preview — a reasoning model, one trained, in the words of its system card, "to reason using chain of thought" before it answers — was the best of the seven models the paper tested, and it got query five wrong in nine trials out of ten when asked cold, and wrong in nine of ten again when it was first asked to translate the policy into Prolog and put the question to the program.[^1]

A lawyer asked why would look for the clause that makes the cause of the hospital stay a condition of payment. In the policy as printed there is none. Clause 1.1 conditions payment on the policy being in effect "at the time of the hospitalization for sickness or accidental injury on which the claim for such benefit is premised"; clause 2.1 lists five exclusions, from skydiving to being eighty. The illustration the policy is adapted from, published by Oliver Goodenough and Preston Carlson in 2024 under an open license, has a section headed Benefits that says what is paid and for what. The printed version does not have that section, and two of its clauses still refer to a "Section 5 below" that is no longer below anything.[^2] The paper reads clause 1.1 as requiring that the stay result from sickness or accidental injury, and explains the misses by saying the cause of the claim is "addressed indirectly in the contract." A careful reader can hold either view. So the ninety-percent miss rate is not, on its own, a measurement of whether the model can reason. It is partly a measurement of whether a model — or a person — will supply a clause the text does not contain.

That is the terrain every serious approach to legal AI, meaning software that answers a legal question about a set of facts, has to work on: a text, a question, a gold answer that depends on a reading, and a score that looks like a measurement of reasoning. There are three bets on how to build the software. The claim here is that they are not rivals for the same square.

## The three bets

The first bet is that the model should be trained on law. In 2020 Ilias Chalkidis and colleagues released a family of smaller language models pre-trained on legal text and found that the usual recipes for adapting such models "do not always generalize well in the legal domain."[^3] In March 2024 a group led by Pierre Colombo released SaulLM-7B, built on Mistral 7B and trained on more than 30 billion tokens of English legal text, "the first LLM designed explicitly for legal text comprehension and generation."[^4] The commercial version of the bet is Harvey, which began a beta with OpenAI in November 2022 and by April 2024 was describing a custom case-law model co-developed under OpenAI's custom-model program: fine-tuning through the public interface and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) — fetching relevant documents into the prompt — had, on its account, not been enough; the custom model was trained on the equivalent of 10 billion tokens of case law, and lawyers at ten large firms preferred its output to GPT-4's 97 percent of the time [UNVERIFIED: OpenAI's case-study page rendered no readable text today; the figures are as reported by two secondary sources, note 5].[^5] Thirteen months later Harvey announced that "seven models" from three vendors "now outperform the originally benchmarked Harvey system" on its own benchmark, and added Anthropic's and Google's models to its platform.[^6] In August 2026 it swung back: Harvey Tenet, "a Kimi K3 base that we post-trained together with Fireworks research for long-horizon legal work," on an open-weight model from a Chinese lab.[^7]

The second bet is that the model is already good enough and the craft is in how it is used — the retrieval, the prompts, the tools it may call, and, since the autumn of 2024, how long it is allowed to think. OpenAI's o1 arrived in September 2024; DeepSeek-R1 in January 2025, trained, its authors say, by "pure reinforcement learning" with no human-written reasoning traces; Anthropic's Claude 3.7 Sonnet in February 2025, with an "extended thinking" mode whose budget a caller can set in tokens.[^8] The same Stanford group that printed the policy had already, in October 2024, reported that GPT-4o "fails to encode a simple health insurance contract into logical code" while o1-preview "succeeds."[^9] The bet has scoreboards. The Vals Legal AI Report of February 2025 scored four commercial tools against lawyers given two weeks to answer; Harvey's assistant topped five of the six tasks it entered, with 94.8 percent on document question-answering.[^10] By October 2025 Vals had a general chatbot matching three specialist tools on legal-research accuracy — 80 percent, against a lawyer baseline of 71 — though the three largest research vendors declined to be tested.[^10] The bet also has its critics, and they are the same group. Varun Magesh, Daniel Ho and colleagues at Stanford tested the research tools of LexisNexis and Thomson Reuters in 2024, after the vendors had described their products as "hallucination-free," and found each of them hallucinating — answering incorrectly, or citing a source that does not support the answer — between 17 and 33 percent of the time.[^11] Andrew Blair-Stanek and Benjamin Van Durme put 500 questions drawn from real cases to three leading models, twenty times each at temperature zero, and got contradictory verdicts on between 10.6 and 50.4 percent of them. Their reading of the flips: "In none of them was the legal analysis unsound. They simply weighed arguments or interests differently in different runs."[^12]

The third bet is that the answer should come from something that is not a model: a program, written from the rule, that a machine runs the same way every time, with the model doing the parts programs are bad at — reading the facts, drafting the program, explaining the result. Henry Kautz gave the family a taxonomy in 2020; the relevant pattern has the symbolic system in charge of the decomposition, delegating "narrowly scoped subroutines to neural modules," and the word for the family is neurosymbolic.[^13] In law the lineage is long. In 1986 an Imperial College group published a large part of the British Nationality Act 1981 as a Prolog program; Ken Satoh's Prolog-based system for Japanese civil cases (PROLEG) dates from 2011, Guido Governatori's Regorous compliance checker from 2013, Catala from 2021.[^14] The dataset that made the case in the machine-learning literature was Nils Holzenberger's 2020 statutory-reasoning benchmark on the US tax code, where reading models trained on text did poorly and a hand-written Prolog program solved the task outright; three years later the same group found GPT-3 leaning on remembered statutes, and "performing poorly" on simple synthetic ones it could not have seen.[^15] The LLM-writes-the-program version arrived in 2023 as Logic-LM, which reported an average 39.2-point gain over plain prompting on five logic benchmarks by having the model translate the problem and a solver do the inference.[^16] The February 2025 paper is that pattern applied to insurance, and its guided condition — the model given a fixed schema of facts to encode against — scored 1.00 for both GPT-4o and o1.[^1] Two arrivals in 2026 show the bet's spread. Oskar von Cossel, at the Max Planck Institute in Hamburg, benchmarked Rulemapping, a lawyer-drawn logic tree of and, or and not with a language model confined to the leaves, on 868 social-media posts under the German hate-speech statute: precision 0.80 to 0.86, against 0.34 to 0.49 for the same models prompted with prose, recall held.[^17] And in July 2026 the Axiom Foundation — Max Ghenis, Ariel Kennan and Pavel Makarchuk — launched with "over 3,000 provisions" of US tax and benefit law encoded by an AI pipeline, section by section, every value citing its statute, cross-checked against PolicyEngine and other engines, and compiled to Rust or WebAssembly; "The encoder logs every decision. Disagreements get explained, not erased."[^18]

L4 is in the third camp, and on a particular corner of it. Meng's phrase for the target, in the project's working record, is "a reasoner API to support traceable, audit-grade tool calling by LLM AIs": the model is the "right brain" that talks, the evaluator is the "left brain" that decides, and the model is not in the reasoning loop. A rule marked `@export` in an L4 file becomes a web endpoint and a tool under the Model Context Protocol (MCP), the standard through which assistants like Claude call external tools; an assistant that has the tool answers by running the rule and gets back a decision with its trace and citations attached.[^19] Axiom's verification is differential — its encodings are checked against engines that already exist — and L4's is deductive, in the type checker and in properties the encoding must satisfy; the two can be run on the same law and are not the same test.[^18]

## What the replication showed

The February paper prints no Prolog encoding of the policy, reports averages, and says in a footnote that encodings which "failed to run" were scored by "manually" reasoning through them.[^1] So we replicated it, in L4 and in Prolog, with encoders who had not seen the paper's results, and with a harness that fails an encoding that will not run instead of reading it by hand.[^20] Seven of the nine questions turned out to be mechanical — firefighter, seventy-eight years old, skydiving — and every encoding in every arm answered all seven correctly, 350 items out of 350 once the pilot and the ten-trial arms were pooled. All the variance sat on the two contested items, query five and query four, which never says when the hospitalization happened while clause 1.1 makes that the operative moment.

Query five is worth the walk. Both unguided encoders, in both languages, reading the policy as printed, built no field for the cause of the stay at all: coverage turned on the policy being in effect and no exclusion applying, which is what the printed text says. Given the paper's guided schema — the same list of facts to encode against, in both languages — the L4 encoder classified the self-inflicted punch as neither sickness nor accidental injury in nine trials of nine, and the Prolog encoder classified it as an accidental injury in eight of nine. Same schema, same rule, opposite fact. The expert vocabulary got both encoders to the point where the question could be asked; it did not decide the answer. The scores — 8 of 9 for most trials, 9 of 9 for two guided L4 trials — sit level with the paper's best unguided cell, and they are no evidence that L4 does better: the trial counts are tiny, language and encoder and protocol all changed at once, and one item in nine is eleven points, where the paper's own standard error runs up to eighteen. With the Benefits section restored, in an arm registered before any trial ran, unguided encoders got query five right in 14 of 20 trials instead of 5 of 20, and the plain-prompt condition rose from 1 in 10 to 5 in 10. No condition reached the ceiling on that question, even with the clause and the schema both supplied.[^20]

## Not the same square

Read the three bets against that instance. Whether a deliberate punch is an "accidental injury" is a question of vocabulary — what the word means in insurance — and vocabulary is what the first bet buys. Turning a paragraph of facts into a filled-in schema, and a page of policy into a first-draft program, is drafting under a budget, and drafting is what the second bet buys. Whether facts plus rule yield "No" is the inference, and the inference is the one layer where the third bet removes variance outright: 350 of 350. In the replication all of the remaining variance was in the first two layers. A deterministic core does nothing about them; it moves them to a place where they can be seen, one fact at a time, and argued about.

Rulemapping is the same stack, measured from outside our camp. Von Cossel's tree took an expert about three hours to draw and four to five more to refine against fifty pilot cases; the model at the leaves, under a curated context for each leaf, doubled precision and kept recall, and the named failure the tree prevents is "scope drift," a model mistaking a post it finds offensive for one that is illegal.[^17] The Stanford hallucination study points the same way, with the authors' own caveat attached. In its table of contributing causes, "reasoning error" — the documents were retrieved, they were relevant, and the tool still drew the wrong conclusion — is coded on 61 percent of Westlaw's hallucinations and 49 percent of Practical Law's, against 28 percent of Lexis's; but the causes were assigned by inspection, they overlap, and "Because these are closed systems, we are not able to clearly identify a single point of failure for each hallucination."[^11] That is suggestive, and it is not a controlled comparison of architectures.

Harvey's three years are the first two bets trading places on one layer. A custom model beat GPT-4 in 2024; seven general models beat the custom system within a year; a post-trained open-weight base is the 2026 answer. None of those moves touched the inference layer, because none of those systems has one.

## What the evidence does not show

Rich Sutton's essay of March 2019 is the standing objection to the third bet: "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin," and building in what we think we know tends to lose to search and learning over a long enough run.[^21] An encoding is human knowledge built in. The reply available here is that the encoding is the rule, not a heuristic about the rule — it does not become less true as models improve — and the reply has a cost the essay would recognize: von Cossel's seven or eight expert hours bought one paragraph of one section of one code. His own critique lands on languages like ours. Sentence-based systems such as PROLEG, he writes, require "legal knowledge to be authored in a programming formalism, reintroducing the knowledge-acquisition bottleneck that visualisation aimed to mitigate."[^17] L4 is a programming formalism. The answer on offer is that the encoding reads beside the source, clause for clause, so a lawyer can sign it off, and that a model can be driven against the type checker until its draft compiles and its tests pass. The first of those is a design goal with pilot evidence behind it; the second is described in the paper and not yet an automated pipeline in the tree — what exists is the checker and an authoring skill for a coding agent.[^19]

The February paper's own data also cut against a careless version of the bet. Asked to write Prolog without a schema, five of the seven models did worse than when simply asked the question — 0.78 with no variance became "frequent ambiguities" and lower scores — because the deterministic core evaluates whatever it is given, and a bad program is exactly as deterministic as a good one.[^1] The core moves the error; it does not remove it. Nor did restoring the missing clause settle query five: the interpretive fork survived formalization, and no condition reached the ceiling. Nine binary items, two of them contested, decided by fewer than a hundred trials, is a case study. The Stanford hallucination study is motivating context and no more; there is no encoding or solver condition in it, and only its thirty factual-recall queries have an answer a machine could check.[^11]

The academic version of the position is a paper in draft for ICAIL, the International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Law: constitutive rules as typed total functions, an evaluator whose trace is the explanation, and the language model bounded to ingestion on one side and explanation on the other.[^22] A post on the test suite — the dozen scenarios each party cares about, run on every redraft — is planned and not written.
