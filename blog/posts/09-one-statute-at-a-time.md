We put section 81C of Western Australia's Residential Tenancies Act 1987 in one window, and in the other the sixteen files that rewrite the Act as rules a machine can check and run. We read the section in the Western Australian compilation as at 29 April 2026.

The section lets "one, some or all of the parties" to a tenancy agreement apply to the bond administrator, in the approved form, to have the bond released. Subsection (2): the application "must state the amount of the security bond to be paid to each party." Subsection (3): it "must not be made before the residential tenancy agreement terminates," unless (4) the tenants have given up vacant possession and the parties have agreed in writing to end it.[^7]

Here is what the other window holds — `part-5a-security-bond-release.l4`, at line 27 and from line 31:[^7]

```
@ref Residential Tenancies Act 1987 (WA) s.81C
DECIDE `a security bond release application may be made` IF
        a's `the applicant is one, some or all of the parties to the residential tenancy agreement`
    AND a's `the application is in the approved form`
    AND a's `the application states the amount of the security bond to be paid to each party`
    AND (
            a's `the residential tenancy agreement has terminated`
        OR  `section 81C(4) applies` a
        OR  a's `section 81D applies`
        )
```

A question arrives inside a minute. Subsection (2) imposes a duty about what an application must contain; the encoding has made it a condition of whether an application may be made at all. Leave the amount out, and on one reading you have a defective application, on the encoding's reading no application at all — and which reading a tribunal takes decides real cases. An encoder who meets a sentence like that is told to record a fork: the site, both readings, the one taken provisionally, and why. Nothing here records one. The only register of readings in the whole corpus is a hand-written file under a different subject.[^6][^2]

No compiler settles that question. A tenancy lawyer would settle it in a sentence, and the corpus has a place for her to say so: a gate in the pipeline where a named person signs that the encoding says what the law says. The file listing the people whose signature carries a run past it is called `gate-allowed-signers`. It has held that role since the second of August and has never held a key. Every run that reaches the gate stops there, says why, and refuses to go on.[^1]

The corpus it guards is a public repository, `legalese/canon`. On 9 September 2026 it held 810 subjects — one statute each, or a self-contained piece of one. Three carry an encoding in L4, the language for law our group at Singapore Management University has been building for six years by our count: a Western Australian tenancies Act, the Singapore Penal Code, and a demonstration of the European Union's rules on strong customer authentication. The other 807 are source text and nothing more. All 810 sit on the lowest of three rungs, `draft` — "the encoding exists; no claim of fidelity is made." The two rungs above it — `adversarially-reviewed`, which machine checks can reach, and `reviewed`, which only a signature reaches — stand at zero and zero.[^2]

Meng Weng Wong, the principal research fellow who runs the project, put the ambition on 14 September as "like Wikipedia but a Rule-ipedia": the world's laws, one statute at a time, by volunteers who run the pipeline and lawyers who sit the review.[^4] Writing a statute as a logic program is forty years old — Sergot, Sadri, Kowalski and three colleagues published the British Nationality Act that way in 1986.[^3] Nobody has ever been asked to sign one.

## What a signer signs

The pipeline is one line. In a Claude Code session opened on the repository you type the name of a body of law and the word `go` — `SEC Regulation Crowdfunding: go` is the historical example. A ten-stage driver takes it from there: source text, an encoding, a register of the places where the text admits two readings, an adversarial review, tests, seven projections — the same rules re-emitted in other formats — and a report.[^5] Between the review and the tests sits the gate: HG1, human gate one. That judgment has no oracle. The pipeline's own instructions call completeness of the ambiguity register "unfalsifiable in principle."[^5]

A reviewer signs on their own computer, with a key that never enters our machines. What they sign is a fingerprint of the exact files they stand behind, with the run, the commit and the clock; change a character in any of them and the signature stops matching. A signature is evidence of key custody and not of attention: nothing stops a volunteer having a model do the comparison and signing the output. The design enforces one thing — agents can verify signatures and cannot make them, because the orchestrator holds no key it was not given — and the rest is a person taking responsibility under their own name. One resume path still lets a signature stand for files it never covered, a hole our notes call worth knowing rather than discovering.[^6]

What makes the side-by-side read possible is the eighty `@ref` lines running through the sixteen tenancies files, each naming the provision the rule beside it comes from.[^7] After the reading, one command writes the request, one signs it, and the next run passes the gate instead of exiting at it. How long the reading takes nobody knows: no one has done it once. [NEEDS MENG: who the first signer will be, or whether it is Meng.]

The signature claims little. It "never claims the law is good, or that the encoding is bug-free — machine gates own mechanical correctness. It claims one precise thing: a named expert read this against its source and stands behind the correspondence."[^6] Its source is one compilation on one date. Western Australia reprints continuously and the section on display was inserted in 2024, so a signature has to name the version as well as the files.

That is a statement about two texts, and not advice to anyone about their situation — though whether that characterization holds is one of the open questions below. One thing makes the question live: the reservation, the acts only licensed lawyers may perform. Section 33 of Singapore's Legal Profession Act 1966 lists the acts an unauthorised person may not do — an unauthorised person being one not on the roll with a "practising certificate." One of them is to draw or prepare "any document or instrument relating to any movable or immovable property or to any legal proceeding." Doing it is an offence unless the doer proves the act "was not done for or in expectation of any fee, gain or reward." On our own policy note's reading — a sketch, and not an opinion on anyone's exposure — a volunteer has two answers: no fee was taken, and reading a text against a text is not on the list.[^8]

## Nupedia's twenty-four articles

Nupedia went online on 9 March 2000 with a seven-step review. By the time its server crashed on 26 September 2003 it had approved 24 articles. Its side project, which let anyone edit and reviewed afterward, launched on 15 January 2001; on the day of reading it listed 7,239,498 English articles and 260,720 contributors active in the past month.[^9]

HG1 is a gate of Nupedia's shape, and the design's answer is that the gate governs the label and not the publication. An encoding lands in `canon` as `draft`, public from that moment, and reaches `adversarially-reviewed` when the machine gates pass and whoever drove the run has checked their own encoding — which is why that pass is not the gate. It becomes `reviewed` only on a signature: "a named domain expert has signed HG1: the encoding is isomorphic to its source."[^2] Isomorphic is Bench-Capon and Coenen's word, from 1992, for "the well defined correspondence of the knowledge base to the source texts": one rule traceable to one provision. An encoding can be traceable throughout and wrong throughout, so the signature adds the judgment that the traceable rule says what the provision says.[^10]

A statute has one thing an encyclopedia entry lacks: a referent that can be pinned. Fix the compilation and the date, and encoder and reviewer are reading the same words, so a disagreement localizes to a sentence. But reading is not the whole job. Meaning is settled by construction — how a court reads the words — and by authority — how courts have read them before. Which is why a register of competing readings comes out of the pipeline as an ordinary output, and why a person sits at the gate.

## Why a lawyer would spend a weekend on it

Outsiders already review legal text for the people who publish it: in The National Archives' "expert participation programme," described in 2014, "everyone's work is double checked by reviewers" before publication on legislation.gov.uk.[^12] The text itself has been free for a generation.[^13] Encoded law is already sold, and already given away — AustLII has published executable legal rule bases since the 1990s.[^14][^15]

Public, open-licensed and signed does not mark the difference either: OpenFisca's country packages are public, open-licensed and reviewed through pull requests, and the Axiom Foundation's UK benchmark board runs against a signed corpus release.[^20][^28] A release signature says a build is the one the publisher shipped; a merge approval says a maintainer accepted a diff. HG1 says what neither says: a named domain expert read this encoding against its source statute and stands behind the correspondence. That is the seat we can find nobody sitting in.

Firms sell hours. Lawyers at small US firms on Clio's platform posted an average rate of $341 an hour in 2024, by Clio's index as a secondary page reports it — what they ask, not what they are paid.[^16] The nearest measured encoding cost is in another formalism: Oskar von Cossel's Rulemapping tree for § 130(1) no. 1 of the German Criminal Code took "approximately three hours" of expert work to build and "an additional four to five hours" to refine against about fifty pilot cases.[^17] Eight hours for one sub-number of one paragraph, built from scratch — a different job from reading an encoding that already exists, and no bound on that one.

Meng's estimate for the machine's share of a whole statute is about $20 on a Claude Code subscription, research included — his calculation, and not a measurement. The cost stage records tokens and refuses by rule to print money, and [NEEDS MENG: what the $20 counts (tokens at API list price, a share of a month, the whole subscription), and the token count of one real run].[^18] Under the estimate sits a conjecture that models write L4 without much coaching, and nothing we have run measures it: post 2's replication ran a Prolog arm and an L4 arm, but it changed language, encoder and protocol at once, and its L4 encoders got a block of language gotchas the record flags as treatment-relevant.[^26] The reviewer's hours stay expensive, and the design refuses to remove them.

Those hours are one instrument among several. Post 4 logged twelve well-typed wrong meanings — rules the compiler accepted that said something other than the law does — in six weeks in one corpus. One was caught by reading the section against the file, the HG1 method. The other eleven needed a comparison of two independent encodings of the same rules and a reading of the compiler's own source, and a tenancy lawyer in Perth has neither.

What does a reviewer get for the hours? The briefing's answer is a side practice: a small firm reviews an encoding of the law it knows best — "a niche of landlord-tenant, a state's licensing regime, a corner of financial regulation" — and signs it. Its name goes on the public record beside the encoding, "a durable, checkable claim of expertise, doing something useful for people who are not yet clients." If the law you know best is a niche of that kind, that is the seat on offer. The same paragraph concedes: "We are not there yet."[^6] With no key in the signers file, the only route through the gate today is a recorded waiver, and the signature branch has never run end to end.

## Where a signed encoding goes

An encoding is worth something to somebody who will never open one. A rule marked `@export` becomes an endpoint a program can call, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) tool an assistant can call, and — with one `<script>` tag — a tool a visitor's own browser agent can call on the page.[^23] Deploy the tenancies encoding that way and a tenant's assistant can ask whether a bond-release application can be made yet. Today it gets a decision and nothing else: the tool returns no trace and no citations, because its handler calls the evaluator with tracing off.[^23] That tenant is most of us — we sign leases and policies we do not read, and take the other side's word for what they say. A signature puts a section number and a named reader behind the answer, once the transport carries the trace.

That is the second thing the call asks for. People building legal software want a core that returns the same answer every time and can show its working, so the model in their product calls it instead of guessing — the symbolic half of what post 2 calls a neurosymbolic stack, the half that decides while the model reads and talks.[^19] Models write these encodings now: the Axiom Foundation's pipeline has published over 3,000 provisions of law in its own format.[^20] What a compiler and a gate add is a refusal of ill-typed rules, a signature a model can verify and cannot make, and a record of both. Then L4 compiles out, because "it is rarely where the rule needs to *live*": the benefits agency already runs OpenFisca.[^21]

Meng's positioning for that inventory is L4 as the spiritual heir of LegalRuleML, "a lingua franca for other rules-as-code languages." The metadata layer of that standard worked; it never got an execution semantics, so nothing reasons over it directly and every consumer transcoded out. An execution semantics is what L4 puts under the hub. The field's own live languages for writing norms — eFLINT, Symboleo, Stipula — are not on that backlog.[^22][^24][^25]

## What the call does not have

Nobody has signed. The signers file holds no key, the corpus holds no grants, no subject stands above `draft`, and the first signature will be the first time `reviewed` means anything there.[^1]

A statute nobody has encoded still needs an encoder. A `go` on a subject that has one replays it through the projections; on new law the de novo stages "*validate* an encoding deposited by an agent," and producing that deposit "is still agent work, with you judging it."[^6] That is what the 807 unencoded subjects are: Western Australian Acts with their text fetched, their descriptors written, and nothing else.[^2]

Where the argument wants the registers thick, they are thin. Post 5 named the fork register as the instrument that would one day count how often formalization meets a choice the text left open. On 9 September `canon` held no machine-readable fork register at all — one hand-written register of ten entries, and four such registers in the language repository, covering two subjects.[^2]

The evidence has a numerator and no denominator. Four findings run through the earlier posts, each of them out of the work of writing a law down precisely enough for a machine to check it. A double bind in a data-protection statute, found in a hand-built model of one fragment by walking a machine through every state that model can reach, and already ameliorated by the guidance published alongside the rules. A payout formula that admitted two readings. A rebate that went the wrong way once income crossed a threshold, which a maintainer found the same week by reading the code, independently of us. And the twelve wrong meanings above. Nobody here has counted the encodings that found nothing. Jim Woodcock and three colleagues, surveying sixty-two industrial projects of this kind in a 2009 paper, warned that people who saw no value in the method would be "under-represented among the responses"; that a formalization finding nothing also goes unwritten is our impression, and not their measurement. Four findings are four anecdotes, and the fork counts that would give the rate are empty.[^27]

The critics of the position hold. Mireille Hildebrandt in 2020: "What code-driven law does is to fold enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke, collapsing the distance between legislator, executive and court" — the distance that lets someone on the wrong end of a ministry's reading argue for a court's. Tom Barraclough, Hamish Fraser and Curtis Barnes, to the New Zealand Law Foundation in 2021: rules written in code "should never be given the status of legislation," and "coded models are better understood as a an interpretation of what the law requires" (the doubled article is theirs).[^29] A corpus whose every entry is `draft` until a person signs, whose top status claims only that an encoding is "isomorphic to its source," and whose registers record the readings not taken, agrees with the second and keeps the distance the first is worried about. It answers nothing about what happens once an encoding is the thing people consult. The governments doing this at scale use their own staff and instruments; a drafting office has explored L4 for future drafting, but that is Meng's account and one sentence in our ICAIL paper, and we have no record of an adoption.[^30]

Three legal questions are open, and the project has put none of them to a lawyer. Whether a service that takes a member of the public's facts and returns what a statute requires falls inside the reservation — our policy note found that unlitigated in Singapore, and routed around rather than decided in the United States.[^8] What a signer owes a stranger who relies on a public encoding that turns out to be wrong. Whether a firm's public signature engages the profession's rules on publicity, and what it does when that firm acts for one side of the statute it has signed.[^8]

Then there is maintenance, the failure mode this field has had more than once. The wave after the British Nationality Act paper died of it. Capper and Susskind published the Latent Damage System, the United Kingdom's first commercial legal expert system, in 1988. The Alvey social-security demonstrator, for which Bench-Capon, Robinson, Routen and Sergot formalized supplementary benefit legislation at scale, dates from 1987.[^11] Both are said to have gone stale against amendments nobody was paid to track — the field's received account, which we have not established from either source. Nearer to now, OpenFisca's history records that by 2022 "usage grew in several countries without clear contributions to an increasingly outdated engine," and that civil-society modeling of British and then United States legislation led in 2021 to PolicyEngine — a fork.[^28] An encoding inherits every amendment to its statute, and nobody here has measured what that costs. Greenleaf, Mowbray and Chung wrote in 2018 on sustaining free legal advisory systems; we cite it unread, from behind two walls, and it is where a reader should go first.[^14]

If you want the evidence behind the call, it is in the earlier posts: the double bind and the payout formula in post 1, the compiler's defect log in post 4, the fork register in post 5, the missing tests in post 6. The nearest paper facet is a design note on the political economy of legal legibility, prefigured by the standalone post "Seeing Like a Citizen."[^31]

On the day the first key lands, the signers file stops being deliberately empty — "a small ceremony we have been looking forward to," the briefing calls it.[^6] The encoding it blesses will be one statute, one jurisdiction, one reader's name beside it. Somebody will have had both windows open, the way we did, and read the section against the rules beside it. Then a run reaches the gate and does not stop.
