**STATUS 2026-09-14: INITIAL DRAFT — machine-drafted; not yet persona-reviewed, fact-checked or read by Meng. Citations checked live by the drafter on 2026-09-14.**

There is a file in our repository called `gate-allowed-signers`. It is the list of people whose signature the pipeline will accept as proof that a human being has read an encoding of a law against the law and is prepared to put their name beside it. It has held that role since the first of August and it has never held a key. Every run of the pipeline that reaches the gate stops there, prints why, and exits with status 3.[^1]

The corpus the gate guards is a public repository, `legalese/canon`. On 9 September 2026 it held 810 subjects. Of those, 808 are Western Australian Acts pulled as source text, each with a descriptor and nothing else: a scaffold, and the repository's own header calls it one. Three carry an encoding in L4, the language for law our group at Singapore Management University has been building for six years: the Residential Tenancies Act 1987 of Western Australia, sixteen files at version 0.6.0; the Singapore Penal Code 1871, fourteen files at version 0.1.0, covering the chapters an AI agent is most likely to walk into and not the other five hundred sections; and a demonstration encoding of the European Union's rules on strong customer authentication, eight files, with a hand-written register of ten places where the text admitted more than one reading. Every one of the 810 declares the status `draft`, which the corpus defines as "the encoding exists; no claim of fidelity is made." There are no signatures because nobody has been asked to sign.[^2]

That is the call. Meng's phrase for it, in the note that commissioned this post, is "like Wikipedia but a Rule-ipedia": an encoding of the world's laws, one statute at a time, by volunteers who run the pipeline and by lawyers who sit the review. What follows is what the seat is, why anyone would take it, and what the flywheel does not yet have.

## The seat

The pipeline is one line. In a Claude Code session opened on the repository you type the name of a body of law and the word `go` — "SEC Regulation Crowdfunding: go" is the historical example — and a skill dispatches a ten-stage driver that takes the text from source through an encoding, an ambiguity register, tests, an adversarial review, seven projections and a report.[^3] Between the adversarial review and the tests sits the gate, HG1, human gate one, and it is the one judgment the design forbids a machine to make: whether the encoding says what the law says. The briefing for reviewers puts it in one sentence — "agents can verify signatures and cannot make them."[^4]

Mechanically, HG1 is a detached signature. The driver writes a payload naming the run, the commit, the clock, and the digest of every file in the encoding; the reviewer signs it with `ssh-keygen -Y sign` on a machine the pipeline never touches; anyone can run the matching verify against the public key. Edit a file after the signature and the digest moves, so the signature stops verifying: sign-off binds to content, not to a moment. A reviewer who is still learning may waive the gate instead, on the record, with a reason, and the report prints the waiver in its header. The second gate, which guards anything outward-facing, cannot be waived at all.[^4]

What the signature claims is narrow. In the briefing's words, it "never claims the law is good, or that the encoding is bug-free — machine gates own mechanical correctness. It claims one precise thing: a named expert read this against its source and stands behind the correspondence."[^4] It is not advice to anyone about anything, which matters, because in Singapore the Legal Profession Act reserves a list of acts to people with a practicing certificate — drawing documents relating to property, preparing probate papers, acting as an advocate — and none of them is reading a text against a text and signing a hash. Whether *running* a signed encoding against a member of the public's facts is one of the reserved acts is a question the Act never anticipated and no court here has decided; a working note in our repository walks the statute's sections and finds one carve-out that fits a machine that "renders without choosing," and a power in section 34(2) for the Minister to exempt a class of persons by rule, apparently never used. That note is a policy sketch that has not read its 1988 authority in the original, and says so.[^5] The signature stays on the safe side of the line by claiming nothing about a person.

## Why "Rule-ipedia," and why the closer precedent is a map

Wikipedia is the word people reach for, and its history contains the warning. Nupedia went online on 9 March 2000 with a seven-step review — assignment, lead reviewer, lead review, open review, two rounds of copyediting, final approval — and by the time its server crashed on 26 September 2003 it had approved 24 articles, with 150 more in progress. Its side project, which let anyone edit and reviewed afterward, launched on 15 January 2001 and today lists 7,239,498 English articles and 260,720 contributors active in the past month.[^6] A gate that every article must pass before it exists is what killed Nupedia. HG1 is a gate of that shape, and the design knows it: the gate governs the *label*, not the publication. An encoding lands in `canon` as `draft`, is promoted to `adversarially-reviewed` when the machine gates and an independent adversarial pass are done, and becomes `reviewed` only on a signature.[^2] The 810 drafts are already public. What the signature adds is the one word a court or an agency would need before it relied on the file.

Wikipedia's other mechanisms map more directly than its name does. Its talk page exists "to provide space for editors to discuss editing that page"; its software "makes it easy to reverse errors, and experienced editors watch and patrol bad edits."[^6] The pipeline's equivalents are the fork register and git. When an encoder meets a sentence with two readings, the instruction is not to guess but to record a fork — the site, the readings, the one taken, and why — and the reviewer's job is to rule between them, cite the authority, and demote the losing reading rather than delete it.[^4] That is a talk page with a decision at the bottom, and a revision history in which each ruling is a diff. As for accuracy: the *Nature* study of 2005 compared 42 science articles and counted 162 errors in Wikipedia against 123 in *Britannica*, four serious in each; *Britannica* called the study "fatally flawed."[^6] A statute has a property no encyclopedia entry has, which is that the thing it describes is itself a text, so a reviewer can check correspondence line by line rather than argue about the world.

That property is why OpenStreetMap (OSM) is the better model. Steve Coast founded it in 2004 while at University College London; its data is under the Open Database License; its contributors — 2.25 million distinct ones in the second quarter of 2025, ten billion nodes by that August — survey, trace imagery, and import free data; and its rule for disputes is that "whatever name, designation, etc are used by the people on the ground at that location are used," with a Data Working Group to mediate when two mappers cannot agree.[^7] The ground is the oracle. A law's ground is its source text, and the pipeline's first stage pulls that text and its provenance into a bundle beside the encoding so the oracle travels with the claim.[^3] The lawyers with specialties whom Meng wants ruling between forks are the Data Working Group. The volunteer with a subscription and a weekend is the mapper with a bicycle.

## Why a lawyer would spend a weekend on it

The precedent for volunteer experts curating law is not a startup. In January 2014 John Sheridan of The National Archives wrote that Parliament makes "on average, around 15,000 changes to other pieces of legislation a year," that the Archives had an "expert participation programme" sharing "responsibility for curating and managing the Government's legislation database with others, inside and outside Government," and that "everyone's work is double checked by reviewers before it is published on legislation.gov.uk."[^8] Two decades earlier, in 1992, Peter Martin and Tom Bruce had put the Legal Information Institute (LII) online at Cornell, the first law site on the internet, which now reports over 40 million unique visitors a year; Carl Malamud's Public.Resource.Org, whose banner still reads "Making Government Information More Accessible," took Georgia to the Supreme Court over the state's annotated code and won, 6–3, on 27 April 2020: "The OCGA annotations are ineligible for copyright protection," because "judges and legislators may not be considered the 'authors' of the works they produce in the course of their official duties."[^9] Those projects freed the text. The encodings are the next layer up, and they are the layer that is still paid for one client at a time.

What a firm charges for the equivalent of an encoded statute is not a number anyone publishes, because nobody sells that product; what they sell is the hours. The average small-firm lawyer in the United States billed $341 an hour in 2024 by Clio's index.[^10] The nearest measured encoding cost is from outside our project: Oskar von Cossel's Rulemapping tree for one paragraph of the German hate-speech statute took "approximately three hours" of expert work to build and "an additional four to five hours" to refine against fifty pilot cases.[^11] Eight hours at that rate is a little under $3,000 for one section, before the tooling. Meng's estimate for the machine's share of a whole statute — his calculation, not a measurement; nothing in the repository records a per-statute cost — is about $20 on a Claude Code subscription, research included.[^12] The reviewer's hours are the part that stays expensive, and the part the design refuses to remove.

So the question is what the reviewer gets for them. The briefing's answer is a side practice: a small firm commissions or reviews an encoding of the law it already knows best — "a niche of landlord-tenant, a state's licensing regime, a corner of financial regulation" — signs it, and puts its name on the public record beside it, which the briefing calls "a durable, checkable claim of expertise, doing something useful for people who are not yet clients," and then concedes, in the same paragraph, "We are not there yet."[^4] The mechanism that would make the credential worth having — a signature anyone can verify, bound to content, that nothing about the gate requires the signer to work for us — is built. The market that would value it is not. That is the flywheel's first missing part, and it is listed below with the others.

## What a founder gets

The second call is to the people building the next generation of legal technology, and it is an infrastructure pitch: the symbolic half of a neurosymbolic stack, a deterministic core that a language model can call instead of guess.[^13] Large language models write these encodings now. The Axiom Foundation's pipeline reads a statute section by section and has published over 3,000 provisions of US tax and benefit law in its RuleSpec format; its EncodeBench, formalized in July 2026, scores a model on whether an encoding compiles, passes the repository's tests, and contains "zero ungrounded numeric literals: every number in the encoding appears in the source text," across sixteen United Kingdom cases and six models, with the caveat on its own page that "this run is not reasoning-effort matched."[^14] What we add to a model that writes rules is a compiler that refuses ill-typed ones, a gate that no model can pass, and a corpus in which each of those refusals and signatures is on the record.

An L4 file is a poor place for a rule to live, and that is by design. The page that introduces the exports says it: "L4 is where a rule is written down once, precisely. It is rarely where the rule needs to *live*." The benefits agency already runs OpenFisca; the legal-aid charity already publishes docassemble interviews; the bank's analysts already open DMN tables. So L4 compiles out, under two rules — each export is a compiler over a stated subset rather than a converter, and each "would rather refuse than lie."[^15] Here is what compiles out as of 14 September 2026, read from the binary's own help text and the specifications named in the notes. The deployment row is the one a founder would use first. A rule marked `@export` in an L4 file becomes a REST endpoint on a `jl4-service`, either self-hosted or on Legalese Cloud, with a query-plan endpoint that says which inputs still matter; the same deployment is an MCP server that an assistant calls as a typed tool; and a one-line `<script>` tag registers the deployed rules as WebMCP tools on any web page, so a visitor's browser agent can run an eligibility check without scraping the site.[^17] One limit belongs in the body rather than a note: today the HTTP endpoint returns the full evaluation trace on request, and the MCP tool returns the decision alone, with no trace and no citations — the handler calls the evaluator with tracing off.[^17] As `canon` fills, each subject that reaches `reviewed` is a rule set a service can be stood up on; the encodings are Apache-2.0 and the quoted legal text carries whatever terms its jurisdiction attaches.[^2]

Meng's positioning for the interchange rows is that L4 is the spiritual heir of LegalRuleML: a lingua franca for the other rules-as-code languages, with a backlog of exporters still to build.[^18] LegalRuleML is real — an OASIS Standard since 30 August 2021, edited by Palmirani, Governatori, Athan, Boley, Paschke and Wyner — and it was designed as exactly the hub Meng describes. It is also, by its own §2.3, "independent from any legal ontology and logic framework," which is to say it fixes no meaning for its own operators; our research memo of 16 August found every reasoner that ever consumed it dead or unreachable, its technical committee's repository last pushed on 19 July 2020, and its own community transcoding to SPINdle to run anything. Both of those facts held again today.[^18] What the standard got right is its metadata: `LegalSource` and `LegalReference` following Akoma Ntoso's naming, `TemporalCharacteristic` for in-force intervals, and `Alternatives` for competing readings. Those three map onto L4's `@ref` provenance, its `EVAL … UNDER RULES EFFECTIVE AT` clause, and the fork register so closely that the memo calls the match "independent confirmation that L4's annotation layer is designed correctly."[^18] So the heir claim is this: L4 takes the hub role LegalRuleML was built for and adds what the standard never had, an execution semantics and working compilers out. The debt runs the other way too. A LegalRuleML *exporter* is on the backlog, and the memo's verdict is that it would be a publication-and-provenance artifact rather than an interoperability bridge — roughly 60 percent mechanical, with deadlines, success continuations, parallel composition and traces dropped with a diagnostic because the standard has no place to put them.[^18] No claim of compatibility, round-tripping, or endorsement is made. Akoma Ntoso, the document standard LegalRuleML's references follow — adopted by the Italian Senate in March 2013, an OASIS Standard since 29 August 2018, the base of Brazil's LexML — is already an output: `l4 render --format akn` emits a LegalDocML document today, and the pipeline's own phase script notes that the format is absent from the command's help text and that well-formedness is the only oracle it has.[^19]

## Why the pipeline is cheap, as a conjecture

The $20 rests on an observation that models write L4 without much coaching, and the tidiest statement of why comes from a model. Meng put the question to ChatGPT on 14 September, and its last turn argued that L4 asks a model for several small translations between representations it already knows — English rule to controlled natural language to deontic structure to typed functions to Haskell-shaped machinery — rather than one leap; that the keywords carry their own semantics, so "a person MUST pay the fee" is "already almost its own semantic annotation" where `>>=` is not; and so "language understanding supplies the intended meaning; programming-language knowledge supplies the executable structure," which makes L4 "almost perversely well matched to the medium."[^20] It is a hypothesis, offered by a model about models. Our one measurement is the replication in post 2 — one insurance policy, nine questions, one model alias, scores from 0.900 to 0.967 across five cells — which compared no languages, and whose L4 encoders received a page of L4-specific cautions that the record flags as a treatment the Prolog encoders did not get. So what our record shows is a page of coaching, not none.[^20] The test the claim would have to pass has a shape now: EncodeBench's four gates. L4 has not sat it.[^14]

## What the flywheel does not have

Nobody has signed. The signers file is empty by design, the corpus holds zero `gates/` directories, and the first signature will be the first time the word `reviewed` means anything.[^1] Until then the credential a lawyer would be spending a weekend for is a description of a mechanism.

A statute nobody has encoded is not push-button. The briefing says so in its last section: a `go` on a subject with a committed encoding replays it through the projections and writes the report; on a new body of law the de novo stages "*validate* an encoding deposited by an agent," and producing the deposit "is still agent work, with you judging it."[^4] The 808 Western Australian Acts are that half: text fetched, descriptors written, encodings absent.[^2]

The corpus's own registers are thin where the argument needs them thick. Post 5 named the fork register as the instrument that would one day count how often formalization meets a choice the text left open; on 9 September there was no machine-readable fork register anywhere in `canon`, one hand-written ambiguity register of ten entries, and the four registers that exist are in the language repository, for two subjects.[^2]

Maintenance is the failure mode the field has already had. OpenFisca began in May 2011 inside a French policy unit and now lists models for eleven jurisdictions, from France and Tunisia to Barcelona, Aotearoa New Zealand and the United States; its own history records that by 2022 "usage grew in several countries without clear contributions to an increasingly outdated engine," that a minimal core team survived on a French agency's funding, and that in 2021 "members of civil society started modelling British and then USA legislation, leading to the creation of PolicyEngine" — a fork.[^21] Blawx, Jason Morris's visual rules tool and one of the things we compile to, describes itself as "functional, but it is not production-quality software," "intended for educational and experimental purposes."[^21] The open-source commons that the phrase "flywheel" borrows from is large — a 2024 Harvard Business School working paper puts the supply-side value of widely used open-source software at $4.15 billion and the demand-side value, what firms would spend to rewrite it, at $8.8 trillion — and it is also, as Debian says of itself, "an all-volunteer organization," with the maintenance problems that come with the label.[^22] An encoding inherits every amendment to its statute, one at a time, and nobody here has measured what that costs.

The critics of the position hold. Mireille Hildebrandt in 2020: "What code-driven law does is to fold enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke, collapsing the distance between legislator, executive and court." Tom Barraclough, Hamish Fraser and Curtis Barnes, reporting to the New Zealand Law Foundation in 2021: rules written in code "should never be given the status of legislation," and "coded models are better understood as an interpretation of what the law requires."[^23] A corpus whose every entry is labeled `draft` until a named person signs, whose status vocabulary tops out at "isomorphic to its source" rather than "is the law," and whose forks record the readings not taken, is built to agree with the second and to keep the distance the first is worried about. It does not answer the objection to what happens once an encoding is the thing consulted. A drafting office has looked at L4; none has adopted it; and the governments doing this at scale — Australia's GovCMS ran rules-as-code trials for its agencies through 2024–25, and a Think 7 brief to the G7 in April 2025, which Meng co-authored, asked for a regulatory innovation task force and an open-source, consistent approach in place of vendor-driven ones — are doing it on their own instruments, with their own staff, for their own services.[^24] A volunteer corpus of another jurisdiction's law is a different thing from a ministry encoding its own rules, and the difference is who answers for a wrong answer.

And the unauthorized-practice question is open in the direction that matters. The signature is safe; a service that takes a member of the public's facts and returns what the statute requires is the act the profession's reservation is closest to, and the note that studied it found the question unlitigated in Singapore and routed around rather than decided in the United States.[^5] The design keeps the trace so the answer can be checked, and today the MCP transport drops the trace.[^17]

The academic version of the argument is a design note toward a paper on the political economy of legal legibility — working title "Seeing Like a Citizen" — which treats readable, checkable law as civic infrastructure and the reservation of its application to a profession as one enclosure among several; the unauthorized-practice note above is a sidebar to it.[^25] The posts before this one supplied the evidence: a race condition in a statute, a payout formula with two readings, a rebate that went the wrong way, a verifier that says what it did not look at, a test suite that is missing.

On the day the first key lands, the signers file stops being deliberately empty, which the briefing calls "a small ceremony we have been looking forward to."[^4] Then a run reaches the gate and does not stop. The encoding it blesses will be one statute, one jurisdiction, one reader's name beside it. That is the unit. Everything above is an argument that the unit is worth a weekend, and the repository is where the argument is checked.
