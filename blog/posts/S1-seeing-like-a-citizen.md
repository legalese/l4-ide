There is a web page, still online today, that asks a compliance officer one question at a time — "notifiable data breach?" and "notify?" are two of the strings in its code — and tells them what their organization has to do, each answer citing the clause it came from. The page dates from 2022. That year a government agency, which stays unnamed, asked us to encode the part of Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) that says what an organization must do when it discovers a data breach.[^1] We rewrote it clause by clause in L4, the language our group at Singapore Management University has been building for six years, so that a machine could run it, and I remember the page as generated from that encoding. Whether the 2022 toolchain emitted it, or someone built it beside the encoding by hand, is on no record we have opened. [NEEDS MENG: which it was, and whether any usage figures exist.]

The same engagement had a verification side. Once we understood the fragment we modeled it by hand in UPPAAL, a model checker for processes with clocks. The Act, as the model reads it, gives the organization up to thirty days to assess whether a breach is notifiable, then three days to tell the Commission, and the same three days to tell the affected individuals; the Commission may direct the organization not to tell the individuals. The checker found a state in which the individuals have been told and the Commission has prohibited telling them. Two duties that cannot both be obeyed at the wrong moment: a race condition. The one path that seems to satisfy both — tell the Commission, then stall on the individuals until a direction arrives — breaches the three-day requirement in the interim, which is why a static reading misses it and a timed model finds it. The finding is sound for that model, which sequentialized the notifications by a declared modeling choice, and it was found from the text of the rules alone; the guidance that accompanies the Act documents the bind and ameliorates it in practice, so it is the reader without the guidance who is caught. The first post in this series prints the trace.[^2]

The year after, an insurer with millions of customers, also unnamed, gave us one of its policies to encode.[^3] The rewriting does not let a clause stay vague, and the payout formula would not go in as written: two readings of it gave two amounts. [NEEDS MENG: the shape of the ambiguity — one sentence of arithmetic, names filed off.] The insurer went back over what its adjusters had been doing and found they had been paying the larger. We presented to management, and what we talked about was the liability an ambiguous consumer term carries — framed that way partly so that the adjusters who had helped us were not exposed as overpayers. Management asked about liability back, and never about which amount to pay next. The fix went into the wording, at the level of the logic. The presentation is lost, and I am working from memory.[^3]

Who in that transaction could have read the policy and known what it paid? Not the policyholder, who had signed it. Not the adjusters, who were paying one reading without knowing there was another. Not, until the rewriting forced the question, the insurer that had written it. The document had been applied claim after claim and was illegible to every party it bound. An encoding that had to choose was what put the choice in front of them.

In both cases the person bound could not read what bound them, while the other side could read a great deal about the person. James C. Scott's _Seeing Like a State_ (1998) is the account of how that second half came to be: permanent surnames, so that an official who had never met you could tell you from your neighbor; land surveys that turned a village's overlapping claims into parcels with one taxable owner each; the census. Scott called the property the state was after _legibility_, a population an official can read, and later called state-making "the conquest of illegibility."[^4] His legibility runs one way, from the official down to the subject. Whether the subject can read the rules that bind them is not the book's question. The law's own moral claim depends on it: _ignorantia juris non excusat_, ignorance of the law is no excuse, only makes sense if the law can be known by the person it binds.

In 2013 Carl Malamud's nonprofit, Public.Resource.Org, put the Official Code of Georgia Annotated on the internet. Georgia sued in 2015, claiming copyright in the annotations to its own code, which a LexisNexis division had drafted under contract for the state's Code Revision Commission, and lost in the Supreme Court on April 27, 2020, five votes to four. Chief Justice Roberts wrote that the Commission was an arm of the legislature, and that what a legislature authors in the course of its legislative duties cannot be copyrighted.[^5] The free-access-to-law movement Malamud works in began with Cornell's Legal Information Institute in 1992, and its Montreal declaration of 2002 calls public legal information "part of the common heritage of humanity."[^5] A code you may download is still a code you cannot run.

## How big the gap is

The World Justice Project (WJP) ran legal-needs surveys in 102 countries and published the totals in 2019. Thirty-six percent of people had experienced at least one justiciable problem in the past two years — a problem the law could do something about, from debt to housing to family. Of those with a civil or administrative problem, 49 percent could not meet the need it created, about 1.4 billion people summed over the world.[^6] In the United States the Legal Services Corporation (LSC) reported in 2022 that low-income households got no legal help, or not enough, for 92 percent of the civil legal problems that had a substantial impact on them.[^7]

Those figures count problems a lawyer would recognize. Rebecca Sandefur, the sociologist who has done most to measure this, wrote in _Dædalus_ in 2019: "People do not consider law as a solution for their justice problems; they do not think of their problems as being 'legal'." The most common reason people give for not going to a lawyer is something other than the cost.[^8] My own shorthand for this is the man on the street who is not quite sure what his legal situation is, under the law or under a handful of insurance policies he signed without really understanding them — and one notch up, the startup founder who is the entire legal department and would rather download an app.[^9]

Sandefur also counted the tools. Her 2019 survey for the American Bar Foundation found more than 320 digital legal tools for non-lawyers in US jurisdictions. About three quarters provide legal information of some type. About half help the user take any action at all. Her explanation is regulatory: the regime "limits most assistance with legal problems to services provided by licensed attorneys," non-lawyers "are usually prohibited from offering legal advice," and so "most US tools stay in the realm of 'legal information'" — general statements about the law that never touch the user's own facts.[^10]

## What legible would look like

In the summer of 1983 Marek Sergot, Fariba Sadri, Robert Kowalski and three colleagues translated a large part of the British Nationality Act 1981 into Prolog. Their 1986 paper records that the facts of a case "are obtained interactively" through an expert-system shell, and that the shell "also provides explanations when requested by the user." In January 1984 they demonstrated it to the Home Office, to officials who had been involved in drafting the act. Writing rules in logical form helps with drafting and redrafting them, the authors add — a point they credit to Layman Allen "as long ago as 1957."[^11] The instrument, the interview, the explanation, the drafters in the room: every piece of the 2020s rules-as-code pitch is in that paper.

A2J Author, begun in 2004 at Chicago-Kent, turned the interview into a guided form for people representing themselves in court; docassemble, which Jonathan Pyle wrote in 2015, is the open-source platform now widely used in legal aid. France's OpenFisca, conceived in 2011, is the engine behind the state's own benefits simulator, and Catala, from 2021, keeps a statute's text and its code side by side; L4 compiles to both. New Zealand's Better Rules discovery of 2018 and an OECD primer of 2020 carried the practice into government under the name it now goes by, rules as code.[^12] Plain-language law is a different project, and the US Plain Writing Act of 2010 says so in its definitions: a covered document "does not include a regulation."[^13] Plain language attacks the words. Legibility in Scott's sense is whether the structure can be seen through the words, and a plainly worded rule with three cross-references and a proviso is still a rule most readers cannot run.

An L4 rule compiles today to a docassemble interview, so a legal-aid organization already on docassemble can ask a member of the public only the questions the answer depends on and, where the rules carry citations, show on the verdict screen which rules produced the result.[^14] The same rules can be served as a decision API, with a Model Context Protocol (MCP) endpoint an AI agent can call as a tool and an execution trace on request.[^15] What comes back from either is a derivation — which rule fired, on which facts — with a section number under each step when the encoding has one.

Large language models give a fluent paragraph instead. In 2024 a Stanford group tested the commercial legal research tools sold as the fix for hallucination — Lexis+ AI, Westlaw's AI-Assisted Research, Ask Practical Law AI — and found that they hallucinate between 17 and 33 percent of the time by the paper's own abstract; the authors' summary puts Westlaw's tool above 34.[^16] The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) settled with DoNotPay, "the world's first robot lawyer," for $193,000 in February 2025, on a complaint that it never tested whether its AI lawyer worked like a human one.[^17] The rates will move. A generated paragraph carries no derivation to check. An executable encoding says what these rules, applied to these facts, produce, and shows the work. The sensible architecture uses the model to talk to the person and the encoding to decide what is said.

Someone still has to be allowed to tell the person what the rule says. Upsolve, a nonprofit, set out to train non-lawyer volunteers to tell people sued in New York over a consumer debt which box to check on the answer form; it has been in federal court since 2022 over whether that is the unauthorized practice of law, and as of March 2026 the state's rules stand. In Singapore the Legal Profession Act makes it an offense for an unauthorized person to act as an advocate or solicitor whether or not money changes hands, and a 1988 case reads that to cover giving advice on legal rights and obligations.[^18] Who may read the rule aloud, and to whom, has evidence of its own, and gets a post of its own.

## What the state's own legibility did

Australia's Robodebt scheme, from 2015 to 2019 under one name or another, took a welfare recipient's annual income from the tax office, averaged it into fortnights, and raised debts on the difference from what the person had declared. The Federal Court's summary of the 2021 class settlement put the debts at "at least $1.763 billion" — Australian dollars — asserted against about 433,000 people, of which about $751 million had been recovered. The Royal Commission that reported in July 2023 found the averaging "inconsistent with social security legislation." The Department of Social Services had been advised in November 2014 that it "did not accord with legislation," and the Commission found no evidence that the advice reached the department running the scheme.[^19] A state that makes its own law machine-readable can be wrong at scale. The machine read the citizen perfectly, down to the fortnight, and the citizen could not read back.

Seeing like a citizen would be the reverse: the derivation on the notice, with the rule text beside it, so that a wrong rule is one anyone can point at. Even then, a person on a benefit who receives a derivation of a debt has to know enough to distrust it. Mireille Hildebrandt's objection — that code-driven law "fold[s] enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke," taking from the citizen the chance to contest the interpretation in front of a court[^20] — is answered only in part by a derivation on a screen. The interpretation can be argued with, by a person who rarely has the standing, the money or the time to argue.

The pilots do not show adoption. Two engagements, one agency and one insurer, each signed off by one business executive who was not a programmer and who, as I remember it, could read the encoding — an impression from two people, not a study. A wizard on the web for which we have no usage figures. A docassemble export with fidelity reports and no legal-aid organization yet running it in front of a client.[^3] Both engagements produced a finding, and nothing in our record counts the encodings that produced none. That is the survivorship pattern Woodcock and Larsen found in Amazon's published formal-methods record this June, and it applies to us before it applies to Amazon.[^21] Sandefur's finding that the groups least able to reach a lawyer are also least able to use the tools built in a lawyer's place — 75 percent of the tools she surveyed require English — applies to anything we build until shown otherwise.[^10] And the primary sources are enclosed. A trusts precedent from 1904 that every student is taught turned out to be on no free database, and the British and Irish Legal Information Institute (BAILII), a free-access charity of Malamud's movement, now sits behind a proof-of-work wall to keep scrapers out.[^22]

If a jurisdiction published its rules in an executable, cited, derivation-producing form, let tools show that derivation to the public, and the justice-gap numbers for that jurisdiction did not move, then legibility would not be the binding constraint. The constraint would be where Sandefur has been pointing all along: whether people recognize the problem as one the law can answer. No jurisdiction has run that experiment.

The political-economy paper in the L4 series, planned and not yet drafted, takes up what is only pointed at here: who fills the gap when a state under-provisions legal legibility, and on what incentives. Meanwhile the page from the data-breach pilot is still online, asking its questions and citing its clauses.
