In 2013 Carl Malamud, through the nonprofit he had founded six years earlier, Public.Resource.Org, put the Official Code of Georgia Annotated on the internet. Georgia sued him in 2015. The state's position was that the annotations printed under each section — summaries of the court decisions and opinions that say what the section has been held to mean, prepared by an arm of the legislature — were copyrighted, and that a citizen who wanted to read the code the way the legislature read it should pay. The Supreme Court decided the case on April 27, 2020, five votes to four, Chief Justice Roberts writing: what a legislature authors in the course of its legislative duties cannot be copyrighted, annotations included.[^1] Seven years, one man, one book. Three years after that, the D.C. Circuit held that Malamud's posting of privately written safety standards, the ones building codes and consumer regulations incorporate by reference, was fair use, because they too had become the law.[^1]

The state that sued Malamud had spent centuries doing the opposite of what it accused him of. James C. Scott's *Seeing Like a State* (1998) is the standard account. Permanent surnames, so that an official who had never met you could tell you from your neighbor; cadastral surveys that flattened overlapping customary tenures into parcels with one taxable owner each; standard weights and measures; the census. Scott called the property the state was after *legibility* — a population an official can read — and in a later essay described state-making itself as "the conquest of illegibility."[^2] He died on July 19, 2024, at 87, having taught two generations of readers how a government comes to see its people.

Nothing in the book runs the other way. The citizen has been made legible to the state; the law has not been made legible to the citizen. That is a strange asymmetry to have tolerated for so long, because the law's own moral claim depends on the missing half. *Ignorantia juris non excusat* — ignorance of the law is no excuse — is a sentence that only makes sense if the law can be known by the person it binds.

## How big the gap is

The World Justice Project (WJP) ran legal-needs surveys in 102 countries and published the totals in June 2019. Thirty-six percent of people had experienced at least one justiciable problem in the past two years — money and debt, housing and neighbors, public services, family, work. Of those, 49 percent on average could not meet the legal need the problem created; summed over the world, about 1.4 billion people. The report's larger figure, 5.1 billion, adds people without identity documents or land tenure and people living under extreme injustice, and the authors say themselves that it is an aggregate to be broken down rather than quoted whole.[^3] In the United States the Legal Services Corporation (LSC), the federal funder of civil legal aid, surveyed more than 5,000 adults in 2021 through NORC at the University of Chicago and reported in 2022 that 74 percent of low-income households had at least one civil legal problem in the past year and got no help, or not enough, for 92 percent of the ones that had a substantial impact on them. Fifty-three percent did not know whether they could find and afford a lawyer if they needed one.[^4]

Those are lawyers' numbers, in the sense that they count problems a lawyer would recognize. Rebecca Sandefur, the sociologist who has done more than anyone to measure this, makes the point that reframes them: most people do not experience their problems as legal at all. "People do not consider law as a solution for their justice problems; they do not think of their problems as being 'legal'," she wrote in *Dædalus* in 2019, and the most common reason people give for not going to a lawyer is not the cost.[^5] A woman whose landlord has stopped doing repairs has a landlord problem. A man whose insurer has paid less than he expected has an insurer problem. Meng's version, from the conversation this blog grew out of, is the man on the street "who isn't quite sure what his legal situation is, under the law, or even under a handful of insurance policies that he signed without really understanding them," and one notch up, the startup founder who is the entire legal department and is used to downloading an app.[^6]

Sandefur also counted the tools. Her 2019 survey for the American Bar Foundation found more than 320 digital legal tools for non-lawyers in US jurisdictions. About three quarters provide legal information of some type; only about half help the user take any action; the commonest form is a long list. Her explanation of why the tools do so little is the second half of this argument in one paragraph: the regulatory regime "limits most assistance with legal problems to services provided by licensed attorneys," non-lawyers "are usually prohibited from offering legal advice," and so "most US tools stay in the realm of 'legal information'" — general statements about the law that never touch the user's own facts.[^7]

Gillian Hadfield's *Rules for a Flat World* (2017) gives the economist's version. Law is infrastructure, and the legal infrastructure we have is "too slow, costly, and localized for increasingly complex advanced economies," leaving about four billion people in developing countries outside formal legal structures altogether.[^8] Her remedy is a market in legal infrastructure with the state as regulator of private rule providers. Ours is narrower and sits underneath hers. Before anyone can compete to supply legal understanding, the law has to be in a form from which understanding can be mechanically produced.

## What legible would look like

The idea that a statute could be a program that answers a citizen's question is forty years old. In the summer of 1983 Marek Sergot, Fariba Sadri, Robert Kowalski and three colleagues translated a large part of the British Nationality Act 1981 into Prolog. The paper they published in 1986 records that the facts of an individual case "are obtained interactively" through an expert-system shell, that the shell "also provides explanations when requested by the user," and that they demonstrated the system in January 1984 to Home Office officials who had drafted the act. They add that Layman Allen had made the case for a logical form of rules as an aid to drafting "as long ago as 1957."[^9] The instrument, the interview, the explanation, the drafters in the room: every piece of the 2020s rules-as-code pitch is in that paper.

What was missing was delivery. A2J Author, begun in 2004 by Chicago-Kent's Center for Access to Justice and Technology with the Center for Computer-Assisted Legal Instruction (CALI), turned the idea into guided interviews for self-represented litigants, running on HotDocs templates through LawHelp Interactive; by September 2007 Idaho's legal-aid site had 29 interviews up and was getting 58,000 hits a month.[^10] docassemble, which Jonathan Pyle wrote in 2015 at Philadelphia Legal Assistance after deciding HotDocs interviews could do more than they did, is the open-source successor that most legal-aid interviews now run on.[^11] On the government side, New Zealand's Better Rules discovery in 2018 put drafters, policy people and developers in one room for three weeks to produce "human and machine-consumable versions of rules"; the OECD's 2020 primer *Cracking the Code* made rules as code a named practice; and this March the Dutch government hosted a Rules as Code conference in The Hague, under a European Commission program, asking "what are the terms and conditions for scaling up Rules as Code."[^12] The plain-language movement, meanwhile, got the Plain Writing Act through the US Congress in 2010, requiring agencies to write for the public in words the public can read.[^13] Plain language attacks the words. Legibility in Scott's sense is about whether the structure can be seen through them, and a plainly worded rule with three cross-references and a proviso is still a rule most readers cannot run.

Our own contribution to this lineage is small and specific. In 2022 we worked with a government agency, which stays unnamed, on Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) and its data-breach notification obligation. The encoding drove a web wizard: in Meng's description, end users could "input facts about their situation, and see what their legal obligations were — and why the system arrived at the answers it did, with citations back to the source rules in natural language." The wizard is still on the web, and the questions it asks are in its bundle: "notifiable data breach?" and "notify?".[^14] The verification side of the same engagement found a race condition in the rules, which is the first post in this series. The other output, the citizen-facing one, turned a statutory obligation into something a compliance officer with no lawyer could put facts into and get an answer out of, with a reason attached and a section number under the reason.

The insurer pilot, in 2023, is the same lesson from the other side. A large insurer, with millions of customers, gave us a policy to encode. The formalization exposed an ambiguity in the payout formula under which two readings gave two amounts, and the insurer, it turned out, had been paying the larger.[^15] Consider who in that story could have read the policy and known what it paid. Not the policyholder. Not the adjusters, who had been paying one reading in good faith for years. The document that governed millions of people's money was illegible to every party to it, and an encoding that had to choose was the first reader to notice there was a choice. In both engagements the client assigned a business executive, not a programmer, to review the encoding, and by Meng's account they were able to read it and sign off — an impression from two people, not a study.[^15]

That is the technical shape of legibility, and it is the shape the tooling is built around. An L4 rule compiles today to a docassemble interview, so that a legal-aid organization already running docassemble can ask a member of the public only the questions the answer depends on and, where the rules carry citations, show on the verdict screen which rules produced the result.[^16] The same rules can be served as a decision API, including a Model Context Protocol (MCP) endpoint that an AI agent can call as a tool.[^17] What comes back from either is a derivation: which rule fired, on which facts, citing which section. The large language models (LLMs) that arrived in 2023 give a fluent paragraph instead, and the measurement of how often the paragraph is wrong is sobering. Dahl, Magesh, Suzgun and Ho at Stanford, asking models specific and verifiable questions about federal cases, found GPT-4 hallucinated 58 percent of the time and Llama 2 88 percent.[^18] The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) went after DoNotPay, which had marketed itself as "the world's first robot lawyer," in September 2024, alleging it "did not test whether its AI lawyer operated to the level of a human lawyer" and had not retained attorneys to check its outputs; the final order in February 2025 carried $193,000 and a notice to every subscriber from 2021 to 2023.[^19] The difference between the two kinds of tool is the difference between an answer and a citation. A model can say what the law probably is. An executable encoding says what these rules, applied to these facts, produce, and shows the work; the sensible architecture uses the first to talk to the person and the second to decide what is said.

## Who is allowed to say it

The thing standing between the citizen and a legible law is not only the text. It is a license.

Upsolve is a nonprofit founded in 2016 to help people file for bankruptcy for free. One quarter of the cases filed in New York's courts are consumer debt collections, many for debts not actually owed, and a defendant who does not answer gets a default judgment; the state created a check-the-box form for answering, and there are not enough lawyers to help people fill it in.[^20] Upsolve trained non-lawyer volunteers, among them a pastor [UNVERIFIED: the co-plaintiff's identity is from a search summary of the Second Circuit's opinion, which returned 403 today], to give free, individualized advice on that one form. Under New York's unauthorized practice of law (UPL) rules that is a crime, so Upsolve sued first. A federal district judge enjoined the state in 2022, finding the rules as applied to be a content-based restriction on speech. On September 9, 2025, the Second Circuit vacated the injunction: the rules regulate speech, the court agreed, but by the circumstances in which it is given rather than by what it says, which calls for intermediate scrutiny, which the district court had not applied. On remand, in March 2026, the district court applied it and upheld the rules, which "advance the state's content-neutral interests without burdening a substantial amount of speech."[^21] As of this writing a trained volunteer in New York may not tell a neighbor which box to check.

Singapore's version of the rule is worth reading closely, because it does not say what people assume. The Legal Profession Act 1966 (LPA) has no general "practice of law" offense. Section 33 lists acts an unauthorized person may not do — act as an advocate or solicitor, draw a document relating to property or a proceeding, prepare probate papers, send a letter threatening proceedings — and the phrase "legal advice" appears nowhere in the Act. Advice is caught through a 1988 case, *Turner (East Asia) v Builders Federal*, which reads "acts as an advocate or a solicitor" to cover anything "customarily … within his exclusive function to provide, eg giving advice on legal rights and obligations."[^22] Two features matter to anyone building a tool. For that general limb, doing it for free is no defense; the fee only enters as a reverse burden under section 33(2), where the accused must prove there was no expectation of reward. And the High Court held in *Public Prosecutor v Lim Tean* this year that the offense is a conduct crime, complete without harm to anyone, and that the provision "serves to help preserve public confidence in the legal profession."[^23] An offense calibrated to the boundary of a profession rather than to harm to a consumer is what a protectionism reading predicts, and the court says so in as many words.

The legislature says it too. On February 27, 2026, in the Committee of Supply debate, a member of parliament who opened by declaring that she practices law in estate matters asked the Ministry of Law to regulate non-lawyer will-writing services: "can we fairly expect an average Singaporean to discern whether a course provider is rendering a legal advice or not," and, in the next breath, "unscrupulous operators also risk tarnishing the reputation of our legal professions."[^24] Read it fairly. Undue influence over elderly testators is a real harm and the interest was declared. But the concession inside her question — that the consumer cannot audit the supply — is an argument for anything that makes quality legible, and a license is only the answer that was available in 1966.

The part the access-to-justice literature does not say, and the part that turns a grievance into a supply-chain problem, is who the prohibition actually binds. Line up the candidates for filling the gap the WJP and LSC numbers describe. A domestic startup or civic project: incorporated, named, reachable, and under section 33(6)–(7) its directors and employees are personally liable for the act. A large platform: partly, with lawyers on staff and the option to switch the feature off in one country. An anonymous free service hosted somewhere else: no forum, no assets, nobody to charge. A hostile state running the same free service: none, by construction. The rule reaches hardest the one supplier who could be held to account and not at all the one who cannot. It does not reduce the number of people getting machine-mediated legal answers. It reduces the number of jurisdictionally reachable people supplying them.[^25]

Regulators who have tried to reopen the question have found it hard going. England and Wales reserved six activities in the Legal Services Act 2007 and legal advice is not among them; anyone may give it.[^26] Utah's Supreme Court launched a regulatory sandbox in 2020, a seven-year experiment now due to sunset on August 14, 2027, in which non-lawyer providers and "technology-based services such as AI" can be authorized to deliver legal services under supervision. Stanford's five-year review, in June 2025, counted 39 authorized entities in Utah in 2022 and 11 by April 2025, after the regulators "drastically reduced the number and types of authorized entities"; Arizona's parallel reform went from 19 to 136. The Utah sandbox, the reviewers wrote, "has not attracted the kinds of technologies that perhaps regulators had hoped for."[^27] Singapore holds a lever it has not pulled. Section 34(2) of the LPA lets the Minister exempt by rules "any class of persons each of whom, satisfies such requirements, and does such act in such circumstances, as may be prescribed." The power was added in 2011. We searched the subsidiary legislation for rules made under it and found none, which means "not found," not "none exist."[^28]

So what should a builder ask for? The engineer's instinct is to interpose layers — the company sells software, the software fills a template, an agent picks the clauses, the customer signs — and section 33(2), with its "directly or indirectly," is the one subsection drafted against exactly that. Even where indirection works it wins by making sure there is nobody to sue, which concedes the regulator's premise. The better ask is architectural. A system whose answer type is a string can always say "you should incorporate offshore." A system whose answer is a record — the criteria, stated generally; the derivation, showing which rules fired; the citations back to the source text; the questions the user must still decide for themselves — cannot express a recommendation, and the reason it cannot is the type rather than a filter.[^29] That is a condition a Minister can write into a section 34(2) exemption and an auditor can verify once, instead of a classifier somebody has to trust per transaction. It costs the chat interface: the moment the system can talk freely, it can advise. What survives is a structured interview that returns a derivation and leaves the open questions to the person.

## What the state's own legibility did

A state that makes its own law machine-readable can be wrong at scale. Australia's Robodebt scheme, from 2015 to 2019, took a welfare recipient's annual income from the tax office, averaged it into fortnights, and raised debts on the difference from what the person had declared. The Federal Court, approving the class settlement in 2021, put the numbers at debts of at least $1.763 billion unlawfully asserted against about 433,000 people, of which about $751 million had been recovered. The Royal Commission that reported in July 2023 found the averaging "inconsistent with social security legislation," and that the social-services department had been advised in November 2014 that it "did not accord with legislation" — advice the Commission found no evidence had reached the department running the scheme.[^30] The rules were perfectly legible to the machine. They were wrong, and the person opening the debt notice had no more ability to see why than the policyholder had to read the payout formula. Legibility to the citizen means the derivation is visible to the citizen, with the rule text beside it, so that a wrong rule is a wrong rule anyone can point at. Even then, a person on a benefit who receives a derivation of a debt has to know enough to distrust it.

Mireille Hildebrandt's objection is the one to hold in mind here. Code-driven law, she wrote in 2020, "fold[s] enactment, interpretation and application into one stroke," and what a citizen loses when the law is disambiguated in advance is the chance to contest the interpretation in front of a court.[^31] An encoding that shows its derivation answers part of that: the interpretation is on the screen and can be argued with. It does not answer the rest, which is that the person on the other side of the screen rarely has the standing, the money or the time to argue.

The pilots do not show adoption. Two engagements, one agency and one insurer, each signed off by one executive; a wizard on the web for which I have no usage figures; a docassemble export with fidelity reports but no legal-aid organization yet running it in front of a client. Sandefur's finding that the groups least able to reach a lawyer are also least able to use the tools built in a lawyer's place — 75 percent of the tools she surveyed require English — applies to anything we build until shown otherwise.[^7] The licensing analysis is Singapore's and New York's; the section 34(2) search was keyword-based; *Turner* has been read as a 2021 judgment quotes it, not in the original.[^22] And the primary sources are enclosed in ways that bite our own work: a trusts precedent from 1904 that every student is taught turned out to be on no free database, and the free-access charity that holds much of the Commonwealth's case law now sits behind a proof-of-work wall to keep scrapers out.[^32]

What would overturn the position is easy to state. If a jurisdiction published its rules in an executable, cited, derivation-producing form, exempted derivation-only tools from its practice restrictions, and the justice-gap numbers for that jurisdiction did not move, then legibility would not be the binding constraint and the constraint would be where Sandefur has been pointing all along, in whether people recognize the problem as one the law can answer. No jurisdiction has run that experiment. Utah ran a smaller one and narrowed it.

The paper this post prefigures is the political-economy facet of the L4 series, in design, on legal legibility as civic infrastructure: what happens when a state under-provisions it, who fills the gap, and on what incentives. That paper carries the argument about the filler and the supply chain that this post only points at.
