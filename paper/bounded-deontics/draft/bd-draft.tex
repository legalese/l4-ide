\begin{abstract}
Every legal ``must'' invites the question \emph{or else what?} We treat that question as
constitutive of obligation rather than as a challenge to it: the deontic force of a rule
is \emph{bounded} by a goal and by the ordering of preferences against which compliance
and breach are judged. We model the object level of a contract as a labelled transition
system---the ``letter''---and \emph{derive} the bounded \MUST{} as goal-domination, an
action on every path to a goal (equivalently, a planning landmark), rather than declaring
it. Layering a \emph{party-indexed} ordering source over this party-neutral necessity
yields a teleological obligation; admitting two orderings---the law's and the agent's---
recovers Holmes's bad man and Hart's internal point of view as the divergence and the
coincidence of one object, and renders the force of a ``must'' the gap, in the agent's
ordering, between complying and breaching. We claim no new logical primitive: the
Anderson--Meyer reduction, planning landmarks, betterness orderings, and strategic ability
are all conceded prior art. The contribution is their composition, its jurisprudential
reading, and an executable instantiation in the legal DSL L4.
\end{abstract}
