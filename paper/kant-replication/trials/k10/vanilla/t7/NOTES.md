# Notes on judgement calls

These are the points in the nine-question set where the contract text required an
interpretive choice rather than a mechanical lookup.

## Q1, Q8 vs Q9 — "arising directly or indirectly out of" requires a causal link, not mere status

Section 2.1 excludes events "causing sickness or accidental injury arising directly or
indirectly out of" (among others) service as a fire fighter, service in the military, and
service in the police. Q1 ("burns suffered while doing my duty as a firefighter") and Q8
("injured in a military training exercise") both state the excluded service _as the cause_
of the injury, so the exclusion applies to both (answered No).

Q9 is worded differently: the stated cause of injury is "my son biting me in the ankle,"
and the query separately notes "I was serving as a police officer at the time of
hospitalization." I read this as a status fact (the claimant's occupation at the relevant
time), not a causal claim that the bite arose out of police duty — nothing ties a son
biting an ankle to police service. I treated the police exclusion as not triggered, since
2.1 excludes events arising out of the listed service, not injuries suffered by people who
happen to hold that occupation. Answered Yes. This is the one place the contract's
"arising out of" language actually does interpretive work rather than being redundant with
a simple category match.

## Q4 vs Q6/Q7/Q9 — reading "wellness visit provided/submitted at month X"

Section 1.3 sets two distinct deadlines: the wellness visit itself must occur no later
than the 6-month anniversary, and written confirmation of it must be supplied no later
than the 7-month anniversary. The queries only ever give a single figure ("confirmation
given/proof provided/submitted at month X"), describing the confirmation-submission act,
not the underlying visit date directly.

I treated submission time as an upper bound on visit time (you cannot submit confirmation
of a visit that hasn't happened yet), so:

- Q4: confirmation given at month 8 — this alone breaches the 7-month submission deadline,
  regardless of when the underlying visit occurred, so 1.3 is "not satisfied in a timely
  fashion" and cancelation is deemed under 1.2. Answered No. (Section 3.1's worldwide
  coverage means "traveling abroad" is not itself a problem — the late confirmation is
  what drives this answer.)
- Q7 (month 2) and Q9 (month 6, read as "no later than" the 6-month mark, i.e. inclusive):
  both satisfy the submission deadline, and since submission cannot precede the visit, the
  visit itself was also necessarily within the 6-month sub-deadline. Treated as timely.
- Q6: proof at month 6.5 would leave the visit-timing sub-deadline genuinely ambiguous on
  this same reasoning, but it doesn't matter — Q6 is independently excluded by the
  skydiving clause (2.1.1), so I didn't need to resolve it.

## Q5 — "accidental injury" is undefined, and there is no self-inflicted-injury exclusion

Q5 describes hospitalization from punching one's own face "to show off," with an explicit
stipulation of no fraud or misrepresentation. Two things drove the answer:

1. Section 2.1's exclusion list is exhaustive on its face (skydiving, military, fire
   fighting, police service, age ≥ 80) and contains nothing about self-inflicted,
   intentional, or reckless acts — a category many real accident policies exclude by name,
   but this one does not.
2. "Accidental injury" is not defined in the contract. Reading it by ordinary meaning, the
   _act_ (punching oneself) was deliberate, but a hospitalization-grade outcome from a
   stunt intended to impress friends is not what the actor presumably intended or expected;
   I treated the unintended severity of the result as enough to keep this within
   "accidental injury" absent a contrary definition.

I answered Yes, on a literal reading of what the contract actually excludes rather than
what a typical accident policy might exclude. I treated the explicit "I did not commit
fraud or misrepresentation" as foreclosing the only textually related ground for
cancelation (Section 1.2's fraud/misrepresentation clause), and read it as a signal that
the fraud question, not the "was it truly an accident" question, is what the query wants
tested. This is the least certain of the nine answers; "I do not know" was the main
alternative I considered here.

## General

- Applied the standing preamble (all unmentioned conditions/exclusions assumed satisfied
  or inapplicable; agreement signed and premium paid) to every question, so age, activity,
  or timing facts not mentioned in a given query were not treated as issues for that
  query (e.g. Q2 and Q3 give no wellness-visit facts, so 1.3 was treated as "still
  pending"/non-issue there).
- The contract text provided stops at Section 3.6 (Policy Term) with no benefit schedule
  or defined-terms section (Section 5, referenced for premium/term, is not in the
  supplied text). None of the nine queries turned on benefit amounts or a defined term
  that would have required that missing material, so this did not force an "I do not
  know" answer anywhere.
